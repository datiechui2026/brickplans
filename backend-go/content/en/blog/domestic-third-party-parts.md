# Buying Third-Party Brick Parts: A Beginner's Sourcing Guide

Anyone who builds MOCs knows the frustration: the instructions can be beautiful, but if you can't gather the parts, nothing gets built. LEGO loose elements are expensive and the channels are limited, so more and more builders are turning to the third-party market for Chinese-made brick parts. After several years of development, domestic loose parts are now genuinely mature in both precision and colour matching. The real question is how to judge quality, where to buy, and how to avoid the usual traps. Here is everything I've learned from the mistakes I've made.

## The main channels for third-party Chinese brick parts

There are roughly four categories of buying channel today. The first is per-part online shops that sell loose elements by part number. These suit builders filling a list item by item from a set of instructions: shipping is fast and the colour range is complete, which makes them the first choice for most builders. The second is weigh-and-buy bulk shops, which sell parts by weight in a blind-box style. They're cheap in volume but you have to sort everything yourself, so they suit stocking up a library of basic bricks. The third is factory-direct channels, such as Gobricks' own store, where quality is the most consistent. The fourth is second-hand platforms, where bulk lots from experienced builders leaving the hobby often turn up as bargains at half the price of new parts, sometimes less.

## How to judge third-party part quality

The third-party market is a mixed bag, so judging quality comes down to three checks. First, look at the mould source: Gobricks OEM parts fit tightly and hold colour evenly, and are widely regarded as the top tier. Many Chinese sets label themselves as using Gobricks parts, and looking for that mark saves a lot of guesswork. Second, check the sprue marks and flash: turn the part in your hand, and if your fingertip clearly catches on flash, it's low-end tooling. Third, check colour consistency: try to buy everything for one set of instructions from a single shop in one order, so you avoid colour differences caused by mixing batches. It's worth buying a small test batch first to confirm the tolerances are fine before placing a large order.

## The practical steps for buying from a parts list

Once you have a set of MOC instructions, purchasing can follow four steps. Step one: export the parts list into a spreadsheet, group it by colour and part number, and count the quantity of each element. Step two: separate generic basic parts (bricks, plates, round bricks, connectors) from special parts, then send the basics to weigh-and-buy or value shops and the specials (technic parts, ball joints, light-up elements) to Gobricks or original LEGO, so critical positions keep their precision. Step three: compare prices item by item across several third-party shops, because the same part number often varies by 30-40% in price. Step four: before ordering, confirm stock levels and colour batches, and note down any out-of-stock parts so you can decide whether to substitute or wait for a restock. Followed properly, this process usually saves 20-30% off the cost of ordering the whole list straight from one shop.

## Common pitfalls to avoid

There are a few traps almost everyone hits when buying third-party Chinese brick parts. The first is photos that don't match the product: some shops use high-precision renders instead of real photos, and the colour difference only shows up on arrival, so check buyer photos and follow-up reviews first. The second is fake Gobricks claims, where a shop describes ordinary parts as Gobricks OEM; you can ask to see the packaging or the OEM code. The third is the transparent parts trap: domestic transparent elements vary a lot in clarity, so for visible parts like windows and car lights, buy batches that reviewers explicitly describe as high-clarity. The fourth is the shipping cost trap: loose parts are charged by weight, so one combined order is much better value than several separate ones. Avoiding these pitfalls in advance saves a great deal of rework time.

The third-party market for Chinese brick parts is mature enough that, with the right channels and proper quality checks, you can build a MOC that looks 90% as good on 30% of the budget. The BrickPlans (brickplan.cn) community collects a large number of builders' purchasing lists and price-comparison notes, so if you're unsure about a part number, search the community for the configurations other builders have shared and you'll often skip a lot of wasted effort. Building up a parts library isn't something to rush: stocking up slowly and buying what you need is the most comfortable way to play.
