# Brick Warship MOC Instructions: Where to Find and How to Judge

I have been building with bricks for over a decade, and warships have always been my favourite subject. My collection of warship MOC instructions has grown quite a bit over the years. This review looks at where these instructions come from, what separates a good one from a bad one, how hard they are to build, and what the finished model is actually like — plus the mistakes I made along the way so you can avoid wasting money on the same ones.

## Where people actually get these instructions

There are three main sources: instruction-sharing communities and forums, individual designers' own pages, and swaps within builder groups. Quality and update frequency vary enormously between them. Community sites such as BrickPlans sort warship MOC instructions by subject, which saves a lot of hunting around. Paid instructions have generally been checked by the designer and are more consistent in quality; free ones are fine but you have to do the filtering yourself.

## How to judge instruction quality

A usable warship MOC instruction set comes down to two things: a complete parts list and clear step diagrams. I have reviewed a lot of these, and the good ones label the part number for every step and include a sorted parts list so you can bag pieces by colour — that makes the build far more efficient. The worst case is blurry step images where the part numbers do not match the list; you end up halfway through and have to tear it down and start again. Clarity of the diagrams is the single biggest factor in whether a set of instructions is worth building.

## Assessing build difficulty

Warship instructions span a huge range. Small-scale ships use few parts and can be finished in a few hundred steps, which suits newcomers as practice. Large-scale models run to two or three thousand steps and call for plenty of specialised parts, so they are better suited to experienced builders. My advice for beginners is to start with instructions totalling one or two thousand parts, get comfortable with the process, then scale up. When you read through the instructions, pay attention to how they explain special parts and connection methods — that alone saves a lot of rework.

## The finished model and what to watch for

The end result depends mostly on the designer's skill. Good instructions produce a hull with clean lines and balanced proportions that looks impressive on a shelf. One thing to watch: some designs call for fairly obscure parts, and rounding them up is not cheap, so estimate the total parts cost before you commit. Also keep the original instruction file — you will want it if you dismantle and rebuild the model later, or repaint it.
