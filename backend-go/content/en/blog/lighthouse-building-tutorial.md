# How to Build a LEGO Lighthouse: Base, Tower and Lamp Room

A lighthouse is probably the most photogenic subject in the whole maritime MOC category. The red-and-white striped tower and the turning lamp room read instantly, whether the model sits on a desk or in a beach diorama. This **lighthouse building tutorial** ignores brands entirely and focuses on structure and technique, walking through the base, the tower, the lamp room and the rotating light step by step.

## Step 1: Plans, Parts and Scale

Settle the scale before you open a single bag. A lighthouse breaks into three parts: the foundation, the tower body and the lamp room, and the tower body usually makes up about 70% of the total height. Decide how tall the finished model should be first: a mini desk version can stay under 30 cm, while a display-centerpiece scene can go past half a meter. Once the height is locked in, work backwards from your baseplate size to the tower diameter. Too wide and the tower looks bloated; too narrow and there is no room for the internal staircase.

On the parts side, a cylindrical tower eats enormous quantities of 1x1 and 1x2 elements. If you are building from loose stock, sort same-color parts by size before you start. If you cannot find a complete plan, don't panic: brickplan.cn carries plenty of brick lighthouse plans, and searching for "lighthouse" turns up versions with parts lists and step-by-step instructions, which is far easier than guessing from a render.

## Step 2: A Solid Footing and Base

The foundation decides whether the whole tower stands. The recommended approach is to lay a large baseplate first, then add a ring of thick plates around its perimeter to form a plinth slightly wider than the tower. In a seaside scene, leave space on the foundation for rocks and use irregular elements to suggest waves breaking against them. Make sure to bury a few vertical reinforcement columns in the center of the foundation, running up into the tower body. These are the spine of the entire structure.

The base itself is usually built as a series of stepped tiers that draw inward, which both suggests the wave-breaking design at the foot of a real lighthouse and gives the tower a more natural transition. Inset each tier by half a brick to one brick of width. Inset too aggressively and the model looks top-heavy.

## Step 3: The Tower Body and Its Taper

A real lighthouse tower is a cone, wider at the bottom than the top. In brick the usual trick is a "step-in" every few courses. After four or five rings of constant diameter, inset one full ring so the circumference shrinks by one brick. This mimics the conical profile while using fewer parts and staying stronger than changing the diameter on every course. At each step-in, connect the inner and outer structure with plates so the courses cannot shift out of alignment.

The tower interior normally needs a core column, with the staircase spiraling up around it. If space allows, build a genuinely walkable spiral staircase from slope pieces and tread plates; it is one of the most impressive details in any lighthouse MOC. Plan the window positions in advance, and line up the window frames floor by floor so the exterior reads as orderly.

## Step 4: The Lamp Room and Rotating Light

The lamp room is the soul of the build. It is usually a little narrower than the tower, with transparent elements forming a ring of windows to create the glasshouse effect. Cap it with a ring of dark-colored eaves, then a conical or domed lamp housing.

If you want the light to actually turn, leave room at the bottom of the lamp room and connect a simple gear train to a rotation motor or a hand-crank mechanism. The lazy option is to fix the lamp in place under a transparent hemisphere. The advanced option is to mount two or three lamps side by side on a rotating bracket so the beam sweeps across the water; at night it looks fantastic. Make sure the gear train is anchored firmly, or it will fall apart after two rotations.

## Common Pitfalls and Finishing Touches

Beginners tend to trip up in three places. First, irregular step-ins leave the tower leaning. Second, no transition between the lamp room and the tower body, so the top ring looks abrupt. Third, the internal staircase and the core column fight for the same space, and halfway through the build you cannot fit the assembly inside. Lay the internal structure out on the table first, then enclose it in the tower, and test-fit more times than you think you need.

For the finish, add a small door and a short jetty at the foot of the tower, or place a minifigure in a trench coat standing under the light. Put the finished model on a shelf or into a diorama, add a warm-toned brick light, and the whole lighthouse comes alive. If you are not done after that, the brickplan.cn blog also has lighthouse review articles, so you can compare how different sizes of plan turn out and find inspiration for your next tower.
