# How to Plan a LEGO Airport Scene: Terminal, Runway, Tower

I have been building with bricks for over ten years and have made plenty of city street scenes, but the airport is one of the few subjects I have torn down and rebuilt several times. The reason is simple: an airport scene is not about stacking a few buildings, it is about spatial logic. The terminal, runway, aircraft, tower and ground vehicles all have to sit in the right place relative to each other before the whole thing reads as an airport. This guide lays out the full planning approach and the mistakes to avoid, so you can skip the detours I took.

## Set the baseplate first: spatial layout for the airport

Before you lay a single brick, decide the size of your baseplate and how it will be divided. In my experience, start by marking three longitudinal bands on a standard baseplate: the runway and taxiway take the widest band, the terminal and apron sit in the middle, and the tower plus support area hug the outer edge. Leave enough room for an aircraft to turn around, and keep at least one fuselage width between adjacent stands. Sketch a top-down plan on paper before you build. Do not start snapping bricks straight away, because relocating things later is the most time-consuming part of the whole job.

## Terminal and apron: the core skeleton of the airport

The terminal is the face of the airport and also its load-bearing core. My habit is to start with a two-storey building on a raised base: the ground floor holds the check-in hall and the jet bridge entrance, while the upper floor becomes a waiting and viewing deck. Use transparent pieces for floor-to-ceiling windows and the glazed connector. Inside the building, stack solid bricks to form load-bearing walls, then apply decorative elements to the facade; that way it stays rigid and still has detail. The apron spreads out directly in front of the terminal, so plan the connection points for ground vehicles and jet bridges in advance. Adding those sockets early makes it much easier to drop aircraft in and hang bridges later.

## Fitting the runway, taxiway and aircraft together

The runway is the part of an airport scene that delivers the biggest visual payoff and also the part most likely to go wrong. Build the runway surface from long white bricks and use light gray bricks for the centerline markings. Stagger the seams and never let a joint run continuously down the full length, or the finished surface will warp and lift at the edges. Differentiate the taxiway with dark gray bricks and use curved elements to transition at its ends. For the aircraft, build it as a separate module: once the fuselage is finished, leave connection points underneath for the landing gear and wheels, then clip the whole thing onto the apron and runway at the end. This keeps swapping and repositioning flexible.

## Tower and ground vehicles: making the scene feel alive

The tower does not need to be tall. I usually build three or four storeys with a panoramic observation window on top, and place it between the runway and the terminal at the visual focal point. That gives the sense of control without blocking the main structure. Ground vehicles are what make an airport scene feel operational: build one or two each of baggage carts, shuttle buses and fuel trucks, make sure the wheels actually turn, and echo the terminal's color scheme. These little vehicles are small and structurally simple, which makes them ideal practice builds. Scatter several of them across the apron and the whole scene immediately looks like it is running.

## Lighting and finishing details: lifting the overall effect

Details decide whether the airport has that cinematic feel. I use transparent pieces plus small light kits for the runway edge lights and the terminal window lights; once they are switched on at night, the layers of the airport appear instantly. Then add the radar antenna on top of the tower, direction signs on the apron and small seats in the waiting area, filling the space piece by piece. To build an airport this complete, plans and parts planning matter a great deal. Take a look at https://brickplan.cn to see other builders' airport creations and their parts lists; working from references saves a huge amount of trial and error.
