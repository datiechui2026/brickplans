# Brick Roller Coaster MOC Guide: Track, Supports, Lift & Station

A roller coaster is the most dramatic large ride you can put in an amusement park brick build, and also the most demanding. Plenty of builders start with real enthusiasm and end up with a track that does not flow, a car that stalls on the first hill, or supports that give way after two laps. This guide takes the build apart step by step, from track sectioning and support structure through the chain lift and station to the loop, so the coaster you finish is one that actually runs.

## 1. Set the Scale and Layout First

Before you lay a single piece of track, draw the plan-view route on the baseplate. Start with a 32x32 stud baseplate and run the track as a rectangular loop or an S shape, so a limited footprint still gives you a decent drop. Keep the track width consistent throughout, and err on the side of a large turning radius: go too tight and the car gets thrown off the rails when it takes the corner at speed. Mark out the four zones before you lay anything down — highest point, descent, loop section and the gentle return run. Planning them first saves a great deal of rework later.

## 2. Track Sections: Join Straights and Curves by Number

The track is the skeleton of the whole ride, so join it in sections and check each section as you go. Sort your straight track, curve track and hill track by number first, then connect them one at a time, running your hand along the running surface after every joint to confirm there is no step or misalignment. Stagger the joints rather than bunching every seam into the same row — the load spreads more evenly and the car runs noticeably smoother. On the descent, keep the incline between 20 and 30 degrees. Steeper than that and the car takes off; shallower and it will not roll at all. This angle is the single biggest factor in how a brick roller coaster MOC feels when you let the car go.

## 3. Support Structure: The Hidden Skeleton Under the Track

The part of a coaster that fails first is rarely the track — it is the supports. Every elevated stretch needs its own column underneath; do not count on the two ends to carry the middle. Build the columns from technic beams or vertical rods on a base and stand one every 8 to 12 studs, adding diagonal bracing at curves and at the crests and dips. Under high track, tie the columns together with a cross brace so the track cannot spread outward and drop the car off the rails. Keep column bases planted on the baseplate or on the station structure, and the whole ride becomes stable.

## 4. Chain Lift: Let the Car Climb to the Apex Itself

If you want the coaster to genuinely move, the chain lift before the highest point is the detail that makes it work. The standard approach is to run a row of long toothed track or gear rack along the centerline of the climb, connect the gear at the bottom with chain links or tread links, and turn a handle to draw the car slowly up to the crest — past the top, gravity takes over. Even without a motor, hand-cranked gears deliver the complete climb, release and roll cycle, which makes this the best starting point for a beginner who wants to understand a mechanical structure.

## 5. Station Design: Home for Dispatch and Return

The station is both the start line and the return point, and getting it right is what closes the loop of the whole build. Put it on a flat section, on a base slightly wider than the track, with railings on both sides, a dispatch opening at one end and an arrival opening at the other, and room in the middle for a ticket booth and a queue of minifigures. When the car rolls back in it stops naturally against an end stop; the builder pushes it back to the dispatch position and hooks it onto the chain, and one operating cycle is complete. Build the station as a separate module so the entire coaster can be taken apart and moved.

## 6. The Loop Challenge: The Advanced Showpiece

Once straights and hills are under control, the loop is the next test. A loop is extremely sensitive to curvature, so pre-build the full circle on the ground first and push the car around it repeatedly, then raise the whole assembly once you are satisfied. The entry slope has to be steep enough that the car arrives with enough speed to clear the top rather than stalling there, and the supports on both sides of the loop need obvious extra reinforcement. For a first attempt, use a large-radius loop — the success rate is far higher.

## 7. Summary and Plan References

When you build a brick roller coaster MOC, keep this order in mind: plan the route, lay the track, raise the supports, add the chain lift, and finish with the station. Work through it in that sequence and the track flows, the structure holds, and the car both climbs and rolls — a coaster that genuinely operates.
