# How to Store MOC Instructions: Digital and Paper Backup

Short answer first: how do you store brick MOC instructions? The answer is a dual digital backup plus moisture-proof paper storage. Keep the original PDF in cloud storage and on a local drive, one copy in each place, and put paper plans in clear sleeves away from direct sunlight. Do that and you will still be able to build from them ten years from now.

Instructions are the soul of an MOC. Plenty of builders have paid a few hundred yuan for a set of plans, or spent hours drawing their own, only to lose them to a dead hard drive or a damp drawer. By the time they want to rebuild the model, it is too late. Storing MOC instructions properly is a small skill worth taking seriously.

**Step 1: Back up the digital file right away.** The moment you receive a PDF, save the original file to cloud storage and to a local hard drive. The two locations must be independent of each other so a single failed storage medium cannot take out both copies. A 50MB instruction file costs almost nothing to duplicate, and that duplication is what prevents the "plans lost, model can never be rebuilt" regret.

**Step 2: Set up a naming convention.** Name files by brand, series, part count and year, for example "brickplan-City Street-2000 pcs-2026". With a rule like this you can find one specific file out of 100 in about 30 seconds, which is far more efficient than naming files whatever comes to mind.

**Step 3: Store paper copies against moisture.** Paper instructions are most vulnerable to damp and fading. Sort them into clear plastic sleeves or zip bags, then place them in a storage box with desiccant packs and keep humidity between 40% and 60%. Keep them out of direct sunlight, because UV exposure will visibly yellow the paper within 2-3 years.

**Step 4: Scan the important ones.** Hand-drawn or out-of-print plans are worth converting to digital files with a scanner. A resolution of 300dpi or higher is sharp enough to zoom in on details, and scanning one A3 sheet takes only about 3 minutes, yet it gives the plans a second layer of insurance.

**Step 5: Check and refresh on a schedule.** Every six months, verify that your cloud links still work and inspect the paper copies for damp damage, then repair anything you find. Once this habit is in place, more than 95% of instruction-loss problems can be avoided ahead of time.

Follow this method and a collector with 200 sets of instructions will spend roughly 3 hours on initial sorting, then no more than 1 hour per year on maintenance. That is excellent value, and it is worth every MOC builder's attention.

## FAQ

**Q: What is the safest way to store brick MOC instructions?**
A: Use a cloud drive plus a local hard drive for the digital version, and a storage box with desiccant for the paper version. The two approaches back each other up: if one copy fails, the other is there to rescue you, so the odds of losing the plans drop close to zero.

**Q: Can damp, moldy paper instructions still be used?**
A: Lightly damp sheets can be air-dried in a cool, ventilated spot and pressed flat under a heavy weight, which usually leaves them perfectly usable. If mold has already appeared, scan the sheet for archiving first, then gently wipe the spots with an alcohol swab. Preventing damp in the first place matters far more than fixing it afterwards.

**Q: Is cloud storage safe for instructions?**
A: Mainstream cloud services are secure enough for ordinary files, but turn on file encryption and two-factor authentication anyway. Never treat the cloud as your only storage. For important plans, stick to a local copy plus a cloud copy, and export to an external hard drive from time to time — three layers is when you can relax.

**Q: Are hand-drawn MOC instructions worth scanning?**
A: Absolutely. A hand-drawn plan exists only once. Scan it at 300dpi and you can print and rebuild from it even if the original is lost or damaged. Scanning costs next to nothing and amounts to permanent insurance for the plans.

**Q: What is the difference between digital and paper instructions?**
A: Digital files are easy to search, zoom and share, which makes them ideal for long-term archiving. Paper is direct to read, needs no device, and suits building at the table. Keep digital as the primary copy and paper as the working copy, and retain both for important plans so each covers the other's weakness.
