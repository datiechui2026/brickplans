# How to Build a LEGO Dog MOC: Face, Ears and Poses

The short version: what separates a dog MOC from a cat is the protruding muzzle. The cute, chunky look comes from short legs, a long body and a thick chest, and switching breeds is mostly a matter of swapping parts.

Dogs are one of the most rewarding themes in brick building: the Shiba's goofy grin, the Corgi's stubby legs, the Golden Retriever's floppy ears. Yet plenty of first attempts come out looking like a wolf or a bear, and the culprit is almost always muzzle proportion and ear treatment. This guide breaks the build down into face and features, floppy ears and tail, and standing versus lying poses.

## Face and Features: The Muzzle Makes It Cute

The biggest difference between a dog and a cat is the muzzle: a dog's face juts forward. Start the skull on a 2x4 brick base, then raise the center of the face by 2 to 3 plate heights to form the bridge of the nose, and extend a 1x2 brick forward to create the muzzle. Cap it with a black 1x1 round tile so the side profile reads as a dog rather than a cat. Set the eyes slightly higher and slightly further back than the base of the muzzle, keeping 2 to 3 studs between them. For that squinting Shiba smile, tuck a 1x1 curved slope plate under each eye to push out a gently upturned cheek line. Mount a pink 1x1 tile upside down and angled into the mouth gap so only a sliver of tongue shows, and the friendly expression appears instantly. Build the mouth gap from 1x1 slope bricks staggered top and bottom; it looks more natural than a printed line piece. Leave some studs facing inward inside the head so you can adjust ear and feature heights later.

## Floppy Ears and Tails: Bringing the Dog to Life

Floppy ears define Golden Retrievers, Cocker Spaniels and similar breeds, and they are the easiest thing to build stiffly. Connect the ear root with a 1x2 hinge brick, then build the ear body from 2x3 plates into a teardrop shape. It hangs naturally and still swings slightly as the head turns. For prick-eared breeds such as the Shiba or German Shepherd, use 1x1 triangle bricks or small slope bricks, with a hinge plate at the root to control the angle of the tip. For the tail, mount the base on a ball joint, then add a hinge brick every 3 to 4 studs along the length. A Shiba's curl stacks 3 to 4 curved 1x2 bricks rolling inward, while a Golden's feathery tail widens layer by layer with tiles. Test every articulated joint through two full rotations before installing it: too loose and it sags, too tight and it snaps.

## Body Proportions and Poses: Skeleton First, Shell Second

Half the charm of a dog is proportion: short legs, long body, thick chest. Take the Corgi: build the torso 8 to 10 studs long and 3 to 4 studs wide, with legs only 2 to 3 studs tall, raised using 2x2 bricks. Push the chest forward with curved 1x3 bricks and taper the belly layer by layer. For a standing pose, place the four feet at the corners of a rectangle with the center of gravity over its middle, and tilt the front legs forward 5 to 10 degrees for a more lifelike stance. For extra stability, widen the paws to 2x3 plates. A lying pose works completely differently: extend the front legs forward on hinge bricks with the paws crossed, fold the hind legs to either side of the body, drop the whole torso by one plate height and lift the head slightly. Build that skeleton first, then fill in the torso and wrap the curved shell, and you have a dog sunbathing on its belly. Either way, follow skeleton first, shell second, to avoid rework.

## Swapping Breeds and Colors

Once the base structure is in place, changing breed means changing parts. For a Corgi, lengthen the torso, shorten the legs and use an orange-and-white two-tone scheme. For a Shiba, build prick ears and a curled tail in yellow and white, with a slightly shorter muzzle for a stronger smiling face. For a Golden Retriever, go with floppy ears and a feathery tail, all golden with a lighter belly. For a Dachshund, stretch the torso past 14 studs and compress the legs to 1 stud tall, and the cuteness doubles. If you want fur texture, clip thin rods into 1x1 bricks with clips for a ruffled look, or stagger slope bricks to suggest curls. Find reference photos and lock in proportions before you start each breed; it saves a lot of time.

## FAQ

**Q: What is the biggest difference between building a brick dog and a brick cat?**
A: The muzzle: a dog's face juts out. Start the skull on a 2x4 brick, raise the center of the face by 2 to 3 plate heights for the nose bridge, then extend a 1x2 brick forward for the muzzle and cap it with a black 1x1 round tile.

**Q: How do I keep floppy ears from looking stiff?**
A: Connect the ear root with a 1x2 hinge brick and build the ear body from 2x3 plates into a teardrop shape, so it hangs naturally and swings slightly with the head. For prick-eared breeds, switch to 1x1 triangle bricks with a hinge plate at the root to set the tip angle.

**Q: What proportions should a short-legged dog like a Corgi use?**
A: Build the torso 8 to 10 studs long and 3 to 4 studs wide, with legs only 2 to 3 studs tall raised on 2x2 bricks. Push the chest forward with curved 1x3 bricks and taper the belly; the charm comes from short legs, a long body and a thick chest.

**Q: How do I change parts to get different breeds?**
A: Once the base structure is set, it is all part swaps. A Corgi gets a longer torso, shorter legs and orange-and-white colors; a Shiba gets prick ears, a curled tail and yellow-and-white colors; a Golden Retriever gets floppy ears and a feathery tail; a Dachshund gets a torso over 14 studs long with legs compressed to 1 stud.
