# How to Pick a Vehicle MOC Plan: Sports, Off-Road or Machine

When you get into vehicle building, the first step isn't buying parts — it's choosing the right **vehicle MOC plan**. Vehicle blueprints are the messiest category in all of MOC building and carry the most parameters: two models can both be "a car," yet a sports car, an off-roader and a piece of construction machinery sit on completely different levels when it comes to scale, wheel setup, play features and difficulty. This review breaks down the key points of choosing a plan from a practical building standpoint.

## Vehicle categories: decide first whether it's for display or for play

Sports car plans chase accurate lines — a low, wide stance and oversized wheels — and the finished model is usually a static display piece, with steering and opening doors counted as bonus features. Off-road plans almost always include suspension and linked steering, with a high chassis and big tires; pushing one around is more fun than looking at it. Construction vehicles (excavators, loaders, cranes) emphasize mechanical structure — hydraulic cylinders, gearboxes, articulated booms — and are usually the hardest. Trucks are simpler in structure but large in volume, while police cars and fire engines sit between sports cars and off-roaders, balancing looks with hands-on play.

## Scale: fix the scale before you pick the plan

The most common vehicle scales are 1:8, 1:10 and 1:14. A big 1:8 supercar easily runs three to four thousand pieces — an imposing size and a high parts cost. 1:10 sports cars and off-roaders are the mainstream: proportions stay balanced and there's room inside for steering and suspension mechanisms. 1:14 is mostly used for trucks, fire engines and similar vehicles; the size is moderate, which suits builders on a tighter budget or anyone touching vehicle plans for the first time. The same model at different scales can differ two or three times over in part count and difficulty. A reliable vehicle MOC plan states its scale right in the title or description rather than leaving you to guess.

## Wheels and suspension: what defines the quality of the chassis

When you open a plan, look first at how the wheels and suspension are designed. For off-road plans, check whether there is independent suspension and how much travel the shocks have — the part numbers for shock absorbers and suspension arms will be written directly into the parts list, and they're hard to substitute if missing. Suspension on sports car plans is mostly decorative, holding a low stance. Construction vehicles use a uniform wheel size but demand serious load-bearing structure, and reinforcing the chassis frame rails is the hard part. If a plan clearly specifies hub size and tire diameter, the author took scale seriously — those plans are usually the more trustworthy ones.

## Play features: are steering and opening doors worth it?

Play features are where vehicle plans trip people up most often. A steering mechanism looks simple, but linking the steering wheel to the front wheels requires precise gear and linkage layout; without step-by-step instructions in the plan, it's easy to build wrong. Details like scissor doors and gullwing doors depend on special hinge parts that are hard to gather. The articulated booms and buckets on construction vehicles need gear trains working together, which is clearly more complex. For newcomers, most entry-level vehicle MOC plans are considered acceptable if the steering is solid — save opening doors and linked mechanisms until you have more experience.

## Difficulty tiers for plans

The tiers commonly used in the community today: one to two stars for pure static exterior models with common parts, suitable for beginners; three stars for models with steering or simple suspension, the level of most sports car and police car plans; four stars for independent suspension plus multiple linkages, where off-roaders and some fire engines sit; five stars for fully mechanical construction vehicles or flagship supercars, which you should attempt only after building a few three- and four-star plans. Choosing a vehicle MOC plan ultimately comes down to matching your own experience and budget — flip through the preview images and step-by-step screenshots before you commit and you'll have a good sense of what you're getting into.
