# Brick Forklift MOC Review: A Real Lifting Mast and Counterweight

A forklift is the most mechanically expressive subject you can build in the construction-equipment MOC genre. What sells it is not the bodywork but the mast and forks that genuinely rise and fall — get that right and a small model keeps you busy for hours. In this review I built a forklift instruction set with a working mast and went through it item by item: the lifting mechanism, the fork assembly, and the counterweight balance.

## 1. Instructions Quality and Structural Fidelity

The headline feature of this plan is that the **mast lift is real**. The designer uses a **rack-and-pinion setup plus slide-rail parts**: turn the handwheel on the side and the forks travel up smoothly by roughly eight centimeters, with damping that feels right and no creeping back down on their own. During the lift, the inner and outer mast rails are held in line by **dovetail-style guide pieces**, so there is no side-to-side slop — noticeably more stable than a plain sliding-rod solution. The instructions mark the lifting travel and the gear meshing positions with **cross-section callouts**, so a careful build usually works first time. The parts list is fully color-separated and breaks out the mechanical components into their own section, which makes it easy to order just the pieces you are missing.

## 2. Fork Geometry and Detail

The fork assembly is where the plan shows real care. The **forks are built from long bars with curved front tips**, giving the slight upward sweep of a real forklift — it looks correct and it helps when you slide a pallet on. Fork spacing can be adjusted slightly to take pallets of different sizes. On the detail side, the **cab roof uses transparent parts**, so you can see the steering wheel and control levers inside; the engine cover at the rear opens to reveal a **small internal-combustion engine model** built from cylinder parts and an exhaust pipe. Front and rear wheels are different sizes, with the rear wheels deliberately kept small to reflect how tight a forklift turns. The color scheme is classic red and black, which reads clearly at a glance.

## 3. Build Experience and Counterweight Balance

The mechanical work — mast, steering, drive — accounts for about a third of the build, and the **mechanics-first, bodywork-second** sequencing makes sense. The model comes in at around nine hundred parts; the mast section demands patience, while everything else moves along quickly. **Counterweight balance** is the make-or-break point on any forklift: the plan puts a removable **counterweight block** at the rear, filled with dark-colored bricks. Unladen it sits rock solid, and with a large pallet on the forks there is enough rear weight to stop the model tipping forward. If you want to lift heavier loads, the design leaves an **extension slot for adding extra counterweight blocks**, which is a genuinely practical touch. The front axle uses **paired wheels side by side** for better grip.

## 4. Pros, Cons, and Who It Suits

Pros: **the lifting mechanism genuinely works, the structure is stable, and the detailing is faithful** — it is as good to display as it is to play with. Cons: the wide variety of mechanical parts pushes **spare-parts cost up**, and the mast rails need a little fine-tuning and lubrication (leave a small gap between the contacting faces of the guide pieces as you build). **Who it suits:** builders who enjoy mechanisms and construction-equipment subjects, and it works very well as a teaching aid for basic mechanical principles. Beginners should build the chassis first to get familiar with the structure before tackling the mast module.
