# Brick Truck MOC Review: Tipper, Semi Tractor and Pickup Guide

Trucks are the most down-to-earth subject in brick vehicle MOC building. Tipper trucks, semi tractors and pickups each have their own appeal, and a truck is often the first vehicle MOC a builder tries. The structures look simple, but there are plenty of traps once you start putting parts together: chassis that flex, cabs with the wrong proportions, cargo beds that will not stay attached. This review draws on several brick truck MOC blueprints I have built myself, and breaks the experience down into the three parts that matter most — chassis, cab and cargo bed.

## Chassis Structure: The Skeleton of a Truck MOC

The chassis is where truck MOCs go wrong most often. A tipper truck or a semi tractor needs a chassis that is long enough and stiff enough, or the whole model sags in the middle as soon as the cargo bed goes on. A good blueprint starts with longitudinal main rails built from technic pieces, then adds cross members along both sides so the two rails form one complete load-bearing frame instead of two separate beams.

When you evaluate a blueprint, focus on two things. First, check whether the front and rear axles use a hub plus driveshaft structure rather than plain wheels pinned to the frame — that is what gives the finished model a believable drivetrain. Second, check whether the suspension is adjustable. A chassis that can be set up for different surfaces is the difference between a static display piece and something that actually feels like a vehicle when you roll it across the table.

## Cab and Details: Proportions Decide Realism

The hard part of a truck cab is proportion. A cab-over truck and a long-nose conventional truck are very different in nose length, and if the blueprint gets the ratio wrong the finished model looks top-heavy no matter how clean the detailing is. I reviewed a cab-over tipper blueprint where the designer used curved pieces and slopes to reproduce the rounded corners of the cab, made the doors openable, and even fitted a steering wheel and seats inside. That level of interior detail is what separates a serious blueprint from a quick shell.

## Cargo Bed and Moving Features

The cargo bed is what decides how useful a truck MOC is to play with. On a tipper truck, the bed is best built as a tipping structure: technic pieces plus pins stand in for the hydraulic ram, so you can simulate the lifting process rather than just posing the bed at one angle. On a semi trailer, the bed should be detachable so you can set up loading and unloading scenes. A pickup is the natural place to add a bed cover and a roof rack, leaning into an off-road custom look. These moving details are exactly the part of a brick truck MOC blueprint that is most worth collecting — they are what you come back to after the first build is finished.

## Overall Scores and Buying Advice

Across the brick truck MOC blueprints I have built, my scores are: chassis structure 9, cab details 8.5, moving features 8.5. A few suggestions for newcomers. Start with a pickup — it has fewer parts and a structure you can read at a glance, so you learn how a vehicle frame works before you take on anything longer. If you want a big scene, move up to a tipper truck and its lifting mechanism. A semi tractor works best when you pair it with a trailer to build a fleet. Whichever type you pick, confirm that the blueprint includes a parts list and step-by-step build diagrams; running out of a key part halfway through is the most common way a truck build stalls.
