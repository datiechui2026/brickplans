# Brick Train MOC Blueprints: Steam, High-Speed and Freight

The hard part of building brick trains isn't the building — it's picking the blueprint. Steam locomotives, high-speed trains and freight trains are structurally completely different machines, and their blueprints sit at very different difficulty levels. This guide to **brick train MOC blueprints** walks through the specs worth checking for each type, so you don't find out halfway through the build that the wheels don't sit on the track.

## Steam locomotive blueprints: detail-heavy, start with driver diameter

Steam locomotives carry the nostalgia. Chimney, boiler, coupling rods, cab — the detailing alone can fill an evening. When choosing this kind of blueprint, look first at driver diameter: the drivers set the scale of the whole model, official track is 8 studs wide in gauge, and 4-stud-diameter wheels give the most harmonious proportions. Second, look at the rod assembly. On a rod-equipped locomotive, check that the crank angles are correct — get the angle wrong and the wheels will seize as they turn. Beginners shouldn't jump straight into a complex full-rod blueprint; start with a rodless or single-rod 4-4-0 to learn on, and pick a plan that clearly labels the gear ratio for each rod.

## High-speed train blueprints: the nose is hardest, and the easiest to fake

The difficulty with a high-speed brick train is the nose. Plenty of blueprints just stack slope bricks into a point — fine in side view, a box from above. A proper high-speed blueprint should build the nose from 2x2 curved and round pieces combined with hinges to create a sloped transition, angle the cab windshield, and use plates and tiles in different shades along the side to suggest the livery. When choosing, check whether the plan includes cross-section views of the nose; if it doesn't, corners have almost certainly been cut. High-speed trains are also mostly display models, so don't agonise over whether it can corner — a straight-track base is actually more stable.

## Freight train blueprints: the cars are the point, the structure the simplest

If you want something that couples up and runs as a multi-car consist, freight is the best choice. The blueprint logic is the simplest of the three: one powered locomotive plus a number of cars, and a car is just a baseplate plus side panels plus a coupler. When choosing, focus on coupler type. Standard couplers have more slack and suit rougher play; magnetic couplers look more realistic but can uncouple on curves, so the blueprint will state a compatible radius — just follow it. Also check whether the plan includes a mix of open-top cars, tankers and flatcars: a single blueprint that builds a whole consist is the best value.

## Track and power: the parts blueprints most often skip

A good **brick train MOC blueprint** is also judged on whether it explains the moving parts. A competent train plan covers at least three things: a motor bay (how many studs of space are reserved for the power module), the gear reduction ratio (1:2 to 1:3 is the usual recommendation), and an opening for the power supply or battery box. A blueprint with no motor bay is basically a static model — decide before you buy whether you want a train that runs or one that sits on a shelf. On track, wide-radius curves are far more stable than tight ones: if a plan's stated minimum curve radius is under R40, it will derail on exit.

## Which of the three to choose

In short: for nostalgia and detail, pick a steam locomotive blueprint and watch the drivers and rods; for a technical look, pick a high-speed blueprint and scrutinise the nose; for consist play, pick a freight blueprint and check couplers and car types.

| Type | What to check first | Watch out for | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Steam locomotive | Driver diameter (4 studs on 8-stud track), rod crank angles | Full-rod plans are hard; start with a rodless or single-rod 4-4-0 | Detail and nostalgia |
| High-speed train | Nose cross-section views, hinged curved slopes, angled windshield | Slope-brick points that look like boxes from above | Display and styling |
| Freight train | Coupler type and stated curve radius, variety of cars | Magnetic couplers uncoupling on curves | Coupling up and running multi-car consists |

For a first blueprint, start with freight or a simplified steam locomotive: parts are widely available, and the cost of getting it wrong is low.
