# Stegosaurus MOC Review: Back Plates and Four-Legged Stance

Short answer first: when you judge a stegosaurus plan, check only two things — are the back plates held by two SNOT connection points, and does the four-legged center of gravity sit directly under the torso? If both are true, a run of 10–14 plates will look right.

The stegosaurus is one of the most recognizable dinosaurs in brick form: two staggered rows of diamond-shaped plates along the back, four spikes on the tail, and a heavy four-legged stance that looks great on a shelf. But how those plates attach and how the animal stays standing are the two hard problems in any stegosaurus plan. This review breaks a stegosaurus MOC plan down by accuracy, plate structure and stance.

## Accuracy: Plate Count and Arrangement

The stegosaurus's signature feature is the row of plates along its back. Real fossils show the plates alternating along the spine, growing from small at the neck to largest over the hips and then shrinking again toward the tail. Start by counting plates: a good plan usually uses 10–14, arranged in two staggered rows rather than a simple parallel line, so the silhouette has depth from the side. Plate shapes combine wedge elements and curved slopes, with 1x1 slope bricks thinning the edges to suggest a thin, bony plate. The head is tiny — a 2x3 brick plus curved pieces is enough — and the point is the exaggerated contrast between that small head and the massive body.

## Back Plates: SNOT Side Mounting and Sandwich Fixing

The plates are the structural challenge of a stegosaurus MOC. A plate standing vertically on the back with only a single connection point will pop off the moment you touch it. Good plans use SNOT technique instead: reserve sideways studs along the top of the torso (using 1x1 bricks with a stud on one side, or SNOT bricks), then build the base of each plate as a sandwich — two plates with a 1x2 brick clamped between them — and press that onto the side studs to create a two-point fixing. The plates then stand firmly on the back and can still be lifted off as a whole row, which makes transport and storage far easier. For color, dark green plates on a light green body, or sand-colored plates on a brown body, gives clearer separation.

## Stance: Technic Skeleton and Center of Gravity

A stegosaurus's front legs are slightly shorter than its hind legs, so the body sits low at the front and high at the back, with the tail extending horizontally behind. The plan builds a technic skeleton inside each limb: bricks with holes plus axles form the leg bones, connectors lock the knee and elbow angles, and bricks and curved elements wrap the outside as muscle. Feet use widened 2x4 plates, so the contact area under each foot is large and naturally stable. The real test is the center of gravity: the middle of the torso is the heaviest section, and the midpoint between the front and hind legs should sit roughly under that mass. The tail does not need to touch the ground, but it must be long enough to visually balance the bulk of the body. Build the legs and chassis first, confirm the model stands level on all four feet, then add the back and the plates.

## FAQ

**Q: Is this stegosaurus MOC plan worth building?**
A: Yes. The stegosaurus is highly recognizable and a reasonable size, and the finished model has real presence on a shelf. The key is choosing a plan that handles both plate fixing and four-legged balance, and the difficulty here is medium.

**Q: How do you fix the plates so they do not fall off?**
A: Do not use a vertical single-stud connection. The mature solution is SNOT side mounting: reserve side studs along the top of the torso, build the plate base as a sandwich of two plates with a 1x2 brick between them, and press that onto the studs for a two-point fixing. The whole row can still be removed in one piece.

**Q: How do you keep a stegosaurus standing on four legs?**
A: Build a technic skeleton inside each limb from bricks with holes plus axles, lock the knee and elbow angles with connectors, and widen the feet with 2x4 plates. The middle of the torso is the heaviest part, so the midpoint between front and hind legs must sit directly under that center of gravity, and the tail should be long enough to balance the body visually.

**Q: How many plates look accurate?**
A: Usually 10–14, staggered in two rows along the spine, growing larger from the neck toward the hips and then smaller again, which is what gives the side view its depth. Shapes combine wedge and curved elements, with 1x1 slope bricks thinning the edges.
