# Dinosaur Park MOC: Baseplate, Dinosaurs and Build Order

The short version first: how good a dinosaur park looks is capped by its baseplate. This design splits a 48x48 baseplate into an entrance plaza, a herbivore zone, a carnivore enclosure and a jungle area, with each zone separated naturally by stepped rock plates and winding belts of palm trees rather than blunt walls of bricks.

Dinosaur scenes are one of the most popular directions in large-scale MOC building, and the "park" framing gives you something a standalone dinosaur model never can: a story. Prehistoric animals, park facilities and visitor traffic all share one diorama, so what you are really building is narrative. This breakdown works through the design from three angles — terrain, dinosaurs and facilities — and gives a build order you can reuse on your own layout.

## Terrain First: The Ground Comes Before the Story

A dinosaur park's visual ceiling is usually set by the baseplate. This plan divides a 48x48 stud baseplate into four functional areas: the entrance plaza, the herbivore zone, the carnivore enclosure and the jungle exploration area. Between them, **stepped rock plates** at varying heights and **winding palm tree belts** do the separating, so the zones read as one landscape instead of stacked rectangles.

There is a useful trick when you build the terrain: lay the base course with 2x4 plates, then step inward along the edges with 1x2 bricks, one layer at a time, to create slopes that look like flaking rock strata. For the water, start with transparent blue plates, add one or two layers of light blue on top for ripples, and finish with a few transparent round plates scattered about to suggest splashes. Once the ground is done, the whole park suddenly gains depth and a sense of place.

## Dinosaur Models: Shape and Scale Are the Trade-Off

Dinosaurs in a park scene do not need the obsessive detail of a display-piece model. What matters more is **silhouette recognition and structural stability**. In this plan the brachiosaurus uses curved tube pieces to describe the arc of its long neck, the diplodocus uses a run of hinge pieces to make an S-shaped tail, and the T-Rex stays upright on a widened leg base.

On scale, follow a principle of visual hierarchy: make the star dinosaurs — brachiosaurus, T-Rex — large and refined, and use simple structures for pterosaurs and small raptors to punctuate the sky and the undergrowth. Tie dinosaur colour schemes to their zone, too. The herbivore zone runs on green and brown earth tones, while the enclosure uses dark grey with red warning accents, so a visitor can read what each area is for at a glance.

## Facility Details: Making the Scene Feel Alive

The extra credit in a park MOC all comes from the facilities: a ticket booth at the entrance, warning signs on the fences, a rope bridge in the exploration zone, even an off-road vehicle carrying visitors. The detail most worth borrowing here is the **fence structure** — 2x2 round cylinder bricks as posts, connected by technic beams as rails. It stays open and see-through while remaining solid, and reads far more like a working park than a solid wall would.

## Recommended Build Order

Work through it as baseplate, then terrain decoration, then large dinosaurs, then small dinosaurs, then facilities and planting, then minifigures as the final touch. Photograph each zone as you finish it so you can adjust everything consistently at the end. Overall, this dinosaur park plan has sensible zoning and solid structure, which makes it a good entry point into prehistoric scene building — following the order above, it takes roughly one to two weekends to complete.

## FAQ

**Q: How should I plan the layout of a dinosaur park MOC?**
A: Start with the baseplate. Use a 48x48 baseplate and divide it into four functional zones — entrance plaza, herbivore zone, carnivore enclosure and jungle exploration area — separated naturally by stepped rock plates and belts of palm trees.

**Q: How do I make the dinosaur models look convincing?**
A: It comes down to shape and scale trade-offs. Large herbivores need convincing neck and body mass, while carnivores need emphasis on the head and hind legs. Better to build fewer species well than to make every dinosaur vague.

**Q: How do I give the scene a sense of story?**
A: Through facilities and visitor flow. Add fences, a viewing walkway, tourist minifigures and signage, and separate the herbivore zone from the enclosure. The picture then tells the story of visitors on a tour, which is far more engaging than simply standing dinosaurs on a plate.

**Q: Any advice on build order?**
A: Terrain before subjects. Lay the baseplate flat in one go, then divide it into zones, then work through terrain, dinosaurs and facilities in turn, and place minifigures and planting last. Rework costs stay low, and you are less likely to crush finished sections.
