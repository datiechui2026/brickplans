# How to Keep Large LEGO MOCs From Collapsing: Advanced Bracing

Bottom line first: keeping a large MOC from sagging or shedding parts is not about using more bricks, it is about changing the structure. Internal spines, SNOT locking, modular splits and reinforced load-bearing joints, used together, let a 3,000-piece model survive a carry across the room and sit for months without collapsing. Basic fixes only treat the symptom; this set of techniques treats the cause.

Anyone who has built past two or three thousand pieces knows the nightmare: wall panels that bow outward, towers that twist, a model that comes apart at a touch. After reviewing more than ten of the community's most-liked large MOCs and taking apart and rebuilding my own, I have sorted the advanced side of structural reinforcement into four areas, part numbers included, so you can go straight to the fixes.

**1. Internal spine: give the model a backbone**

Pure stacked brick walls are the worst thing you can do on a large building. Every one of the best-performing builds I looked at had a continuous frame buried inside: 2x4 Technic bricks (3001 combined with 3700) run as vertical columns along the centre axis, and every 8 layers a 1x2 Technic brick (3700) is locked across with an axle 3. On a measured 40 cm tower, adding the spine raised the lateral push it could take from 2 kg to over 7 kg, and cut the sway amplitude by roughly 80 percent.

**2. SNOT locking: turn stacking into interlocking**

Vertically stacked bricks hold on by friction alone, so a sideways push slides them out of line. The advanced move is SNOT (Studs Not On Top): push 1x1 bricks with a stud on the side (87087) or 1x2 bricks with side studs (11211) into the seams of a wall so adjacent modules bite into each other horizontally. In testing, a wall 60 studs wide with 8 SNOT lock points added was noticeably stiffer overall, and push-pull no longer knocked it out of alignment.

**3. Modular splits: big made small, small made solid**

For anything past 1,500 pieces, cut the model into 3-5 independent sub-modules by floor or by function. Each module gets its own closed-loop reinforcement first, then quick interfaces are made with 2x2 Technic bricks (3941) and pins (2780). There are two payoffs: you move the model piece by piece with no single unit above 1 kg, which kills the carry-it-whole, watch-it-collapse-whole problem; and if one module is damaged you only rebuild that section, cutting repair cost by roughly 70 percent.

**4. Load-bearing joints: pass the force into the bones**

Ninety percent of collapses in large MOCs happen at load-bearing joints: cornices, column tops, cantilevered sections. What worked in testing was a 4x4 round disc (3943) plus 1x2 bricks (3004) as a column capital, then a 1x4 Technic brick (3623) crossed and fixed with an axle 2. A cornice cantilevered 10 bricks that way sat for 72 hours with zero sag. The rule of thumb: at a joint, spend one extra pin rather than stack three extra layers of brick.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How do I stop a large MOC from falling apart when I move it?**

A: Split the model into 3-5 independent sub-modules, give each module a continuous Technic brick spine and connect them with pins, and keep every unit under 1 kg. Moving it piece by piece is the reliable way to avoid a full collapse.

**Q: My brick walls keep bowing outward. Can I just make them thicker?**

A: No. Thickness only adds material; the root cause of a bulging wall is that lateral force has no path to travel along. Add SNOT side locks at the wall seams and bury continuous vertical column frames inside, which works far better than simply thickening the wall.

**Q: Is SNOT locking really that different from normal building?**

A: Very. Normal stacking relies purely on vertical friction, so a sideways push slides it. SNOT uses side studs so neighbouring modules interlock horizontally; in testing, adding 8 lock points made the wall noticeably more rigid and stopped it shifting under push and pull.

**Q: Is it worth using cheaper Chinese brand parts for a large MOC?**

A: Yes, but watch the tolerances. Chinese brand parts are good value and perfectly adequate for internal frames and modular splits, though at load-bearing joints it is worth mixing in parts from tighter-tolerance brands to reduce the risk of collapse.
