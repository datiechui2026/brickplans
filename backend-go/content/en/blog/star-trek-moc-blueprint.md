# Star Trek LEGO MOC Blueprints: Enterprise Build Review

Bottom line first: the hardest part of any Star Trek MOC is closing the edge of the saucer hull cleanly. Start with a shuttlecraft under a thousand parts, then work up to the Enterprise-D.

## Why Star Trek is prime MOC territory

Among brick builders, **Star Trek LEGO MOC blueprints** remain one of the most instantly recognisable categories. Unlike Gundam or Star Wars subjects, Federation ships have no aggressive angles or dense mechanical greebling. What they have instead is a smooth, streamlined hull: the saucer primary hull, the long warp nacelles, and the signature grey, white and red livery. That "simple but not easy" design language is exactly what tests an author's skill, and it is why the subject has stayed popular for so long.

## Classic ships worth the challenge

The must-build list starts, of course, with the Enterprise. Whether it is the TOS-era NCC-1701, the film-era A, or The Next Generation's D, every generation has a completely different hull proportion. The D's saucer paired with a flattened hull is a long-standing wish-list subject for many builders, and the finished model displays beautifully. Next come the shuttlecraft: small, structurally straightforward, and ideal for practice. Compact cruisers like Voyager sit in the middle difficulty band and offer the best value there, with a moderate part count that still carries the full character of the ship.

## Hull shaping: saucer sections and warp nacelles

The core challenge is hull shaping. A saucer hull needs smooth, gradual curvature; the usual approach is SNOT (studs not on top) combined with wedge plates and curved bricks, closing the edge layer by layer so there is no visible stepping when viewed from the side. Obvious steps wreck the accuracy. The warp nacelles then have to solve the "floating" problem: trans-clear elements used as connecting struts keep things rigid while mimicking the suspended look of the engines. Many good blueprints also tuck light kits inside the nacelles, and once lit the model's fidelity doubles — it looks superb in a display cabinet.

## Signature colours and part sourcing

Federation livery is light grey for the hull, dark grey for deck lines, and red accents. On sourcing, light and dark grey are the most widely available colours in the brick ecosystem and can be gathered cheaply through Bricklink, Gobricks and similar channels. Red accent parts are used sparingly, so breaking up a bulk lot or donor set covers them. What is genuinely scarce are the curved bricks and wedge plates the saucer needs. Check the blueprint's parts list before ordering and prioritise those key elements, or you will stall halfway through waiting on parts.

## Difficulty tiers and advice for newcomers

I'd split the **Star Trek LEGO MOC blueprints** on the market into three tiers. Shuttlecraft and small craft are entry level: under a thousand parts, simple steps. The Enterprise-D and Voyager are intermediate, demanding SNOT technique and careful build sequencing. Large-scale subjects like the NX-01 or an oversized Enterprise are expert tier — often tens of thousands of parts, and you will need your own plan for sourcing the gaps. Beginners should start with a mid-sized ship around the thousand-part mark, get the saucer edge technique down properly, then move up to bigger scales; the sense of achievement climbs steadily with you.

## What makes a Star Trek MOC actually good

A good **Star Trek LEGO MOC blueprint** is judged on more than looks. Three things matter: first, structural strength — does a long hull have a spine running through it, or will it go loose after repeated handling? Second, proportion — does the relationship between saucer and nacelles match the source design? Third, build experience — are the steps broken down clearly, and is there any redundant structure added purely for the sake of complexity? Meet all three and it is worth your time and parts.

Happy building, captains — may the warp nacelles always be with you.

## FAQ

**Q: Which ship should a Star Trek MOC beginner build first?**
A: Start with a shuttlecraft or small craft under a thousand parts. The structure is straightforward and the parts are cheap; get the saucer edge technique down first, then take on the Enterprise-D.

**Q: How are Star Trek MOC blueprints graded by difficulty?**
A: Roughly three tiers: shuttlecraft and other builds under a thousand parts are entry level, the Enterprise-D and Voyager are intermediate, and oversized subjects like the NX-01 at ten thousand-plus parts are expert tier.

**Q: Which parts are most likely to run short mid-build?**
A: Curved bricks and wedge plates for the saucer hull are the usual culprits. Light and dark grey are easy to source, so check the parts list before ordering and fill the key gaps first to avoid stalling.

**Q: How is the floating effect on the warp nacelles achieved?**
A: Trans-clear elements act as connecting struts, keeping the assembly rigid while mimicking the suspended engine look. Many blueprints also embed light kits inside the nacelles, which doubles the fidelity once switched on.
