# How to Build a Museum MOC: A Beginner's Guide

If a church MOC is about a sense of the sacred and a castle MOC is about epic scale, a museum MOC is about something quieter: a calm, cultured building that feels like it has a story to tell. A good museum model doesn't need show-off techniques stacked on show-off techniques. What makes people stop and look is that it reads as a small temple of human civilization — columns across the entrance, light falling through a skylight, a dinosaur skeleton and a row of framed paintings waiting quietly in the galleries. This guide walks you through the whole process, from planning to the last brick.

## Step 1: Set the Scale and Footprint

Decide the scale before you touch a brick. Standard minifigure scale (about 1:42) is the best choice, because minifigures can walk inside as visitors and the building instantly comes alive. At minifigure scale, a single-storey museum with an atrium works well on a 32x32 or 48x32 stud baseplate, with the main body held to roughly 30 to 40 bricks tall. Lay out the outline with baseplates first and mark the portico position with temporary pillar supports — far more efficient than building and rebuilding as you go. Once the scale is locked in, every wall thickness, door width and display plinth height follows from it, and the model won't end up looking top-heavy.

## Step 2: Portico and Pediment — The Museum's Calling Card

The signature facade of a classical museum is columns plus a pediment (the triangular gable). In a museum MOC, the portico usually stands on 4 to 6 round columns. Build the capitals by stacking 1x1 round plates to create tiered detail, and stack the shafts from 2x2 round bricks section by section. For the pediment, use slope bricks and triangular plates to form the triangular roof, then finish the edge with a row of dentils — alternating 1x1 jumper plates with 1x2 plates with studs is all it takes. For colour, keep to off-white and light grey as the main tones with dark grey for the base; it reads as stone and ages well. Put a double door in the centre of the facade with pilasters on either side, and the whole thing immediately has presence.

## Step 3: The Skylit Atrium — Make Light Part of the Design

The atrium skylight is the most photogenic structure in the whole build, and it isn't complicated. Leave an opening of roughly 8x8 in the centre of the roof and glaze it with trans bricks or trans panels. Below that, use SNOT (studs-not-on-top) techniques to build a ring of inner beams, and make the atrium two storeys of open void, with the surrounding gallery platforms cantilevered out on 1x2 plates plus railing pieces. To imitate daylight, hide a row of light bricks (the pale glowing kind) under the trans bricks. Switch them on at night and the light spills down the atrium to the ground floor — the atmosphere of the whole building doubles.

## Step 4: Exhibit Design — Dinosaur Skeletons and Picture Frames

The exhibits are the soul of a museum. Two types give the biggest payoff. The first is a dinosaur skeleton: build a simplified T-rex skull in white or tan, add a spine made of alternating 1x1 round plates and short axles, put it on a support frame, and stand it in the middle of the gallery. The second is framed art: use 1x1 tiles with printed pieces, or simply build a frame from dark bricks with a printed picture brick sandwiched inside, and hang a row of them along the wall. Add glass display cases (enclosed with trans bricks plus a light at the base), info labels (1x1 tiles with printed patterns) and a few minifigure visitors, and you have a gallery with an actual story.

## Step 5: Keeping Brick Costs Under Control

Cost is what most builders worry about. A museum MOC is a "lots of small bricks" build, and the bulk of the budget goes on off-white and light grey basic bricks, trans pieces and round column parts. There are three steps to saving money. First, count the quantity of each colour from your parts list, and source basic bricks from bulk brick bags or second-hand bricks first. Second, buy the special parts — round columns, trans pieces — together in one order to consolidate shipping. Third, simplify the texture on large areas such as the roof and the base, and spend the detail only on visual focal points like the portico and the atrium. As a rule of thumb, a standard 32x32 museum can be kept to a brick cost of between 300 and 500 RMB, which is far better value than a retail street scene set of the same size.

## A Final Word

Put the dignity of the portico, the brightness of the skylight and the liveliness of the exhibits together, and you have a museum MOC with a real sense of story.
