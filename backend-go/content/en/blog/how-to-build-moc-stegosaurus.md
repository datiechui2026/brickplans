# How to Build a Stegosaurus MOC: Frame to Plates Tutorial

Bottom line first: build a technic four-legged frame, then SNOT side-mount 10–14 back plates, then shell the body out and run a standing test. Under 300 pieces, a beginner can finish a first stegosaurus in about 1–2 hours.

The stegosaurus is the most recognizable dinosaur MOC and one of the easiest to start with: a stocky body, four feet flat on the ground, two rows of plates along the back, and plenty of shelf presence once it is done. The moment you actually start building, though, two hurdles appear — how to attach the plates, and how to keep four legs standing. This tutorial splits the build into four steps; follow them and you will usually get it right the first time.

## Before You Start: Parts and Instructions

Get hold of instructions with a parts list first (brickplan offers high-resolution downloads with a one-click parts list), then gather parts from that list. A stegosaurus depends on three families of parts:

- **Technic (the frame):** technic beams, cross axles and axle bushes, used for the load-bearing four-legged frame;
- **Slopes and curved parts:** wedge bricks and curved slopes, used for the back plates and the spine;
- **Basic bricks and plates:** 2x4 and 1x2 bricks, 1x4 and 1x2 plates, used to fill out the torso.

Sort everything into trays by colour and type so you are not hunting for parts mid-build. For a first attempt, read the instructions all the way through once to get a feel for the overall proportions before you touch a brick.

## Step 1: Build the Torso Frame and Get the Center of Gravity Right

The hard part of a stegosaurus is not the shape — it is keeping the weight balanced over four feet. The right approach is **frame first, shell later**: build a horizontal main beam from technic beams as the spine, then attach two vertical beams on each side of the spine as the support columns for the four legs, with cross axles pushed through the holes to lock them together.

When the frame is done, press down on it by hand to confirm all four legs take load evenly and nothing wobbles or leans before you go any further. Plenty of beginners fail here because they build the outer shape first and add the frame afterwards — the legs give way and the whole body sags.

## Step 2: Back Plates with SNOT Side Mounting

The soul of the stegosaurus is those two rows of diamond-shaped plates along its back. They cannot be attached studs-up in the normal way; they need **SNOT (Studs Not On Top)** side mounting: turn the side of the brick outward, then apply slopes onto it.

The method: build a lateral brick wall along each side of the spine, thin the plate edges with 1x1 slopes, and alternate wedge and curved parts to reproduce the fossil arrangement — small at the bottom, larger above, then tapering again. Good instructions usually call for 10–14 plates, arranged in a staggered double row rather than two plain parallel rows, so the profile reads with real depth from the side.

## Step 3: Head, Tail and Spikes

The head is the classic small-head-on-a-big-body look: use 2x3 bricks plus curved parts for the snout, and pick out the eyes with 1x1 round plates or printed parts. The point is the exaggerated contrast between the tiny head and the bulky body.

After the tail narrows down, finish the end with four spikes — a real stegosaurus had four tail spikes. Mount thin cone or slope parts at an angle, keeping them left-right symmetrical and at a consistent angle. The tail spikes are the easiest thing to get crooked, so look straight down from above once they are on and check the alignment.

## Step 4: Legs, Shell and Stance Adjustment

Now shell it out: lay basic bricks along the frame to build the body surface, tucking the belly in a little to get that low, heavy look, and add small "toe" parts at the end of each leg so the feet meet the ground more naturally.

When the shell is complete, run the stance test: put the model on a flat table and give it a gentle push to see whether it is stable. If it tips forward or back, the center of gravity is probably too far forward — one or two weight bricks in the tail will fix it.

## FAQ

**Q: What is the first mistake people make when building a stegosaurus MOC?**
A: Shelling the body before adding the frame, which leaves the legs soft and the model sagging. The correct order is to build the technic four-legged frame, confirm the load is even, and only then shell it out — that is how the center of gravity stays stable.

**Q: Why do the back plates have to be SNOT side-mounted?**
A: Because the plates are thin sheets that need to stand vertically on both sides of the spine. SNOT turns the side of the brick outward so slopes can be attached to create that thin-sheet feel; ordinary studs-up building cannot produce the layered look.

**Q: Can I build a stegosaurus with basic bricks only, without technic parts?**
A: Yes, but the four-legged load bearing will be weaker. You can substitute brick columns reinforced with plates for the technic beams and pack the inside of the legs solid; for the mini display tier that is entirely sufficient.

**Q: How many back plates look most accurate?**
A: Good instructions usually use 10–14 plates, growing from small at the neck to large at the hips and tapering again, arranged in a staggered double row instead of two simple parallel rows — that is what gives the fossil-like layering in profile.
