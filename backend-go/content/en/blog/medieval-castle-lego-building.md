# LEGO Medieval Castle Build Guide: Walls, Towers and Moats

Ask any MOC builder to picture a medieval castle and the same image comes up: towering walls, high towers, a moat wrapping the base, and a drawbridge coming down. The appeal is that a castle is a complete defensive system, and every part of it has to look good and do a job at the same time. This guide covers the five areas that decide whether a build works: wall structure, tower design, moat and drawbridge, medieval detailing, and scale planning.

## Wall Structure and Interlocking: The Foundation of a Castle Build

The wall is the soul of the castle and the part that tests your fundamentals hardest. The first lesson is staggered interlocking: every seam between bricks should be covered by the brick sitting above it, so the courses bite into each other the way a real brick wall does. That is what stops a wall from coming apart.

Don't just stack solid bricks. Leave a hollow cavity inside the wall and use pillars or vertical plates as a skeleton, then skin the outside with bricks. It saves parts and stays rigid. Add a horizontal reinforcement course at regular intervals, running long plates through the wall, and a long wall will still hold its shape when you carry it.

## Tower Design: From Square Towers to Round Ones

Towers are the visual high point of the build and the most rewarding part to get right. Square towers are the easy entry point: wrap the four corners with corner bricks, and make the interior a removable cylinder that holds a staircase or hides loose parts. For a round tower with more character, ring the circumference with curved slopes, offsetting each course by half a brick, and taper gradually into a cone roof.

Don't forget arrow slits in the lookout at the top, the thin vertical openings that immediately read as defensive. Tower height should sit at roughly twice the wall height; go much taller and the build turns top-heavy, so press a few extra weighted layers into the tower base.

## Moat and Drawbridge: Water Features That Bring the Castle to Life

You don't need real water. A blue transparent baseplate plus several layers of transparent pieces in different shades gives the surface depth. Build the banks from dark bricks and leave a half-brick drop between the water and the bank, and it reads as real.

The drawbridge works like this: fit two rotating hinge pieces on the inside of the gate, build the deck from light wood-plank tiles for grain, then press two dark strips across it as reinforcement ribs. Raised, the deck slots neatly into the recess in the gate; lowered, it rests on the stone pier on the far bank. The mechanism is simple, but it is the interactive detail builders come back to most.

## Medieval Details: Stone Texture, Crenellations and Flags

Details decide whether a build reads as a castle or as a pile of bricks. For stone texture, mix light and dark grey bricks and swap in an occasional different shade as an old stone, then set a few bricks slightly back to suggest weathering. Studs-out wall bricks also work well for a rough, coarse stone face.

Crenellations are the most classic medieval element of all: alternate studded bricks with low flat plates along the wall top and you have a standard battlement. Don't skip flags on spires and the gatehouse, either. A flagpole piece with a patterned flag standing in the wind instantly gives the castle a lord. A shield-shaped crest plate above the gate ties the whole thing together.

## Scale and Overall Planning: Decisions to Make Before You Start

The place most castle builds go wrong is scale control. The minifigure is your natural ruler: the gate's clear height has to let a mounted figure ride through, crenellations should reach roughly chest height on a figure, and both step height and wall thickness should be measured against the figure's body.

Before you pick up a brick, sketch a top-down plan on paper. Mark the wall perimeter, the number of towers and the moat width, then estimate your parts count so you aren't reordering halfway through. For the foundation, use a baseplate raised by one layer as the castle base, and set the whole castle on a large baseplate so you can carry it out for display later.
