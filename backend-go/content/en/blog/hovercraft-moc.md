# How to Build a LEGO Hovercraft MOC: Skirt and Airflow

Bottom line first: the appeal of a hovercraft is not the hull — it is the skirt and the airflow. A row of hinge pieces gives the skirt its flexible sway, and a cross axle with a three-blade propeller, geared to a crank handle, gives you propulsion you can actually see when you turn it.

A hovercraft is one of the few vessels that never touches the water. A fan forces air under the hull to form a cushion that lifts the craft off the surface, and then a propeller or air rudder drives it forward. Reproducing one as a brick hovercraft MOC is not hard because of the shell; the difficulty is making two abstract ideas — the skirt and the airflow — read clearly in parts. The guide below works from the chassis up to the superstructure, so you can finish with a model that appears to hover over the desk and still looks complete from every angle.

## Skirt Structure: Making the Hull Look Like It Floats

A real hovercraft skirt is a ring of flexible rubber. There is no elastic material in the brick system, so hinge pieces take over that job. Run a row of hinges — 1x2 plates with hinge, for example — around the outer edge of the hull bottom so the skirt panels can swing slightly, then connect several tiles at an angle to build a wavy hem. That reproduces the outline of the skirt and makes the hull read as suspended a short distance off the ground. Keep the hem two to three studs deep: any taller and it looks clumsy, any shallower and the cushion has no visible thickness.

## Fans and Airflow Propulsion

The propulsion system is the soul of this hovercraft MOC. Fit two Technic fans at the stern: a Technic cross axle running through a bush to a three-blade propeller, connected by a gear train to a crank handle. Turn the handle and the blades spin, demonstrating airflow propulsion in the most direct way possible. If you would rather build a static display model, use a transparent curved piece as a shroud instead, leave the blades visible inside, and add transparent blue parts as accents to suggest air rushing through.

## Deck and Cockpit

Lay tiles across most of the deck to get a flat surface, and reserve a cargo area in the center. The cockpit is the visual anchor: use curved elements — a curved windscreen or curved slopes — for a streamlined canopy, transparent parts on both sides as side windows, and place a minifigure with a simple instrument panel inside. Join the canopy to the hull with hinge pieces so it opens and closes, which makes it easy to seat the figure. Do not forget a sloped piece at the bow to suggest the craft running up onto a beach.

## Color Scheme and Details

Classic hovercraft wear white, orange and blue rescue livery. Make the hull white, use orange for the skirt and shroud to create contrast, and keep all transparent parts light blue. Small elements such as lamp posts and bollards can dress the deck, and a printed piece or sticker on the side serves as the hull number. None of this requires special purchases — common basic bricks and Technic pieces are enough.

## FAQ

**Q: My hovercraft skirt keeps collapsing. What should I do?**
A: Add a lateral support beam on the inside of the hem, or replace the hinges with a fixed angle — you trade a little movement for a crisp shape. Connecting the skirt panels diagonally with tiles into a wavy profile also brings the look closer to a real skirt.

**Q: The fans will not turn. How do I troubleshoot?**
A: First check whether the gears are meshing too tightly, then leave a tiny amount of play at both ends of the cross axle. Blades that are too heavy also slow the rotation, so cut back to three blades and confirm the bushes are concentric.

**Q: Which color scheme looks best on a hovercraft?**
A: The classic rescue palette is the safest: white hull, orange skirt and shroud for contrast, light blue transparent parts throughout. Finish with lamp posts, bollards and a printed hull number — no special parts needed.

**Q: How do I make the cockpit openable?**
A: Connect the canopy to the hull with hinge pieces. Use curved elements for the streamlined canopy and transparent parts on both sides for side windows, then fit a minifigure and a simple instrument panel inside. You can display it open or closed.

**Q: Can I turn it into a hovercraft and landing craft combo?**
A: Yes. Leave a connection point in the chassis and use pin pieces to swap the upper module quickly.
