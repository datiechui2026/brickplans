# Whale MOC Blueprint Guide: Building a Blue Whale and Humpback

Among all the subjects builders take on, sea creatures are the hardest to get right, and the whale — the largest marine mammal on the planet — is the endgame for a lot of fans. Whenever I scroll through a whale MOC gallery and hit a model more than fifty centimetres long, I stop and study it. Below is a breakdown of how whale blueprints come together, using the blue whale and the humpback as the reference points.

## Big Volume, Curved Silhouette: Build the Skeleton First, Then Add the Body

The first problem with a whale MOC is sheer size. An adult blue whale reaches thirty metres, making it the largest animal alive today. Reproducing that impact means real volume — but more bricks is not automatically better. The rule is skeleton first. Run a main spine of beams and Technic parts from head to tail, then use hinge pieces to trace the elegant arc of the back: rising gently behind the head, peaking around mid-body, then easing down toward the tail stock.

That single curve carries the character of the whole build. A common beginner mistake is to fill in the body before the shape is settled, and the result looks like a thick cylinder rather than a whale gliding through water. The better approach is to stand up a few curved rib frames first and use them to control the cross-section at each point — belly slightly rounded, back flat and smooth — so the finished model keeps that streamlined swimming posture.

## Skin Transitions and the "Scale" Texture: Detail Drives Realism

Whales do not actually have scales; their skin is smooth but distinctly textured. In brick form we borrow small parts to create a scale-like transitional surface. The gradient from deep blue to light blue to a grey-white belly is the most rewarding part of a whale blueprint. Divide the body into several vertical zones, give each zone its own shade, and use slope bricks at the boundaries to soften the change so the seams never look hard. Pectoral fins should be flat, long and lively, attached with hinge pieces and swept slightly back along the flanks; the dorsal fin needs to flow naturally out of the spine curve. Then run fine horizontal plates across the belly to suggest the throat grooves of a baleen whale, and the skin suddenly reads as alive.

## Tail and Blowhole: Where the Sea Giant Comes Alive

The flukes spread like a fan and are the most dynamic part of the whole animal — in whale blueprints the tail is usually what separates a good build from a great one. Use double-layer wedge plates to shape the fan outline, then hinge the tail stock so it bends naturally, as if the whale were swinging its tail before a dive; with the trailing edge tipped slightly upward, the sense of lightness appears on its own.

The blowhole is the whale's most recognisable feature. On a blue whale it sits forward on top of the head, and the exhaled spray rises in a vertical column. In a MOC you can suggest that column with transparent parts or pale blue small pieces bursting up out of the opening, with a few droplets scattered around it. That one accent instantly gives a static brick whale the illusion of breathing.

## Base and Display Design: Giving the Giant a Home

A model this large deserves better than a hasty stand. Give the whale a semicircular dark blue base, build rolling swells from blue parts in several shades, and top the crests with transparent light blue pieces to suggest moving water. Fix the whale at a slight tilt — dynamic enough to feel alive, but angled so viewers can take in that beautiful curve from end to end. A piece of coral or a starfish in one corner adds a bit of story, though it should never compete with the animal for attention.

What makes building a sea giant so satisfying is that it tests your understanding of form: how to keep proportions accurate at a huge scale, how to build rich layering out of a monotonous blue, how to convey motion with static bricks. Beginners should start with a medium-sized whale and refine the skeleton and curve from there.
