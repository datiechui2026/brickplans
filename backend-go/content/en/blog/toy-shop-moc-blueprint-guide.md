# Toy Shop MOC Blueprint Guide: Windows and Toy Walls

The shop that stops people on the street is rarely the restaurant — it's the toy store. Walls of figures, a glittering window, shelves stacked to the ceiling: instant happiness density. To build a small store that makes people linger, the two make-or-break problems are the window display and the toy wall, and it all starts with picking the right **toy shop MOC blueprint**. This guide walks from choosing a plan through build order so beginners skip the usual detours.

## 1. How to pick a reliable toy shop MOC blueprint

### Set the scale and footprint first

Toy shops are usually designed at 1:48 street-scene scale. A single bay of 16×16 studs fits neatly between neighboring buildings; a double bay of 32×16 has room for a full window plus storefront. Measure your baseplate before you commit, so the finished shop actually fits your block. If you need a corner layout, look for plans with an L-shaped base.

### Check the parts list and printed elements

Toy shops lean heavily on printed parts: the sign, the storefront lettering and the figure faces should be printed rather than stickers, which stay flat and last longer. Before ordering, confirm the parts list is complete and check whether alternate color codes are noted, so you don't come up short on a key color.

## 2. Window display: turn "being looked at" into "being envied"

### Structure of the glass window

The window is the shop's face. Build the big glass panel at the base level with trans-clear plates and a thin frame — a main window 6 studs wide by 8 studs high works well. Use curved trans-clear pieces at the corner so light passes through more cleanly. Raise the interior baseplate by one layer so the figures appear to stand on a platform.

### The three-tier display method

Display works on staggered heights: large figures and a small track train at the bottom, mid-size animals and minifigures in the middle, small accessories on top. Build steps from slope bricks and supports of varying height so the eye is drawn naturally to the focal point.

## 3. The toy wall: full shelving carries the atmosphere

### Building the shelves

For the toy wall, alternate 2×2 pillars with horizontal plates to make the shelving, leaving one empty slot every two studs. Keep shelf depth within 4 studs so it never pushes past the wall; use brown or white for the shelf boards consistently, and let the figure colors become the star.

### Creating the sense of density

The illusion of a packed wall doesn't require actually packing it. Three to five figures per shelf, offset at irregular intervals, is enough. Fill the upper shelves with small items and keep large items low for a dense-top, sparse-bottom rhythm. Add a column of display shelving on each side of the doorframe, and put a giant figure at the corner as the shop's prize piece.

## 4. Signage, counter and overall color scheme

### Sign and storefront

Build the sign from slope bricks for a raised-letter effect, or use a printed sign piece outright. Candy colors suit the storefront — a lemon-yellow doorframe against a blue-green window is instantly recognizable. A red-and-white striped awning is the most photogenic roof in any street scene.

### Counter and floor

Place the counter to one side of the door and complete the circulation with three elements: countertop, cash register and a small shopping bag. Lay a light gray plate as the floor base and add black-and-white checker tiles for extra character.

## 5. Build order and common problems

### Recommended order

Baseplate and walls first, then shelving and the window interior, and finally the sign, awning and minifigures — this avoids rework.

### Common pitfalls

First, thin window glass pieces tend to warp, so reinforce them with a deep-clutch frame during installation. Second, shelves that are too deep bow the wall outward — remember that display beats stacking. Third, wrong colors: check every color code against the list before ordering.
