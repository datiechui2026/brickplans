# MOC Scale Guide: Choosing the Right Ratio for Your Build

The short answer: pick your scale before you pick your parts. Minifigure scale lands at roughly 1:45 and is the most versatile baseline you can work from, and a first build is safest as a small house on a 32x32 baseplate or a 1:72 car.

Before starting any MOC, the first decision is rarely which parts to use but how large the model should be. Scale determines footprint, part count, detail density and build difficulty. Get it wrong and you are looking at rework at best, or tearing the whole frame apart and starting over at worst. This guide breaks down the common scale systems so you get it right the first time.

## Minifigure scale: the most intuitive baseline

Minifigure scale is the most common reference point in MOC building. The "1:1 relative" idea means treating the minifig as your measuring stick: a standard minifig stands about 4 cm tall and represents a person of roughly 1.8 m, which works out to about 1:45. For street scenes, rooms, mechs and shops — anywhere people are meant to be active — minifigure scale is the right call. Build doorways so they clear the top of the minifig's head by one or two units, and keep floor height around 6 to 8 bricks so figures can move in and out naturally. There is a rough-and-ready test for whether a scale reads correctly: stand a minifig inside the model and look at it. If nothing feels off, you are fine.

## Vehicle scale: the classic 1:48 and 1:72

Cars, tanks, aircraft and trains follow a different system. Military and civilian vehicle MOCs commonly use 1:48, putting a complete vehicle at roughly 20 to 30 cm — a good balance between detail and part count. 1:72 is more compact, well suited to squadrons, convoys and diorama scenes. If you want vehicles and minifigs in the same shot, remember: minifigure scale is about 1:45, very close to 1:48, so the two mix directly. 1:72 vehicles are noticeably undersized and pair better with micro scale. For trains, 1:48 gauge is common, and you need to plan enough room for the turning radius in advance.

## Micro scale: big stories in a small space

Micro scale compresses an entire city, a warship or a whole narrative into a palm-sized footprint. It does not chase part-for-part realism; it implies rather than reproduces. A single 1x1 brick stands in for a tower, an antenna becomes a lighthouse. There is no strict number to hit — the core principle is internal consistency. As long as every object in the scene shrinks at the same rate, it reads as visually correct. Its big advantages are a low part count, a small footprint and great photos, which makes it ideal for beginners practising their skills and for display bases.

## Modular baseplates: 32x32 and city blocks

Street-scene MOCs inevitably run into modularity. Classic modular buildings use a 32x32 stud baseplate (about 25.6 cm square), with the building separate from the baseplate so it can be lifted and connected freely. Scale choices have to be made together with baseplate constraints: how tall each floor sits, how many studs wide the windows are, how many studs to leave at the doorway — all of it has to fit the 32x32 footprint. When placing several modules side by side, standardise floor heights and window-line heights so the blocks read as one neighbourhood. If space is tight, 16x16 or 24x24 baseplates work too, but the sense of scale has to be retuned.

## How scale drives part count and detail density

The larger the scale, the more parts and detail you need — almost exponentially. Take the same locomotive: 1:72 might take 300 parts, 1:48 around 800, and minifigure scale (about 1:45) over a thousand. Larger scales let you fit interior structure and working features; the trade-off is that instructions must be more precise and your parts stock deeper. Check your inventory before you commit: if loose parts are scarce, favour micro scale or 1:72; if you have plenty of the same brick on hand, then take on a larger scale. Scale should also match purpose — large scale for pure display, small scale for scene modules you want to produce in quantity, which saves both money and effort.

## Mixed-scale scenes and beginner advice

Skilled builders often mix scales: a high-detail minifigure-scale building in the foreground, micro-scale silhouettes behind it, using visual layering to hide the difference. To learn the trick, remember three rules: foreground large, background small; objects sharing the same plane must share a scale; use terrain, lighting and haze to mask the transition. For beginners: don't overreach on your first MOC. Start with a minifigure-scale cottage on a 32x32 baseplate or a 1:72 car, build your intuition for scale selection first, then work up to micro cityscapes and large-scale vehicles. Every instruction on brickplan lists its scale and part count, so picking projects by those labels saves a lot of detours.

## FAQ

**Q: For a first MOC, how do I decide between minifigure scale and micro scale?**
A: If you want doors and windows — a scene where people are active — use minifigure scale at about 1:45. If you only want to suggest a city or a fleet in silhouette, use micro scale: fewer parts, smaller footprint.

**Q: Can minifigure scale and 1:48 vehicles go together? Is the gap big?**
A: Yes. Minifigure scale is about 1:45, only a hair off 1:48, so they mix in the same shot almost directly. 1:72 vehicles are clearly undersized and suit micro scenes better.

**Q: For the same locomotive, how much does the part count differ between 1:72 and 1:48?**
A: A lot. 1:72 runs about 300 parts, 1:48 needs 800, and minifigure scale at about 1:45 goes past a thousand. The bigger the scale, the harder part count and detail are to control.

**Q: For a modular street, does the baseplate have to be 32x32?**
A: No. Classic modular buildings use a 32x32 baseplate, about 25.6 cm square, which is the most universal. If space is limited, 16x16 or 24x24 works, but floor heights and window lines need readjusting.
