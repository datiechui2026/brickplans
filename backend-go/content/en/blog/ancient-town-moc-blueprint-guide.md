# Brick-Built Ancient Town MOC: Layout, Roofs and Water Lanes

Among Chinese-style builds, an ancient town MOC is the most recognizable subject, and the one that tests your grasp of Chinese structure hardest. Dark roof tiles, white walls, upturned eave corners, a water lane running between the houses — the picture is already there before you place a single brick. Compared with a modern street scene, it asks for more empty space and more deliberate unevenness. Get those two things right and the build has real atmosphere. This guide starts with the layout and works toward a miniature water town that breathes.

## 1. Plan the Water Lane and Main Street First

The soul of an old town is water. Before you start building, sketch a single winding water lane on the baseplate. Lay light blue transparent pieces as the water surface, then add dark blue pieces as ripples. The channel runs through the middle of the town and divides the buildings into two banks: a main street on one side, waterfront houses on the other, with one or two small stone bridges linking them. That "one river, two banks" arrangement gives the scene depth and a story immediately.

Layout is about density and breathing room. Pack the buildings along the river a little tighter, and leave a small square or a dock open at the corners. Don't make the main street run dead straight — offset it slightly, let it narrow and widen, so the eye actually wants to walk down it. Build the houses as modules you can lift out whole. That makes it much easier to run wires for lighting later and to rearrange shopfronts, and modularity is the most practical labor-saving trick on a large town scene.

## 2. Dark Tiles, White Walls and Upturned Eaves

The look of the buildings comes down to dark tiles and white walls. Build wall surfaces from white plates and trim the base course with dark grey or stone-colour pieces. For the roof, stack dark cyan and navy tile pieces into upturned eave corners: raise the corner gradually with slope pieces, then use small reversed pieces to create the upward curl. Press a straight ridge line along the top with long dark bar pieces, and you have a clean Chinese roof.

There is a useful order to building eaves: frame first, tiles after. Build the roof pitch skeleton in dark grey pieces, then tile layer by layer from the bottom up with staggered joints. It stays stable and gives the layered look. Gable walls can be flush gable style or horse-head wall style — stack white tall wall pieces into stepped fire walls. Put a few horse-head walls at different heights side by side and the Jiangnan silhouette appears at once. Use dark brown wood-colour pieces for doors and windows, with red lanterns as accents: restrained, with a note of festivity.

## 3. Covered Bridge, Stone Street and Shop Details

The covered bridge is the most photogenic structure in the town. Build the bridge body and the bridge pavilion from wood-colour beam and column pieces, deck it with stone-colour plates, and add wooden railings on both sides. The river passes underneath; put an oil-paper umbrella and a pedestrian minifigure on the deck and the scene comes alive. For the stone street, combine light grey and dark grey small plates with staggered joints. Don't lay them too flat — slight bumps read as an old street worn smooth by time.

Shops along the street are where the lived-in feel comes from: a tea house with a hanging banner, a pastry shop with stacked steamer baskets, a cloth shop with bolts of fabric hanging out, a tavern with wine jars set out front. Use vertical wooden plaque pieces for the signs, add red lanterns and warm yellow lantern light, and the whole street feels inhabited. At the waterfront houses, add a few stone steps down to a small dock and moor a black-awning boat there — the daily life of the water town writes itself.

## 4. Colour, Lighting and Finishing Advice

Colour in an old town depends on leaving space empty. White walls should take the largest area, blue-grey roofs cap them, wood-colour doors and windows plus lanterns act as accents, and at most you add one touch of green — dark green small pieces for plants and moss — to break it up. Don't fill every surface with colour; restraint is what gives a Chinese ancient town its confidence.

For lighting, hang warm yellow lanterns under the eaves, let warm white light show through the interiors, and use a blue-purple light strip on the water to suggest reflections. Switch it on as daylight fades and the misty, rain-soaked mood arrives instantly.

A sensible build order is water system and main street first, then the building modules, then the covered bridge and shops, and finally the lighting all at once. Stock up on white plates, dark cyan tile pieces, wood-colour column and beam pieces, and lantern and light pieces.
