# Farm MOC Tutorial: Fields, Barn and Farmhouse

Of all the themes you can build in brick, a farm is one of the most relaxing. It doesn't chase the tight, straight lines of a street scene, and it doesn't hinge on poseable joints the way a mech does. A farm MOC lives on horizontal layering, a warm and coordinated palette, and that instant sense of somewhere people actually live. This tutorial breaks a lively mini farm down piece by piece, from the baseplate up to the barn roof.

## Baseplate Planning: Set the Layout Before You Touch a Brick

The first thing you build is not the barn — it's the ground. Start from a 32x32 baseplate. Go much larger and the project tends to stall half-finished; go much smaller and you can't fit both a proper set of crop rows and a farmhouse. While you plan, reserve a zone for each job: the barn in one corner as the visual anchor, the fields taking up a broad slab, and the pond and fences threaded through the gaps between them.

Don't leave the ground dead flat. Stack slope plates into a low, gently rolling rise, then lay a small pond from transparent tiles, and the ground plane instantly gains depth.

## Barn and Farmhouse: The Scene's Focal Point

The barn is the face of the whole farm, so the structure has to play up the roof and the door. Build a double-pitched roof from red slope bricks and cap the ridge with a dark ridge brick running down the middle. Make the arched front door generous — large enough to slide hay bales or a tractor inside. Use white or beige plates as the base for the walls, then run brown bricks over them as timber framing; two exposed upright posts read far better than one solid wall.

Pair the barn with a small farmhouse beside it, adding a chimney and a little attic window. The difference in height is what gives the cluster its charm. This section sets the tone for the entire farm MOC, so keep it to three main colours — red, white and brown — with at most a touch of grey as an accent.

## Fields and Fences: Laying Out Neat Crop Rows

The fields are where a farm build breaks away from a street scene. The method is simple: lay green plates as the base, then swap in brown or tan plates every other row to create the furrows. Plant neat rows of 1x1 green round plates along the ridges as seedlings, and harvest season has arrived.

For the fence, ring the plot with brown bar pieces and posts, and leave a working gate at the entrance — a couple of hinge pieces let it swing open and shut. It looks good and it marks the edge of the scene. Don't fence everything in too tightly: leave a gap that leads through to the pond, and the flow through the layout feels natural.

## Animals and Plants: Injecting Life Into the Scene

A farm without animals is incomplete. A cow, a pink pig, a chicken — the ready-made animal pieces are the least effort by far. If you want more of a DIY flavour, small figures built from smooth plates work just as well.

For planting, use brown trunk pieces with dark green and light green foliage interleaved to make a few trees, then scatter them at the corners of buildings and along the pond edge. Stack orange plates into small rounded piles for hay bales and the harvest atmosphere jumps immediately. These accessories take up very little space, yet they are exactly what draws the eye and supplies the energy in the frame.

## Colour and Detail: What Decides the Finished Quality

One rule covers the palette: keep the body warm and the accents bright. A red roof, white walls and brown fences are the safe combination for a farm; the green of the fields and the blue of the pond do the lifting, while yellow or orange hay adds the warmth. Details are what separate a decent build from a great one — a well in the yard, a swallow's nest under the eaves, a mailbox and a scarecrow by the front, and a rutted lane laid from grey bricks. All of them make the farm look as though someone has lived there.

If you're after realism, weather a few wall sections slightly, using dark small bricks to mimic the marks of sun and rain.

## Build Order Summary

Run through the sequence one last time: lay the baseplate and stack the terrain first; then build the barn and farmhouse to lock in the visual centre; next come the fields and fences, spreading out the crop rows; after that, place the animals and trees as accents; and finish with the details — the path, the scarecrow and the rest. Follow this order and you'll be far less likely to tear it all down and start over.
