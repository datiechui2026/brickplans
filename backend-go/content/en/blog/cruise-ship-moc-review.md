# Cruise Ship MOC Review: Inside a 2,000-Piece Ocean Liner

## Instruction Quality and Scale

This is a 2,000-piece-class cruise ship MOC, and the parts list alone runs to four pages. The designer has helpfully grouped the parts by deck level, so you can buy and sort in stages instead of dumping everything onto the table at once. The step diagrams use a two-view layout — top-down plus side elevation — and wherever the hull curves in, there is an extra cross-section view to show how the arc is closed. The level of polish sits close to what you would expect from a commercial set's instruction booklet.

Because of the sheer size, building deck by deck in separate batches is strongly recommended. Gathering every part before you start makes a noticeable difference to how the whole project feels.

## Structural Accuracy and Hull Layering

The structural reproduction is the headline act here. Working up from the keel, the hull stacks in order: engine room level, cabin level, deck level, and finally the bridge. The designer uses different brick heights to mark out each functional zone, so the layering reads very clearly even before any detailing goes on.

The bow gets a proper bulbous bow, and the stern carries the funnel and lifeboats. Stripe detailing on the funnel immediately gives the ship that long-haul ocean liner look. Up on deck, the leisure facilities are all present — pool, sun loungers, parasols — and the density of small detail is high throughout.

## Color Scheme and Detail

The palette is classic cruise liner: a predominantly white hull, blue decking, and a red funnel for contrast. It is an enduring combination that looks good on a shelf and photographs well.

The designer leans heavily on trans-clear parts for portholes and glass curtain walls, and light passing through them reads beautifully. The deck-level leisure zone uses mini accessories in a range of colors; the individual pieces are tiny, but arranged neatly they add a lot of life. Finished, the model measures over fifty centimeters long, and it has real presence.

## Build Experience and Time Cost

The build runs roughly ten to twelve hours in total, best split across three or four sittings. The repeated cabin modules along the bottom of the hull get a little tedious, but once you reach deck level, small scenes start appearing one after another and the fun picks up noticeably.

A couple of practical notes. Some of the long, thin parts can warp in transit, so check them before you begin. Trans-clear parts clutch tightly, so take care when you need to disassemble. Overall it is a large project that demands patience but pays you back generously.

## Pros, Cons, and Who It Suits

**Pros:** professional structural reproduction, rich detail, an impressive finished size, and first-rate display presence.

**Cons:** a very large part count and a correspondingly high cost, a long build time, and trans-clear plus long thin parts that need careful handling. It is not a project for impatient beginners.

This one suits experienced builders who enjoy large ship subjects. If large-scale vessel designs are your thing, you can search "cruise ship" or "cargo ship" on BrickPlans (a MOC instruction sharing platform, https://brickplan.cn) — the site carries maritime designs at a range of different scales.
