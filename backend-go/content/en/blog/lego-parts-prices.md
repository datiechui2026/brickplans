# LEGO Parts Prices: A Guide to Buying Individual Bricks

Half the fun of the hobby is building, the other half is collecting — and collecting eventually runs into price. Two 2x4 bricks can sit at wildly different points on the scale, and a MOC builder working through a parts list can watch most of the budget disappear on fill-in orders alone. Here is how LEGO part prices actually work, where to buy loose parts without overpaying, and whether compatible brands are worth using.

## Per-Piece Prices: From Cents to Hundreds

LEGO part prices vary enormously, driven mainly by rarity and mould cost. Common basic elements stay cheap:

- **Basic bricks (1x1, 2x4 and so on)**: roughly RMB 0.1–0.5 each, with less common colours costing more.
- **Standard plates, slopes and curved pieces**: roughly RMB 0.2–1 each, the everyday backbone of any parts order.
- **Gears, hinges and special connectors**: roughly RMB 1–5 each, mostly used for structural connections.
- **Rare and exclusive parts**: anywhere from RMB 10–100 each — printed elements, transparent pieces, or moulds exclusive to one set.

For most builders the real expense isn't standard parts, it's old moulds that have been out of production for years. That's why long-time hobbyists stockpile: once a part retires, its price often multiplies several times over.

## Where to Buy Loose Parts Cheaply

The thing to avoid when buying individual parts is paying over the odds. Here is how the main channels compare:

1. **Official Pick a Brick wall (PaB)**: buy by the cup or by the piece at a low unit price. Good for bulk basic parts, but stock is random and the selection is limited.
2. **Official Bricks & Pieces online store**: the widest selection and guaranteed authenticity, but higher unit prices and a minimum order quantity. Best for small top-ups of scarce parts.
3. **Secondhand parted-out market**: the best value by far, and many MOC builders fund their hobby by breaking down sets. Buying a whole set to part out usually lands 30%–50% below the cost of buying the same pieces individually.
4. **Compatible parts from Chinese brands**: the lowest prices, and hard to avoid on a tight budget — but you need to check compatibility and colour matching piece by piece.

A tip: the same part can differ several times over in price between channels. Compare in bulk with a parts tool before you check out rather than paying on impulse.

## How Compatible Chinese Parts Compare

Chinese brick brands have developed fast in recent years, and both tolerance and feel have improved. For parts buyers, the trade-offs look like this:

- **Price**: compatible parts typically cost only 1/3 to 1/5 of genuine LEGO, so the same budget buys far more pieces.
- **Precision and feel**: the major manufacturers now control clutch power and tolerances well, though some less common parts still vary in how tight or loose they sit.
- **Colour variation**: the colour systems are not identical to LEGO's, and the difference is most obvious in dark colours — watch for it when mixing.
- **Compatibility**: basic bricks, plates and common structural elements are mostly fully compatible, but plated parts, light-up elements and patented structural pieces still differ.

**The takeaway**: use compatible parts where they save money — internal structure and anything hidden. Use genuine LEGO for visible surfaces and anywhere a precise fit matters. Mixing the two is the smartest way to control a parts budget.

## Sourcing Advice for MOC Builders

If you build MOCs regularly, buying parts needs a strategy:

1. **Make the list first, then buy**: turn the parts list from your instructions into a single order so you don't pay shipping several times over.
2. **Buy basics in volume**: stock up on common bricks and plates through PaB or secondhand part-outs to push the unit cost as low as possible.
3. **Move early on rare parts**: when a part is close to retiring and the price is still reasonable, buy it — don't wait for the aftermarket to spike.
4. **Mix in compatible parts**: use them for the internal structure of anything that isn't a display model and save a serious amount of budget.
5. **Keep your colour families consistent**: a MOC lives or dies on how it looks as a whole, so try to source one colour family from a single channel — mismatched colour batches can spoil the finished build.

## A Final Word

Once you understand the pattern, pricing stops being intimidating: save with compatible parts where it doesn't show, spend on genuine LEGO where it does, and put the budget where people will actually see it.
