# Brick Globe MOC Review: A Spinning World at 40 cm

The short version first: this brick globe MOC measures about 40 cm across, builds a near-perfect sphere using a segmented-ring-plus-vertical-rib frame, and genuinely spins once finished. Ocean blue covers roughly 70 percent of the surface. It works as a display piece and as a geography teaching aid at the same time.

Brick globes have become one of the most popular MOC categories in recent years. They keep the practical appeal of a geographic ornament while offering plenty of building fun. The model reviewed here uses a sphere around 40 cm in diameter paired with a curved stand, and the finished globe really rotates. Put it in a room and you get style plus a bit of learning.

## Shape Accuracy: The Cleverness of the Sphere Frame

Building a true sphere out of bricks is the hardest part of this type of model. This globe MOC uses the classic "segmented rings plus vertical ribs" skeleton: several rings of curved bricks establish the lines of latitude, vertical brick columns connect them into a latitude-longitude grid, and small panels fill the gaps in that grid to complete the spherical surface. The builder uses deep blue for the oceans and splits the continents into green and earth-yellow zones. The outlines of the five continents are drawn with combinations of differently shaped bricks, so they read clearly from a distance and, up close, become a pixel-style world map made of several thousand bricks.

## Color Scheme: Clean Blues and Greens

Ocean blue is the base color and takes up roughly 70 percent of the sphere. The landmasses use grass green, light green and earth yellow to distinguish plains, mountain ranges and deserts, and a ring of gold printed tiles runs near the equator as a decorative band. The stand and base are dark brown, giving a warm-cool contrast against the blue sphere. The overall palette is clean and bright, and it fits just as well in a study as in a child's room.

## Movable Design: A Globe That Actually Turns

Unlike static architectural MOCs, movement is the core selling point here. The sphere mounts on the curved stand via an axle running through the north and south poles, with smooth axle sleeve parts fixing both ends. The rotation damping is well judged: a light push sets it spinning smoothly, with no slop and no binding. There is also a small compass decorative element at the base of the stand. It does not really indicate direction, but it is a fun touch. Some advanced versions of the build also let the sphere tilt 23.5 degrees along its latitude line, reproducing the Earth's axial tilt and pushing the display quality up another notch.

## Build Experience: The Challenge Starts at the Sphere

The first half of the build (base and stand) is easy going and takes about half an hour. The real challenge begins with the sphere: the latitude rings have to be aligned precisely to the instructions, and a small offset affects how flat the surface ends up. The designer splits the sphere into upper and lower hemispheres built separately, then joins them with hinge parts; closing them requires a little patience to line up the seam. Total build time runs about 8 to 10 hours. The middle stretch is fairly repetitive, but watching the sphere take shape is genuinely rewarding.

## Parts List

The model contains roughly 3,200 pieces. The workhorses are 1x2 curved bricks and 1x3 curved plates, which form most of the spherical surface. Next come large smooth tiles, used to give the oceans their flat, glossy feel. The continent outlines use a small number of slope bricks and wedge bricks. Special parts include the axle, axle sleeves, curved stand connectors and the gold printed tiles. There are not many of them, but every one is critical. The parts list in the instructions is bagged by hemisphere, which makes finding pieces very efficient.

## FAQ

**Q: How does a brick globe MOC get close to a true sphere?**
A: Through the "segmented rings plus vertical ribs" skeleton: curved bricks form the latitude rings, vertical brick columns connect them into a latitude-longitude grid, and panels fill the gaps in the grid to create a complete spherical surface.

**Q: How big is the finished globe, and does it turn?**
A: The sphere is about 40 cm in diameter and sits on a curved stand, and the finished model really rotates. Deep blue marks the oceans, while green and earth yellow zones mark the continents. The outlines read clearly from a distance, and it is educational to have on display.

**Q: How many parts does a brick globe take?**
A: It is a mid-to-high piece count project, with the sphere built from several thousand bricks and the whole model often above 2,000 pieces. Curved bricks, panels and small square bricks dominate. Working section by section, ring by ring, is the recommended approach.

**Q: Is a globe MOC a good challenge for beginners?**
A: It is better as a second project. The sphere is repetitive and demands patience, and following the instructions takes about 10 to 15 hours. Beginners are safer starting with a vehicle or small building under 500 pieces.
