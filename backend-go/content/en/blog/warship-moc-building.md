# Brick Warship MOC Review: Hull Lines and Turret Build Tips

Verdict first: a warship is the ultimate test of line work. Lay bricks sideways with SNOT to build the hull sides, taper inward course by course following the plan, and you get that waisted curve running from bow to stern — then you layer the deck and turrets on top.

Warships are one of the hardest subjects in brick MOC. They are not boxy like a tank, and they do not win on pure streamlining like an aircraft. A warship's beauty comes from the hull silhouette above the waterline and the density of detail on the deck. This plan is based on a modern destroyer, roughly 70 cm long, which puts it at about 1:300 display scale. Here is the build broken down into three layers: hull, deck and turrets.

## Hull Lines: From Keel to Waterline

The place warship MOCs go wrong most often is a hull that gets fat — from the side it reads like a flat loaf of bread. The smart move in this plan is using SNOT for the hull sides: bricks are laid sideways so the smooth face points outward, then stepped inward course by course as drawn, producing the waisted curve from bow to stern.

Build the inner keel frame first, running technic bricks lengthwise through the whole ship, with a transverse rib every 4 studs. That is what keeps a 70 cm hull from feeling soft when you pick it up. The waterline is color-separated with dark gray against the main hull color, plus a ring of thin plate and tile strips to simulate the boot topping — visually it instantly reads as a ship rather than a brick box. The bow sheer, the upward curve of the deck, has to be stepped up course by course exactly as the plan shows. That curve is where the model's character comes from, so do not rush it.

## Deck Layering: Big Planes First, Small Parts After

The deck is the densest area of the build. The plan splits it into three sections: the forward section carries the main gun and the close-in defense system, the midships section holds the bridge and funnel, and the aft section is the helicopter platform. Each section is assembled on its own small baseplate and then mounted onto the hull as a complete unit. That makes each stage easier to handle, and it avoids flipping the whole 70 cm ship over and over, which is how structures start working loose.

Deck line detailing uses 1x1 tiles laid stud by stud to mimic the anti-slip deck texture. Side railings combine 1x2 parts with bar ends and short transparent rods, which keeps them open and see-through while still reading as metal. The helicopter platform is marked out with dark gray round plates forming a circle, and the detail lands nicely at this scale.

## Turret Details: A Warship Is Only Good If It Trains

The turrets are the most praiseworthy part of this plan. The main turret connects to the deck through a turntable part, giving a full 360-degree rotation. The barrel is built from round rod elements with a thicker muzzle end, and elevation is fine-tuned with ball joints. The close-in defense gun uses small universal joints for a movable mount, so play value is high.

Because barrels and radar parts are small, order about 10% extra spares so a lost piece does not stall the build. On compatibility, the plan lists generic part numbers, and Gobricks parts substitute perfectly well. Overall, this warship plan is solid on both hull lines and turret detail. It suits builders with intermediate experience, and the finished model absolutely holds its own on a display shelf.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I stop a brick warship hull from getting fat?**
A: The hull sides are the key. Use SNOT to lay bricks sideways with the smooth face out, then step inward course by course following the plan to form the waisted curve from bow to stern. Viewed from the side, it no longer looks like a flat loaf of bread.

**Q: How should the deck be layered?**
A: Big planes first, small parts after. Lay the main deck plane with large plates, then stack the superstructure and hatches on top. Two layers give the deck a height difference, and the ship only becomes interesting to look at once the detail density is there.

**Q: How do I make a turret look right?**
A: A warship is only good if it trains. Fit a turntable part under the turret for 360-degree rotation, and build the barrel from a long cylinder part or a technic axle. The barrel length should be about 0.4 to 0.6 times the beam of the ship — get that proportion right and the presence follows naturally.

**Q: Which details matter most on a warship?**
A: Masts, radar antennas, naval guns, lifeboats and railings are the priorities. These are piled up from thin rod parts and small plates, and you need to keep them symmetrical left to right so the finished model has the working feel of a real warship.
