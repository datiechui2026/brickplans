# Brick Tiger MOC Blueprint: Complete Beginner's Guide

Tigers are perennial favourites in the animal MOC scene. The black-and-yellow striping, the heavy forelimbs and that commanding head were practically made to be built in brick. Yet plenty of builders download a tiger MOC blueprint, start snapping, and end up with something that looks more like a large house cat. Usually the problem is the blueprint they picked or the order they built it in. This guide walks through the whole workflow, from choosing a file to finishing the model.

## Step 1: How to Pick a Blueprint Worth Building

Tiger MOC blueprints vary wildly in quality. When you are comparing options, check three things first:

- **Is there a complete parts list?** A usable blueprint should give the part number and quantity for every element, so you can order exactly what you need instead of guessing.
- **Are the steps granular enough?** For complex areas like the head and limbs, look for a blueprint with more than 30 steps. Fewer than that and you will spend half the build squinting at one image and improvising the rest.
- **Are there multiple angles of the finished model?** Front, side and top views let you judge the proportions before you commit, so you do not discover after the last brick that the head is oversized and the body too short.

## Step 2: Breaking Down the Tiger MOC Structure

A well-made tiger blueprint usually divides the model into four modules:

1. **Torso skeleton:** Lay 2x4 and 2x6 bricks in a cross-bonded pattern to form the internal frame, then attach plates to the outside using side building (SNOT). This saves parts and keeps the whole thing rigid.
2. **Limbs and paws:** The forelimbs should be noticeably thicker than the hind legs. Stack 1x1 round plates with studs to suggest the toes, and swap the claw tips for dark-coloured elements to sharpen the contrast.
3. **Head:** This is the soul of the model. Build the muzzle from wedge bricks angled into a stepped, protruding shape, and add two triangular pieces above the eyes to mimic the dark brow markings.
4. **Tail:** Link several hinge pieces in a chain, then cap the end with a dark segment so the tail reads as curled and in motion.

## Step 3: Three Common Ways to Handle the Stripes

Striping is the dividing line between a generic big cat and a convincing tiger. There are three main approaches:

- **Stickers or printed parts:** Quick and easy, but they limit posing and look flat. Fine for a first attempt.
- **Colour-swapped bricks:** Replace the yellow bricks along the flanks with black ones, dropping them in wherever the blueprint places a stripe. This gives the most three-dimensional result.
- **Side-mounted layers:** Hang 1x2 black bricks off the sides of the body to form the stripes. Strong depth, but it eats more parts.

Beginners should start with the second method, then move on to the third once the build feels comfortable.

## Step 4: Parts and Cost Estimate

For a mid-sized build of around 1,200 pieces, the parts budget breaks down roughly like this: basic bricks and plates run about 200 to 300 CNY, shaped elements such as wedge and curved slope bricks add 150 to 250 CNY, and hinges, printed parts and other special pieces come to around 100 to 200 CNY. That puts most builds in the 500 to 700 CNY range. If you already have a loose-parts stash at home, your real outlay will be considerably lower.

## Step 5: Common Failure Points to Watch

Three problems come up again and again:

- **Do not build the torso too slim.** A tiger needs bulk and weight. When in doubt, add more.
- **Keep the stripes symmetrical.** Note where you placed each black brick on the left flank, then mirror that pattern on the right.
- **Reinforce the limb joints with cross axles.** Without a technic axle through the connection, the finished model will not stand up and tends to topple.
