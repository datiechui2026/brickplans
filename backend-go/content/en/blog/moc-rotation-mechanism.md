# Brick MOC Rotation Mechanisms: Gears, Turntables and Axles

Short version first: the secret to a good rotation mechanism is gearing down for more torque, matching a big turntable to a big load, and locking the axle in place with a cross axle plus bushes. And always spin-test the mechanism before you close up the shell.

Rotation is the simplest way to make a MOC move, and it delivers the biggest visual payoff. A Ferris wheel turning slowly, a lighthouse lamp sweeping across the water, a display stand rotating a model so every angle gets its turn — all of these rely on a **brick MOC rotation mechanism**. Plenty of builders hit the same wall on their first attempt: it either won't turn at all, or it stutters and judders. This guide covers the core points, from gears and turntables to axle fixing, so your build spins smoothly.

## Gears: Understanding Ratios and Direction

Gears are the power heart of a brick MOC rotation mechanism. When two gears mesh, the tooth count decides speed and torque: a small gear driving a large gear lowers speed and raises torque, which suits heavy loads that need to turn slowly. The reverse gives high speed and low torque for fast-spinning applications. The simplest rule is "small driver, large driven." For a high-load structure like a turntable, an 8-tooth gear driving a 24-tooth or even 40-tooth gear both reduces speed and multiplies force, so the motor doesn't stall. One more thing: two gears meshing directly rotate in opposite directions. If you need both turning the same way, add an idler gear — a detail that gets overlooked constantly.

## Choosing a Turntable: Big Load, Big Turntable

The turntable is the single most important large part in a rotation mechanism. It has a built-in bearing and can carry a substantial lateral load, which makes it ideal for big, heavy structures such as Ferris wheels, rotating restaurants and carousels. Remember one principle when choosing: the bigger the load, the bigger the turntable. Small display pieces are fine with a standard-size turntable; a large Ferris wheel calls for an oversized one, and preferably a version with locking teeth — lock the position during assembly, then release it to rotate once the model is finished, so the turntable doesn't slip while you're building. Fix the upper and lower halves of the turntable to the base and the upper structure respectively, leaving only the central bearing free to rotate. Otherwise the whole structure will wobble along with it.

## Fixing the Axle: Vertical vs. Horizontal

There are two kinds of axle in a brick MOC rotation mechanism, and they're handled quite differently. The vertical axle (a rotating platform, a lighthouse lamp head) is the easy one: run a cross axle down through the centre of the turntable, limit it at the bottom with a bush and a pin, and lock it off where it exits the top so the axle can only rotate, never slide. The horizontal axle (Ferris wheel, water wheel) needs particular attention to support on both sides: both ends of the axle need a bearing block or a brick with a hole, the rotating face must stay perpendicular to the supporting face, and both ends must sit at the same height — otherwise you get a see-saw judder. Whichever type you use, secure the axle with a cross axle and bushes; less play means smoother rotation.

## Motor Drive: Manual or Powered

Rotation mechanisms come in manual and motorised flavours. Manual builds run off an exposed gear or a crank that you turn by hand — simple, and good for practice. Motorised builds add a motor and battery box for continuous, even rotation, which looks far better on display. When building motorised, first bring the motor's output shaft down to a suitable speed with a set of reduction gears, then send the drive to the turntable axle through a flexible axle or a universal joint. The shorter and straighter the transmission path, the less binding you get. Plan the power supply early: hide the battery box inside the base, route the cable along the inside of the structure, and never run it through the middle of a rotating part — the rotation will eventually twist it apart. ## Parts and Testing: Spin It Before You Shell It

When you build a **brick MOC rotation mechanism**, have these on hand: cross axles, bushes, pins, assorted gears, one large turntable, and a spare motor. Spin-test every gear train before installing it: turn the input shaft by hand, watch whether the drive runs smoothly, and only then add the shell and decorative parts. If the gears mesh too tightly, leave a little clearance; if they're too loose, add a bush as a limit. Once it turns, check stability: widen the base on high-load structures, or hide counterweights inside it — the lower the centre of gravity, the steadier the build. ## FAQ

**Q: My rotation mechanism won't turn and keeps jamming. What should I check?**
A: Start with the gear ratio and axle play. For high loads like a turntable, use an 8-tooth gear driving a 24-tooth or 40-tooth gear to reduce speed and increase torque. Fix the axle with a cross axle and bushes. If the gears mesh too tightly, leave clearance; if too loosely, add a bush.

**Q: How do I pair gears so it turns both smoothly and with enough force?**
A: Follow "small driver, large driven": a small gear turning a large gear lowers speed and raises torque, which suits heavy loads turning slowly. Two gears meshing directly rotate in opposite directions — add an idler gear if you need them turning the same way.

**Q: What kind of turntable should a large structure like a Ferris wheel use?**
A: The bigger the load, the bigger the turntable. A large Ferris wheel is best served by an oversized turntable, ideally a locking-tooth version so you can lock the position during assembly. Fix the top and bottom halves to the base and the upper structure respectively.

**Q: What should I watch out for when motorising a rotation mechanism?**
A: First reduce the motor's output shaft speed with a set of gears, then route the drive to the turntable axle through a flexible axle or universal joint — the shorter, straighter and smoother the path, the better. Hide the battery box in the base and run the cable along the inside of the structure.
