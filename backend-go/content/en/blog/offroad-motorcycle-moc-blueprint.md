# Off-Road Motorcycle MOC: Tires, Suspension and Rider Pose

Start with the short version: an off-road motorcycle MOC lives or dies on three things — knobby wide tires, spring-loaded suspension and forward-raked handlebars — with the center of gravity pressed into the middle of the frame so the bike stands on its own. For the tires, go wide: 56×36mm is the go-to size.

Dirt bikes have long been a favorite subject for brick builders. Chunky tires, exaggerated suspension travel and a rider leaning hard into a corner make a static model feel like it is moving. But instructions alone won't get you a bike with character — hubs, suspension, drivetrain and rider pose each need attention. This guide draws on popular community blueprints and covers everything from parts selection to the finished build.

## 1. Off-Road Tires and Hubs: The First Step

The most obvious difference between a dirt bike and a street bike is the rubber. Prioritize wide-tread, deep-lug off-road tire parts — the ones with big blocky studs. Diameters typically run 56×36mm or 49.6×36mm, paired with an oversized hub so the proportions hold up. For hubs, pick a spoked design or a five-spoke off-road hub; darker colors age better visually. If a blueprint only specifies standard street wheels, you can swap in off-road hubs yourself — but check that the tire's inner diameter matches the hub's outer diameter, or the tire will sit loose and slip off.

## 2. Suspension and Shocks: Getting the Springs Right

Dirt bikes have long suspension travel, and the front forks usually use shock absorber units with springs. Angle the forks slightly forward to mimic a real bike's steering rake — it looks better and carries load more convincingly. At the rear, run two shock springs in parallel so the bike rebounds with authority when compressed; that springiness is what makes the model read as an off-roader. On spring choice: soft springs suit static display, stiff springs suit handling. Good blueprints list recommended part numbers, and following them saves a lot of guesswork.

## 3. Handlebars and Rider: Pose Is Everything

Bar height sets the riding position. Off-road bars sit high, and the rider minifig should grip both bars with the torso pitched forward and both feet on the pegs — a standing riding position is what gives the model attitude. Knees slightly bent and hips off the seat makes the classic attack stance, and it photographs best. Jointed leg parts let you dial in the angle, and a ball joint at the waist helps rotate the torso. Finish with a motocross helmet or full-face helmet plus a pair of goggles and the detail level jumps immediately.

## 4. Chain and Drivetrain: Make the Rear Wheel Live

The chain is the detail that sells a motorcycle MOC. Blueprints usually connect the front and rear sprockets with tread link parts or small chain link parts. Watch the match between sprocket tooth count and chain length — too tight and it won't turn, too loose and it jumps off. Leave a gear interface so the rear wheel can be built as a rotating assembly: push the bike and the chain follows, which adds a lot of interactivity. The drivetrain is also the best test of a blueprint's quality — if you get stuck here, consider switching to a different set of instructions.

## 5. Stability and Stands: Stand Still Before It Runs

A motorcycle has two wheels, so a small shift in weight tips it over. Keep the center of gravity in the middle of the frame, with heavy elements like the engine and fuel tank close to the line between the front and rear wheels. If the front end is too heavy, widen the rear track or add counterweight at the front. For display, use a clear stand or a kickstand — it recreates a parked dirt bike and stops the minifig and loose parts from scattering on the floor.

## 6. Common Pitfalls

A few mistakes I've made myself: choosing the wrong tire size so the hub can't grip the tire; front fork springs too soft, so the nose collapses under any load; installing chain link parts backwards, which makes the drivetrain bind; and leaving the minifig's legs at an angle where the feet can't reach the pegs. Before downloading an off-road motorcycle MOC blueprint on BrickPlan, check the parts list against your inventory for the critical pieces, and keep a few spare springs and chain links on hand. Sort parts by the blueprint's numbered steps, too — dirt bikes are full of small pieces, and sorting easily halves your build time.

The real pleasure of an off-road motorcycle MOC is modification. Once you have the blueprint, swap the tires, retune the suspension, adjust the stance — the finished build usually has more character than the reference render. Use this guide to build one you're happy with in the BrickPlan community, and share your parts swaps in the comments.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose tires for an off-road motorcycle MOC, and will street wheels do?**
A: Go for wide-tread, deep-lug off-road tires, typically 56×36mm or 49.6×36mm in diameter, paired with a large spoked hub. Street wheels are narrower and the off-road look suffers.

**Q: What's the most common mistake when swapping in off-road hubs?**
A: A mismatch between the tire's inner diameter and the hub's outer diameter, which lets the tire slip loose. Check both dimensions before buying, and make sure the tire grips without spinning before you build further — don't force it.

**Q: My front fork springs are too soft and the nose collapses. How should I tune the suspension?**
A: Rake the forks slightly forward for stability — it mimics the steering angle and carries load better. At the rear, run two shock springs in parallel; use soft springs for static display and stiff ones if you handle the model often.

**Q: Is a working drivetrain worth adding so the rear wheel and chain turn?**
A: Yes. Connect the front and rear sprockets with tread link parts and leave a gear interface so the chain follows the wheel when you push it — the interactivity is worth the effort. Just match the sprocket tooth count to the chain length.
