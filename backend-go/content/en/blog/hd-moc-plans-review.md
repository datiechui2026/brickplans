# HD MOC Instructions Review: How to Spot Quality Plans

For MOC builders, good instructions are the foundation of a successful build. As the brick community grows, more and more creators publish plans for their models, but quality varies wildly: some files are razor-sharp at any zoom level, others are blurry enough that you can barely tell a 2x4 brick from a 2x4 plate. This review breaks down what actually makes a high-definition MOC plan, so you can pick the best file out of a crowded field.

## What makes a plan truly high definition?

A genuinely HD MOC plan is not just about a big resolution number. We assess plans across the following core dimensions.

### 1. Resolution and DPI

Resolution is the first checkpoint. Good MOC plans are usually exported at **300 DPI** or higher, so part edges stay crisp even when you zoom in. Low-resolution files (72 DPI or below) are tolerable on a phone or tablet, but the moment you enlarge them on a monitor or send them to a printer, you get visible aliasing and blur.

| Clarity tier | Resolution | Zoom behaviour | Recommended use |
|-----------|-------|---------|---------|
| Standard | 72 DPI | Blurry when zoomed, jagged edges | Quick reference only, not recommended |
| HD | 150-200 DPI | Details readable, zoom acceptable | Everyday building, basically sufficient |
| Ultra HD | 300 DPI+ | Sharp edges, clear details | Printed plans, collector-grade builds |

### 2. Clarity of the step breakdown

Sharp images are only half the job; step design matters just as much. A good MOC plan **highlights newly added parts in a distinct colour** in every step and marks the installation position with arrows or numbers. Each step should introduce no more than 3-5 new parts, so the builder is never overloaded with information. This is one of the clearest signals separating professional plans from amateur ones.

### 3. A complete parts list

A truly high-quality MOC plan always ships with a full **bill of materials (BOM)**. The list should include:

- Part numbers (ideally compatible with the LEGO numbering system)
- Part names (bilingual naming is a plus)
- Quantity per part
- Colour callouts
- A total part count

A plan without a parts list will cause real headaches during a build, no matter how high its resolution is.

## Common formats for HD MOC plans

There are three main formats in circulation today.

**PDF**: the most universal option. Vector graphics scale without loss and print cleanly. High-quality PDF plans also include bookmark navigation so you can jump straight to any step.

**Image sequences (PNG/JPG)**: a numbered pack of images, convenient for flipping through one by one on a phone or tablet. HD versions usually run at **1920x1080** or larger per image.

**Digital model files (IO/LDD/Studio)**: viewable interactively in software such as BrickLink Studio, with 360-degree rotation, part hiding and exploded views. This is the highest-spec form of plan, but it demands some software familiarity.

## Where to find the best HD MOC plans

After testing a large number of sources, we narrowed things down to a few reliable channels.

### Dedicated MOC plan platforms

Specialist brick plan communities such as **brickplan.cn** apply clear quality standards to uploaded files. The platform filters for adequate resolution, readable steps and complete parts lists, which saves builders a lot of screening time. The HD MOC plans you find there have usually been validated by the community already.

### International MOC communities

Rebrickable, BrickLink and similar international platforms collect work from top MOC designers worldwide. Popular plans there tend to carry high ratings, and you can read other builders' reviews and real photos of finished models before downloading, which gives you a good read on actual clarity.

### Designers' own channels

Many well-known MOC designers publish HD plans on their own websites or Patreon. Paid plans are often higher quality, with more detailed step notes and more professional layout work.

## How to judge a plan quickly before downloading

A few fast checks will tell you a lot:

1. **Check the file size**: an HD PDF of 20 or more pages is normally **10MB or larger**. If a file is only a few hundred KB, it is almost certainly a heavily compressed low-res version.
2. **Preview the thumbnails**: zoom into a small area of the preview image and check whether part edges are clean and text is sharp.
3. **Count the steps**: if a complex MOC comes with only a handful of step images, huge amounts of detail have been skipped, and it is not a real HD plan.
4. **Look for a parts list**: without one, a plan's professionalism drops off sharply.

## Testing the build experience

We built from several MOC plans from different sources and compared them directly.

**Test setup**: a Technic-style MOC with 3000+ parts, built from plans of differing clarity.

**Results**:

- HD plan (300 DPI): roughly 45 seconds per step on average, no need to re-check details, smooth building.
- Standard plan (72 DPI): roughly 90 seconds per step on average, constant zooming to confirm part positions, and an error rate 3x higher.
- Ultra HD plan plus digital model: shortest build time, with the ability to switch viewpoints at any moment to confirm structure, the best overall experience.

## Verdict and recommendations

Choosing an HD MOC plan is one of the simplest ways to improve your build. Our advice:

- Favour PDF plans at **300 DPI or above**
- Confirm the plan includes a full step breakdown and a parts list
- Use platforms with reviews and quality checks, such as brickplan.cn
- For complex models, consider paying for plans that bundle a digital model file

Good plans make the build more enjoyable, and they also help you follow the designer's thinking more closely, which feeds back into your own MOC design work. Use these checks and you will spend less time squinting at blurry files and more time building.
