# Buying Technic Parts for MOCs: Channels, Checks & Starter List

Most builders run into the same wall after finishing a few official Technic sets: the instructions end, but the ideas don't. That's when sourcing your own parts becomes the next skill to learn. Compared with standard system bricks, Technic inventory is dominated by gears, axles, beams and pins — a huge range in very fine increments, which makes shopping a different game from buying ordinary bricks. Here's how to buy with less waste, fewer returns and fewer surprises at the build table.

## Common Buying Channels and How to Choose

### Official Brand Stores and Major Marketplaces

If authentic parts and consistent quality matter most, start with a brand's own store or the official flagship shop on a large e-commerce platform. Colour matching is reliable, clutch is consistent and dimensions can be trusted — exactly what you want in drivetrains and gear trains, where small tolerances stack up across a long mechanism. The trade-off is price: buying part by part through official channels is the most expensive route, so most people reserve it for the critical pieces rather than bulk pins and axles.

### Chinese Brick Brands' Parts Shops

In recent years many Chinese brick brands have opened loose-part retail, and the value is strong enough that plenty of MOC builders now treat these shops as their main supplier. Two things are worth checking before you order. First, whether the shop prices by weight or by piece count — that changes how you should build your cart. Second, what buyers say in the reviews about tolerance and feel; comments about fit and gear mesh tell you far more than product photos do.

### Second-Hand Sets and Parts Recycling

On a tighter budget, look for used sets that are being broken up and sold as lots, or join a local builders' group where people trade and swap spare parts. Used parts are cheap, but confirm condition before paying. Load-bearing pieces are the ones to inspect closely — gears, cross axles, anything that transmits torque. Worn teeth or rounded axle ends will cost you efficiency in the finished mechanism, and no amount of clever building fixes a slipping gear.

## Key Buying Points to Remember

- **Specification first.** Technic sizes are keyed to the stud grid, and axles, pins and beams all have to correspond exactly. The wrong axle length or the wrong pin type usually means a part simply won't assemble, no matter how you angle it.
- **Colour and batch.** Colour can vary slightly between production batches. For visible bodywork — panels, beams and fairings — buy everything from one shop in one order so the finished surfaces match.
- **Buy spares.** Pins, axles and other small parts disappear into carpets and never come back. Order 10%–20% extra of the small hardware so one lost pin doesn't stall an otherwise finished build.
- **Quality of loaded parts.** Gears, worm gears and universal joints carry the load. Choose brands with a solid reputation for these specific parts, or you'll be dealing with slipping, binding and stripped teeth once the model is under torque.

## A Starter Parts List

For a first MOC, a basic inventory covers most structures: Technic beams in a range of lengths, cross axles with axle bushes, pins in black and grey, straight and bevel gears in the common sizes, a differential, universal joints, a worm gear housing, plus a small number of rubber tyres and panels. Get these general-purpose parts in stock first — they cover the majority of everyday mechanisms — and add specialised pieces only when a specific project calls for them. Storing pins, axles and gears in separate sorted bins also saves real time, since hunting for one 3L axle mid-build is the most common way a session grinds to a halt.

## Final Thoughts

Doing the homework on where and how to buy Technic parts pays off across every build that follows. Start with a small trial order, check the gears and pins yourself once they arrive, and only then scale up your stock.
