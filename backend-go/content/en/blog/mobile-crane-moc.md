# Brick Crane MOC Tutorial: Telescopic Boom and Winch Mechanism

Start with the short version: a crane MOC lives or dies on three things — the telescopic boom, the winch-and-ratchet assembly, and the counterweight. Aim for a winch reduction ratio of at least 1:16, and ballast the tail to roughly 1.5 times the rated load.

Construction-equipment MOCs are the most structurally interesting builds in the hobby, and cranes sit at the top: an extending main boom, a hook that pays out and hauls in, and a counterweight that pins the whole machine to the ground. This guide breaks the build into the four parts that matter — **telescopic boom, winch and ratchet, counterweight, chassis** — and covers the details that decide whether the finished model works or jams.

## Telescopic Boom: The Three-Section Nesting Trick

The boom is the crane's signature. The most practical approach is a **three-section nested structure**: take three square-tube beams of different widths (Technic frame beams in 3x3, 5x5 and 7x7 sizes work well), nest them thick-outside to thin-inside, and use **slider rail pieces** to constrain the free movement between sections so they can only travel fore and aft, with no side-to-side slop.

Two details decide whether this works. The first is **slider clearance**: too tight and the boom seizes, too loose and the sections sag. Leave roughly 0.5 mm of play between each stage, and test the slide before you lock anything down. The second is **extension drive**. The simplest reliable method is to run a single cross axle inside the boom: rotating the axle pushes the innermost section out, and the middle section is dragged along by limit pins. One axle therefore produces the linked sequence — inner section first, middle section following.

## Winch and Ratchet: Raising and Lowering the Hook

Hook travel comes from a **winch assembly**: mount a winding drum on the turntable, drive it through a gear reduction train, and run the line through the pulley block at the boom tip down to the hook. Use a **double pulley block** (one moving sheave, one fixed sheave) at the hook — this halves the line speed but doubles the pulling force, and it matches how a real crane is rigged.

The winch must have a **ratchet lock**. Fit a ratchet wheel and pawl on the drum axle so that once a load is lifted, the pawl engages the ratchet and stops the hook creeping down under its own weight. Beginners routinely skip this step and end up with a crane that drops the load the moment they let go — the whole point of the model disappears. Choose a spring-return pawl and build in a manual release lever so you can pay the hook out on demand.

## Counterweight and Chassis: Stability Comes First

A crane must never be top-heavy. Put the **counterweight** at the rear of the turntable and fill it with dark-coloured bricks or dedicated heavy ballast blocks. Target at least **1.5 times the load** the boom carries when fully extended. If you are short on ballast pieces, tuck a few ball bearings or metal washers into enclosed cavities.

For the **chassis**, tracked or wheeled depends on what you want from the model. A **tracked chassis** (track links, drive sprocket, idler wheel) gives a strong off-road, heavy-engineering look and suits static display or RC conversion. A **wheeled chassis** with a steering axle and outriggers is closer to a real mobile crane, and it lets you add **deployable outrigger structure** — build the four legs as extending units driven by a worm gear and rack, and the whole model jumps up a tier.

## Common Pitfalls and Where to Get Instructions

- **Line knots:** bury the hook knot inside the drum. Anything left on the outside will snag and tangle the line.
- **Gear reduction:** the winch needs a ratio of 1:16 or higher to produce usable torque. Direct drive will slip every time.
- **Boom sag:** add a diagonal tension member (a truss) at the midpoint of a long boom and the droop disappears.

Crane MOCs use a lot of structure and are sensitive to tolerances. A good route is to build a construction-equipment MOC instruction set from brickplan end to end first, paying close attention to how the designer lays out the boom sliders and the ratchet, and then modify boom length and chassis to suit yourself. Once the underlying mechanisms are familiar, the modifications come easily.

## FAQ

**Q: How do you build a crane's telescopic boom?**
A: Use a three-section nested structure: three square-tube beams of different widths (for example 3x3, 5x5 and 7x7) nested thick-outside to thin-inside, with slider rails constraining their movement. Leave about 0.5 mm of clearance per stage and test the slide before fixing anything.

**Q: The hook drops as soon as I lift something. What's wrong?**
A: You are missing the ratchet lock. Fit a ratchet wheel and pawl on the drum axle so the pawl catches the ratchet and stops the hook sliding back down. Use a spring-return pawl and add a manual release lever so you can lower the hook when you want to.

**Q: What reduction ratio does the winch need?**
A: At least 1:16 to generate enough torque — direct drive will slip. The hook should also use a double pulley block (moving sheave plus fixed sheave), which halves line speed but doubles pulling force and is closer to real crane rigging.

**Q: How should I handle the counterweight and chassis?**
A: Put the counterweight at the rear of the turntable and make it at least 1.5 times the load the boom carries at full extension; ball bearings work if you lack ballast pieces. Tracked chassis give the strongest engineering look, while a wheeled chassis can take worm-gear outriggers that noticeably raise the level of the build.
