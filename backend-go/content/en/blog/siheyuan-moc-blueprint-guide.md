# Brick Siheyuan MOC Guide: Build a Beijing Courtyard From Scratch

Chinese architecture is one of the hardest genres in brick building, and a siheyuan courtyard compound is the purest test of it. You cannot freestyle a Western street facade here and get away with it — the whole build lives or dies on a strict central axis and on timber-frame proportions. The compound is organised as a ritual sequence: gate, courtyard, hall. This walkthrough starts at the outermost daozuo range and works inward through the chuihua gate to the main hall, so you can rebuild a complete old-Beijing courtyard with its character intact.

## 1. Overall planning: fix the axis first, then the depth

Everything hangs off the central axis. Before you place a single wall, run a line of light-coloured plates down the length of the baseplate. That line is the shared axis for the daozuo rooms, the chuihua gate and the main hall; every wing room and covered walkway is mirrored off it, plate for plate. Build one side, then copy it exactly to the other — measure twice, because a two-stud drift at the gate end becomes a visible kink by the time you reach the main hall.

For a first Chinese-style MOC, use a single-entry layout (one courtyard): daozuo rooms to the south, main hall to the north, east and west wing rooms facing each other, chuihua gate in the middle. The hierarchy stays legible and the part count stays manageable. Keep the courtyard itself open — that void is the light well and the breathing space of the whole compound, and it is what makes the surrounding roofs read as a courtyard rather than a block of buildings.

## 2. Daozuo rooms and perimeter wall: a steady base

The daozuo range sits at the street edge and faces north into the compound — the first stage of the sequence. Go grey-brick and dark-tile here: alternate dark grey and blue-grey plates to fake fair-faced brickwork, roof the range in dark grey curved tiles, and close the ridge with two small inverted triangle pieces so the roof ends in a clean flush gable. The daozuo is not meant to be ornate. Straight walls and crisp eaves are the point, because they set up the contrast that the chuihua gate will deliver later. On the perimeter wall, add an inward-stepped skirting course one plate back from the face; the shadow line alone changes the whole read of the wall.

## 3. Chuihua gate and veranda: the visual climax

The chuihua ("hanging flower") gate is the showpiece of any siheyuan MOC and the structural headache of the build. Two front eave columns carry it; from the top of each, two short bays cantilever forward. Hang brown small pieces upside down to form carved capitals, then add two pendant pieces below each — that hanging detail is where the gate gets its name. Build the bracket sets from two stacked layers of grey-white small pieces, use dark red plates for the door leaves, and run gold trim pieces around the frame to lift it out of the grey.

Covered walkways join the gate on both sides: columns with projecting eaves, one column every two studs, so the rhythm stays consistent with the gate front. A few minifigures under the veranda with tea cups costs almost nothing and makes the courtyard instantly inhabited.

## 4. Main hall, wing rooms and courtyard details

The main hall faces south and anchors the compound. Raise it one step above the courtyard to mark its rank, then furnish it: a square dining table, high-backed armchairs and a long console table, with a vase and candlesticks on top. East and west wing rooms sit half a level lower, so the primary-secondary height difference reads at a glance from across the yard.

Lay the courtyard floor in stone-slab colours, plant a pine or pomegranate tree in one corner with a water vat beneath it, stick red spring-couplet pieces over the door lintel, and add two small ridge beasts to the main hall roof. These are the details that separate a siheyuan from a generic street build — cheap in parts, decisive in effect.

## 5. Beginner tips and parts list

Practise the chuihua gate as a standalone module first. Once bracket stacking and the hanging pendants feel natural in your fingers, move on to the full compound; trying to learn the technique in the middle of a finished courtyard is where most first attempts stall.

Parts list:

- Large quantities of dark grey and blue-grey plates plus dark grey curved tiles for walls and roofs
- Brown and dark red pieces for beams, columns and door leaves
- Gold trim pieces for accents around the gate and door frames
- Assorted transparent pieces for window lattice and the paper-window effect
- Pine tree, pomegranate tree, planters and a water vat as Chinese decorative elements

Sort into three boxes — blue-grey, brown, red — before you start, and the build runs far more smoothly than digging through one mixed pile.

From the daozuo rooms through the chuihua gate to the main hall and its wings, hold the axis and keep the grey-tile, red-door palette consistent, and the compound will carry the quiet, deliberate feel of old Beijing.
