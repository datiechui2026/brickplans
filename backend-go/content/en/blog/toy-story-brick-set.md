# Toy Story Brick Sets: Woody, Buzz and Andy's Room Reviewed

"To infinity and beyond!" The moment that line lands, a whole generation is back on the bedroom floor with their toys. The Toy Story films have grown up alongside several generations of kids, and Toy Story brick sets are the most direct way to turn that nostalgia into something you build with your own hands. This review looks at the line from three angles: how faithful the models are, how much there is to play with, and how much fun the build itself is.

## Woody and Buzz Minifigures: Where Accuracy Lives or Dies

Minifigures are always the soul of a licensed brick set, and Toy Story sets are no exception. Woody's signature elements are the brown cowboy hat, the red-and-black checked shirt and the yellow polka-dot bandana, and the better sets reproduce those details precisely across two small bricks. The most important part is the face print — those round, expressive eyes and the trademark smile. Whether the print captures the character is what decides if the figure reads as "him" or just as a guy in a hat.

Buzz Lightyear is harder to get right. The purple visor on the green space suit, the chest energy light and the deployable white wings all call for specially moulded parts, and a version whose wings actually fold in and out is worth a lot. Beyond the two leads, Jessie, Rex, Mr. Potato Head and other supporting characters turn up regularly in these sets. Collecting the whole toy box in one room is the long-game pleasure for dedicated builders.

## Andy's Room: A Childhood Shrunk to Brick Scale

If the minifigures are the soul, the scene is the stage. Andy's room is the most recognisable setting in the Toy Story brick line — the blue-and-white bedspread with its star pattern, the wallpapered walls, the toy chest under the bed. Once it is built, it feels like stepping back into that small world that belongs to the toys.

More advanced sets add extras like the nebula lamp by Andy's bed and loose bricks scattered on the floor, which fill the scene out. Other sets recreate famous film moments such as Pizza Planet or the solar tower, sometimes paired with a rotating base or light-up elements so the display effect comes close to a theatre diorama. Modular scene designs can also be combined freely — put Andy's room next to Pizza Planet and stage your own Toy Story plot.

## Play Value: More Than a Display Piece

Play value is where Toy Story sets stand out among licensed brick themes. First, character interaction is rich: lots of figures, lots of accessories, and Woody's lasso or Buzz's helmet can be removed and handled. Second, the scene mechanisms are clever — the toy chest lid opens and closes, the bed lifts to hide minifigures underneath, echoing the films' idea of toys sneaking around when no one is watching. Third, the build itself is enjoyable: fitting Buzz's wings panel by panel, or stacking Woody's cowboy boots in two layers, gives you the odd, delightful feeling of making a toy for a toy.

For families, these sets hit a friendly middle ground — a moderate part count and an approachable difficulty make them a good candidate for a child's first themed brick build.

## Buying Advice: Start With Accuracy and Play Value

When choosing a Toy Story brick set, first confirm the minifigure lineup — ideally you get both leads. Then check whether the scene matches the part of the film you love most. Finally, look at the part count and how clear the instructions are. Beginners should start with a small-to-mid-size set like Andy's room; experienced builders can take on a flagship set with a large cast of figures and a big scene. Whichever you pick, once it is finished and sitting in the display cabinet, that feeling of "the toys really are alive" comes straight back.
