# How to Build a School LEGO MOC: Layout, Scale and Colors

## Plan first: how much baseplate does your school need?

A school is one of the most lived-in themes you can build, and also the easiest to ruin — most first attempts end up as one lonely building sitting on an empty plate. Before you pick up a single brick, sketch a rough campus map on the baseplate: where the teaching block goes, where the sports field goes, which way the main gate faces. On a common 48x48 stud baseplate, put the classroom building on one side, the field and running track on the other, and leave a main walkway down the middle. The layout reads instantly. Step one of this tutorial is not stacking bricks, it is drawing a plan — once the plan is fixed, nothing later goes wrong.

## Zone by zone: playground, classroom, library

**The playground** is the soul of the school and the part that looks best. Build the running track from 1x1 and 1x2 plates in alternating light gray and dark gray to mark out four lanes, lay green plates in the middle as grass, then ring the center circle and penalty area with white round plates or small white elements. To bring it to life, add a pull-up bar, a long-jump sand pit, or even a small swimming pool built from trans-blue parts.

**The teaching building** works best as a standard two- or three-storey slab block: line up window glass elements on each floor, make the entrance hall double-height, and top it with a sign (a few small bricks spelling letters, or a printed sticker, is plenty).

**Inside the classroom**, use an SNOT technique to hang a blackboard on the wall, set out a few small desks and chairs, and stand a teacher minifigure behind the lectern — the atmosphere arrives immediately.

**The library** gets two rows of bookcases built from dark brown shelf plates, with a long table in the middle where minifigures sit and read.

## Minifig scale: make every building line up

When a beginner's school build looks off, the problem is almost always scale. A standard minifigure is about 4 bricks tall, so a classroom ceiling needs to be at least 6 bricks high to avoid feeling cramped, and doorways need a full 5 bricks of clearance so figures can walk in. Props follow the same ruler: desks sit at 2 bricks tall, a basketball rim hangs about 8 bricks above the ground. Stand one minifigure next to your work as a measuring stick and check every prop against it, and you will never end up with a figure scraping its head on the ceiling.

## Generic parts and a color scheme that saves money

The big advantage of a school scene is that it needs **almost no special pieces** — it is all generic bricks. For color, use a reliable formula: walls in cream or light yellow, roofs in brick red or light gray, windows and doors in dark blue or dark green, paving in light gray and dark gray. These are the most common and cheapest parts in bulk lots — around a kilogram of assorted bricks will comfortably cover a whole campus. Green plates for the field and transparent window elements cost a little more, so batch those into one order. If you want to save effort, many city-series sets already ship in school colors and make a cost-effective parts donor.

## Building with kids and in the classroom

A school theme is a natural fit for parents building alongside children and for classroom lessons. With kids, hand over the design decisions — the layout, the color scheme — and let the adult handle the tricky parts: interlocking the walls and building the roof. That way the child exercises spatial thinking without getting discouraged by difficulty. In a kindergarten or primary school brick class, a finished school scene is a ready-made role-play set: once it is built, the children can act out lessons and break time with minifigures. Displaying the finished model on a desk or in a classroom cabinet also builds a real sense of achievement.

## Four things to remember

Building a school MOC comes down to four moves: draw the campus plan first, then work zone by zone through playground, teaching block and library; keep a minifigure beside you as the scale ruler throughout; and control cost with generic parts in a consistent palette. Follow that order and even a complete beginner can put together a convincing mini campus in a weekend.
