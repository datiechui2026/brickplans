# Brick Skyscraper MOC: Structure and Curtain Wall Tips

Skyscrapers are always the star of a brick city skyline, and also where builds fail hardest. A low house can be stacked carelessly and still stand; once the height climbs, the problems show up: the tower sways, the middle bulges outward, and the curtain wall stops lining up halfway up. This guide splits the two core problems — structural rigidity and facade assembly — and covers both from experience.

## Structural Stability: Build the Core First, Then the Shell

The most common skyscraper MOC mistake is building from the outside in — closing the outer wall ring first and filling the interior later. The taller it gets, the more it wobbles. The right order is to build the core tube first: stack 4x4 or 6x6 columns vertically up the exact center of the building, all the way to the top. This is the equivalent of a real tower's elevator shaft, and it is the skeleton of the whole structure. Every 4 to 6 floors of core, tie it into the outer wall once with plates and horizontal beams — effectively adding a floor slab — and the tower will stay steady even at 50 floors. The core must be locked vertically with studded bricks, not stacked from smooth tiles, or it will come apart at the first bump.

## Inter-Floor Reinforcement: Avoid the Soft Middle

On a brick skyscraper MOC, the middle section is where things go wrong, because the loads there differ from the base. Plenty of builders cut corners mid-height and end up with a tower bent like a banana. Two practical fixes: first, add an inner ring beam every 10 floors, using 1x2 bricks with holes at the four corners as vertical ties — think of it as a belt around the building. Second, stagger corner bricks so the joints break above and below; never let the four corner lines stack into one continuous vertical seam. A running seam is the cardinal sin of tower structure. If the building is especially tall, run a long technic beam up through the inside of the core as a steel frame and the strength goes straight to maximum.

## Curtain Wall Assembly: The Secret Is the Gaps

The curtain wall is the face of the skyscraper MOC and the most fiddly part. The key to glass curtain wall assembly is not the glass but the window frames and the gaps: start with dark gray plates as the vertical mullion frame, spacing each bay so a transparent 1x2 glass brick drops in exactly, and keep that spacing identical the whole way up — otherwise it drifts further out of line with every floor. One trick: lay out a frame template on the baseplate ahead of time, then align each level against it to prevent floor-to-floor misalignment. For a mirrored look, back the glass bricks with a bright silver plate and the reflection appears immediately.

## Rooftop Finish: Give the Skyline a Landmark

With the body rigid and the curtain wall lined up, the last thing is the roof. A skyscraper's crown is the memory point of the whole building, so don't close it off with a flat top. Build a stepped setback crown: pull each level in by 2 studs, diminishing level by level, then add an antenna mast or a helipad. Stack the spire from 1x1 round bars and mount a beacon made from a transparent round stud on top. An aviation obstruction light on the roof is standard on real towers, and adding one makes the whole building read as real.

## Pitfall Checklist

A few high-frequency traps to finish: first, too few tie-in floors between the outer wall and the core, so the taller it gets the more it sways; second, corner running seams left unhandled, which show as cracks from the side; third, window frame spacing changed partway through, so the wall leans further out of true the higher it goes. Build in the order of core first, mid-height ring beams second, curtain wall last, and it will hold.
