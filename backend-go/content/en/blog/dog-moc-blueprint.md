# How to Choose a Brick Dog MOC Plan: Shiba to Corgi

Dogs may be the single best animal MOC subject for a builder starting out: the body is small, the structure is fairly forgiving, and you can pose the result in all sorts of appealing ways. The catch is that everyone has seen a real dog up close, so "does it look like one" gets judged instantly. This guide covers how to pick a brick dog MOC plan and how to build it, so you skip the usual dead ends and end up with something worth a spot in the display case.

## Pick the Breed Before the Plan

The most common mistake is choosing a plan first and only then deciding which dog to build. Breed structure differs a lot. For corgis and dachshunds — long body, short legs — you capture the character by raising the torso and widening the body. For shibas and akitas — curled tail, upright ears — the work sits in the face and the tail. Large breeds such as golden retrievers and Labradors mean handling thick limbs and heavy drooping ears. Lock in the breed you want first, then shop plans for that breed, and the likeness improves noticeably. It is very common for one designer to publish several versions of the same breed; favor the version that ships with a complete parts list and step-by-step images.

## Pose Decides Success

In dog MOC plans, the pose is what makes a model read as alive rather than as a sculpture of a dog. Three poses come up most often. Sitting: the weight rests on the hind legs and front paws, with the chest built up from large bricks — the most classic option and the easiest to get looking right. Lying down: the whole torso drops low and the front legs stretch forward, ideal for that hopeful "waiting for a treat" expression. Standing: four legs vertical, which puts the highest demand on the precision of the joint parts and works best for showing off the side profile. Beginners should start with sitting — fewer parts, a stable stance, and a solid feel for the build before taking on more complex dynamic poses.

## Posable Details That Add Value

If you want the finished model to invite interaction, look for three articulation points in the plan. First, the head-to-body connection: a ball joint or hinge part lets the dog lower its head or tilt it to one side. Second, the tail: a corgi's stubby tail is simply plates laid flat against the body, while a shiba's curl is built from several curved pieces stacked into an upward sweep. Third, the ears: on floppy-eared breeds, adding a hinge at the base of the ear lets you pose the moment when the dog pricks its ears up at a sound. These touches usually cost only ten-odd extra parts but lift both the expressiveness and the playability of the build considerably.

## Color and Detail Accuracy

Color is the other half of likeness. Take a shiba: the standard coloring is a red-brown back against a cream belly, and the transition zone can use small dark brown pieces in an irregular mosaic to imitate a fur gradient — far more natural than solid color blocks. Eyes are a small round plate with a transparent piece for the highlight, the nose is a black dome piece, and the tongue is a red 1x1 plate poking out through the gap of the mouth. Add those small details together and the dog suddenly has personality. Do not forget the paws — dark small pieces for the pads and a few light stripes across the front feet, so the model holds up when someone looks closely.

## Where to Find Plans and Parts

Sources for brick dog MOC plans are plentiful today: official builds, player forums and plan-sharing sites all offer plenty of options. When you choose a plan, check the compatibility of its parts list and confirm you actually have the parts on hand before you start. The BrickPlans (brickplan.cn) community gathers a good number of dog plans alongside real player photos — shiba, corgi and husky builds all come with their own configurations and parts lists, so if you want a brick version of your own dog, a search there will usually turn up inspiration. And once the build is done, photograph it: your real dog and the brick dog in the same frame is the best finished-product shot you can get.
