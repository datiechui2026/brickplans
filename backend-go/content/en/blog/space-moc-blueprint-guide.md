# Space MOC Blueprint Guide: Ships, Stations and Starships

Space is the most romantic theme in brick building, and the one that tests design sense the hardest: you carry none of the scale baggage of ground-based models, yet color, structure and detail still have to sell the sci-fi feel. This guide walks through scale planning, color, structure and a realistic starting point for newcomers, covering spaceships, space stations and starships as three separate design problems.

## 1. Decide Scale and Visual Focus First

The biggest advantage of space MOCs is freedom: you are not locked to the 1:48 minifigure scale used for city scenes, and nothing ties you to the ground. But freedom is not the same as winging it. Settle three things before you pick up a brick.

First, the **viewing angle**. Spaceships are usually displayed side-on, space stations work best spanning horizontally or mounted on a rotating stand, and starships live on vertical mass and a looming sense of pressure.

Second, the **hero part**. Choose one element that grabs the eye as the visual center — the ship's cockpit canopy, the station's rotating ring, the starship's oversized engine — and build everything else around it.

Third, **consistent scale**. Cockpits, hatches and antennas all need to read correctly against a standard minifigure, otherwise the model looks like a pile of bricks rather than a vehicle. Most beginner space MOCs go wrong precisely here: building starts before these three decisions are made.

## 2. Color: Cool Tones Hold It Up, Accents Sell It

The premium look of a space build comes from restraint. Start from **white, light gray and dark gray** to mimic spacecraft hulls, then add **blue, purple or fluorescent green** as accents that suggest energy, lasers and alien technology. Transparent parts are close to essential: trans-blue for thruster flames, trans-orange for engine nozzles, trans plates for portholes — and suddenly the model reads as in flight.

The rule that matters most is that fewer colors look better. Keep the whole ship to three or four colors, and use fluorescent parts only in small areas, or the build starts to look cheap.

## 3. Structure and Modularity: More Effect for Fewer Parts

Large spacecraft have big hulls and lots of detail, and stacking bricks the whole way through gets expensive fast. Use a **modular** approach instead: split the hull into a nose section, cockpit, wings and tail, build them separately, then join them with technic pins and axles. That buys you three things.

- **Strength.** Pin connections survive knocks better than plain stacked bricks.
- **Transport.** Modules come apart, so moving a model to a show is painless.
- **Reuse.** Today's ship becomes tomorrow's starship when you pull the wings off and swap in a new set.

Inside the thrusters, build a hollow skeleton and skin it with panels afterward. It saves parts, leaves room to route wiring, and makes adding lights far easier later.

## 4. Ships, Stations and Starships: Three Different Games

**Spaceships** are small in volume but demand real care, and the key is a streamlined silhouette. Transition the hull with slope bricks and curved pieces, give the wings a swept-back or folding shape, cap the nozzles with transparent parts — at 10 to 15 cm long a ship can still look refined.

**Space stations** emphasize a sense of unfolding. Put a core module at the center and radiate outward with solar panels, docking bays and laboratory modules. Lean on thin rod pieces and hinges so the whole thing looks like it is floating in orbit.

**Starships** are the big-engineering option. Chase vertical mass, stacking up bridge decks and hangars layer by layer, then dress the flanks with engine arrays and turrets. On a bookshelf they dominate the room.

Whichever type you build, leave a few **story points** on the hull: a small cargo hold, a hatch that actually opens, a minifigure standing by a porthole. That is what makes a model feel alive.

## 5. How Beginners Should Start

For a first attempt at a space MOC, build a small ship and keep the part count under two hundred pieces. Borrow parts from a retail set to test proportions, or lay the design out in brick software first and confirm the structure makes sense before you build. On a tight budget, spend on the three categories that show results — transparent parts, printed parts and curved pieces — and fill in basic bricks with compatible third-party parts.

When it is finished, don't tear it down immediately. Leave it on the desk for a few days and look at it: where are the proportions off, where is detail missing? Then iterate on those specific spots. Space MOCs get polished one version at a time.

## Closing Notes

Space gives brick building its widest imagination space and newcomers their lowest entry barrier: there is no real building to reproduce faithfully. Coordinated proportions, restrained color and solid structure are enough to produce a striking starship.
