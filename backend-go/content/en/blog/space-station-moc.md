# Space Station MOC: Plans, Modular Docking and Build Steps

Short answer first: when you pick a space station MOC plan, check three things — whether the module breakdown makes sense, whether the docking standard is consistent, and whether it supports free expansion. Start at a desktop scale of roughly 1:110, and make sure every segment shares the same interface spec so you can add modules later.

Among space-themed brick MOCs, a space station plan is one of the best value and longest-lasting picks. It needs no giant launch tower, yet it pushes modular building to the limit: segments dock in orbit and recombine just like the real thing. That makes it fun to build and a great showpiece. This guide walks you from choosing a plan to assembling the skeleton and docking structure.

## 1. Choosing a Plan: Module Design and Expandability

The core of plan selection comes down to three points: **whether the modules are split sensibly, whether the docking standard is uniform, and whether free expansion is supported**. A good plan breaks the station into independent modules — core module, lab modules, solar wings and docking nodes — with all segments sharing one interface spec. That way you can build to the plan and still add your own modules. Next, look at **scale and detail**: a desktop scale of about 1:110 works best, giving plenty of detail without eating up space. Finally, confirm the plan includes step-by-step diagrams and a parts list. Most space station plans on BrickPlan list module count and total piece count, which makes comparison easy.

## 2. Modular Structure: Segments and Docking Explained

The heart of a space station MOC is **modular docking**. The common approach: build each segment as a cylinder, put **standard circular connector pieces** on both ends, and join segments with **axle parts or flange plates** so they click together in one push. The core module keeps docking ports on several sides (front, back, left, right, top, bottom), lab modules hang off the outside, and **truss pieces** stretch the solar wings out to both ends. This structure lets you rebuild the reference model and reconfigure it at will, matching how real stations work. Remember one rule: **unified interfaces, unlimited expansion**.

## 3. Parts List and Build Steps

For parts, base the hull on **white curved and cylindrical plates**, use dark grey plates for the segment rings, and apply **gold foil pieces** to the solar wings. Structural parts include **circular connectors, axle parts, truss pieces and hinge pieces**; detail parts include **porthole pieces, antenna pieces and small lab equipment pieces**. Recommended build order: **core module → lab modules → solar wings → docking assembly**. Build each segment separately first and check its tightness and rigidity on its own, then lay them out on the baseplate in a T shape or a straight line over unified docking points, and finish by unfolding the solar wings.

## 4. Building Tips: Rigidity, Wiring and Display

A space station is a long, thin structure, and the weakest points are always **the segment interfaces and the solar wing roots**. At each interface, use **double locking** (axle plus flange connected together); at the wing roots, run an extended axle through and lock it down so the panels do not sag. If you add lights, use **warm white pieces for porthole glow and blue-purple lights for interior ambience**, and hide the wiring along the inside of the truss. For display, mount it on a dark baseplate and add scattered star pieces or a projected starfield — the station instantly looks like it is running in orbit.

## 5. Pitfalls to Avoid

A few common traps: **do not go too big** — practice on a single segment first, since jumping straight into a ten-plus-segment station often ends in an unfinished build. **Buy plenty of connector pieces**; they are the most critical shared part in the whole model, and running short means stripping one section to finish another. **Do not make the solar wings too wide** — extend past the baseplate's center of gravity and the model tips over, so add a weighted base if needed. **Run lighting from a single power supply** where possible to avoid a clutter of battery boxes. Get these right and the model stays stable and photographs well.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose a space station MOC plan?**
A: Check three things: whether the module breakdown is sensible, whether the docking standard is uniform, and whether free expansion is supported. A good plan splits the core module, lab modules, solar wings and docking nodes into independent modules with matching interface specs.

**Q: What scale should a space station MOC use?**
A: A desktop scale of about 1:110 is best — enough detail without taking up much room. Plans usually list module count and total piece count for side-by-side comparison. Confirm it includes step diagrams and a complete parts list before buying.

**Q: How do segments dock freely with each other?**
A: Through unified docking node pieces and a standard cross-axle interface. When every segment shares the same spec, you can build to the plan or add your own modules. Start with the core module to set the reference, then extend lab modules outward from both ends.

**Q: Roughly how many pieces does a space station MOC need?**
A: A mid-size desktop version runs about 1,500-3,000 pieces and takes 6-10 hours following the plan. Large versions with deployable panels and interior compartments often exceed 4,000 pieces, so build them segment by segment in batches.
