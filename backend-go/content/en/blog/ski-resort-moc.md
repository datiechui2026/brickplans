# How to Build a LEGO Ski Resort MOC: Slopes, Gondola, Chalet

Start with the short version: a ski resort MOC lives or dies on three things — the gradient, the snow surface, and the elevated cable car. Keep the main run at 20 to 30 degrees, and set your two pulley brackets symmetrically before you tension the cable.

A brick ski resort is one of the most rewarding winter builds you can put on a table: white snowfields, a gondola that keeps circulating, and a small timber lodge glowing warm against the cold. It is also the scene where a lot of builders stall halfway, stuck on "how do I hold this slope up" or "why won't my cable car loop." This guide walks through layout planning, snow slope structure, the lift mechanism, and the chalet details in one pass.

## Layout Planning and Baseplate Choice

Decide on footprint and zones before you open a single bag. A typical ski resort diorama starts at a 32x32 stud baseplate; more ambitious builders go to 48x48 or combine several baseplates. Split the scene into three depth layers: the back is the main peak and the ski runs, the middle holds the lift station and the lodge, and the foreground is left for small features — a parking area, a snowman, slalom gates.

Remember that a snow slope eats a lot of height. Use thick baseplates or a double-layer baseplate so the build does not become top-heavy as the slope rises. Always check the finished dimensions printed on the instructions first — a beautiful ski resort MOC that overhangs your desk budget still will not fit.

## Slope Gradient and Snow Surface

Ski runs are the skeleton of the scene, and the gradient you choose drives the whole look. Beginners should aim for a gentle 20 to 30 degree pitch, built up layer by layer on stacked large plates. If you want a steep expert run, use SNOT side-building to create a near-vertical face, then clad the surface in white slope bricks.

For surface texture, lay down large white plates as the base, then scatter 1x1 round plates or studs across it to suggest granular snow, and dot a few trans-light-blue pieces in for icy glints. Edge the run with white curved pieces rather than straight ends — the rounded overhang reads far more like a plowed snow bank. At the bottom of the slope, add a ring of translucent elements to mimic packed ice where the run meets the lift station.

## The Lift Mechanism: The Scene's Moving Core

A gondola that actually circulates is the part people remember. There are two common approaches. The first is a track system: build a closed loop from technic track or chain links, hang the cars on it, and drive them around with gears. The second is a cable system: a thin hose or cord stands in for the steel cable, pulleys sit at each end, and you drive it with a hand crank or a motor.

Either way, confirm on paper that the two pulley brackets sit symmetrically, and that the cable tension is moderate — too tight and nothing turns, too loose and the chairs sag. Build the chairs from 2x2 plates with handle pieces on each side, and hang six to eight of them at even intervals so the motion has a rhythm. Make the towers as truss structures from dark gray technic parts, anchored to the baseplate with cross-axle pieces, and keep the spacing consistent.

## The Chalet and Scene Details

The lodge is the warm focal point of the resort. Build the body in log-tan bricks for a timber feel, cap it with a dual-pitch roof in red or dark brown slope bricks, and don't skip the chimney and the snow on the roof — white plates laid at an angle along the ridge instantly give that just-snowed look. Inside, keep it simple: a fireplace, a bench, and a ski rack. Use transparent windows with warm yellow light bricks and the whole cabin glows after dark.

Around the lodge, add the supporting cast: a snowman (white round base, orange cone nose), minifigure skiers with skis and poles, a lift station platform, signposts and slalom gates. Finish by sprinkling white round plates along the baseplate edges to bury the seams — the scene reads as finished almost immediately.

## Lighting and Display Tips

A ski resort is one of the rare builds that looks better with the lights on. Hide warm LED strips inside the chalet, at the lift station, and along the run; use cool white strips on the shadowed side of the mountain to stand in for moonlight. Once that contrast appears, the depth of the snow slopes doubles.

On parts: white plates dominate the bill, so buy in bulk against the parts list in the instructions. A medium-sized ski resort MOC runs roughly ¥300 to ¥800 in parts.

Slope, lift, and lodge — get those three right and you have a brick ski resort that actually moves. ## FAQ

**Q: What gradient should the ski slope be?**
A: Beginners should build a gentle 20 to 30 degree pitch, stacked layer by layer on raised large plates. For an expert run, use SNOT side-building for a vertical face and clad it in white slope bricks; add a ring of translucent pieces at the bottom to mimic ice where the run meets the lift station.

**Q: How do I make the cable car loop?**
A: Two ways. A track system uses technic track or chain links in a closed loop with cars driven by gears. A cable system uses thin hose or cord with pulleys at both ends, driven by a hand crank or motor. Keep the pulley brackets symmetrical.

**Q: How do I get the just-snowed look on the chalet?**
A: Build the body in log-tan bricks, roof it with red or dark brown slope bricks in a dual pitch, then add a chimney and roof snow — white plates angled along the ridge do the job. Inside, add a fireplace, bench and ski rack, and use warm yellow light bricks behind the windows.

**Q: What is the best lighting setup?**
A: This is one of the few scenes that looks better lit than in daylight. Hide warm strips in the chalet, at the lift station and along the run, and use cool white strips on the mountain's shadow side for moonlight. That contrast doubles the depth of the slopes. A medium-sized build costs about ¥300 to ¥800.
