# How to Choose an Iron Man MOC Blueprint: Armor, Joints, Colors

Iron Man is probably the most popular subject in Marvel brick MOC building, and the quality of the Iron Man MOC blueprints on the market is wildly uneven. Some builds read instantly as one of Tony Stark's suits; others are just a generic robot wearing a red-and-gold shell. For this review I built three blueprints at three different difficulty levels, working through the armor silhouette, the chest arc reactor, and the articulation in the shoulders and legs to pin down what actually separates a blueprint worth buying from one worth skipping.

## Armor Shape and the Red-Gold Color Scheme

The soul of an Iron Man build is that red-and-gold palette. A good Iron Man MOC blueprint keeps the gold armor pieces consistent, using bright gold or pearl gold throughout, and keeps the red a true red with no orange shift. Put those together and the model reads as metal. Color accuracy is really a test of how carefully the blueprint labels its color separation: the parts list should state exactly which sections use gold, which use red, and which use dark red. Do that clearly and the finished model looks like the original suit. Some blueprints chase a lower part count and swap large red areas for generic bricks, and the finished model immediately drops a tier in appearance.

## Helmet Faceplate and the Chest Arc Reactor

The helmet and the chest arc reactor are the two biggest factors in how faithful the model looks. The helmet faceplate has to capture Iron Man's signature curved silhouette, and the eyes are usually done with transparent parts plus stickers to suggest the glowing effect. On the chest, a good blueprint uses a round transparent piece as the core, surrounds it with curved slopes to build the inverted-triangle outline, and once you drop a light brick in behind it the effect lands immediately. A blueprint that gets these two details right will still hold its own on a display shelf even if the joints are only average.

## Shoulder, Arm and Leg Joints

Articulation is what decides whether your Iron Man can hit the signature poses: flight, palm repulsor blasts, and the rest. The shoulder armor should be able to flip upward rather than blocking the arm when you raise it. Use ball joints at the elbows and knees, and give the legs a double bend, and you can get the kneeling blast pose. Watch that the armor plates on the arms and legs are not wrapped too tightly, or pieces will pop off the moment you move anything. The mid-level blueprint I built used swappable hand pieces, letting you switch freely between a clenched fist, an open palm and a repulsor blast hand. Three hand shapes made it far more fun to handle.

## Blueprint Difficulty Tiers and Part Counts

Iron Man MOC blueprints roughly fall into three tiers. Entry level sits around one hundred parts, with a boxy structure and simple colors, and suits anyone building a humanoid figure for the first time. Intermediate runs from two hundred to four hundred parts, adds folding shoulder armor and double-jointed legs, and the instructions are typically split into forty or fifty steps. Expert starts at five hundred parts and up, with layer after layer of armor over the whole body, and it uses a fair number of special parts: curved slopes, ball joints, transparent pieces. Before you pick a blueprint, check the parts list and the share of special parts. If special parts dominate, just buy a full parts pack rather than trying to substitute your way through your own collection.

## Final Recommendations

Overall, choose your Iron Man MOC blueprint by fixing your budget and difficulty level first. Beginners should start with an entry-level blueprint for practice. Experienced builders who care about shape accuracy and poseability should go straight to an intermediate model, and focus on three things: the helmet, the reactor, and the red-gold color separation.
