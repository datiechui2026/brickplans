# Free MOC Instructions: Legit Sources and What to Avoid

Most builders looking for their first MOC start in the same place: hunting for instructions. A good blueprint keeps you from buying the wrong parts and from misreading a step forty pages in, which is exactly why "free MOC instructions" is one of the most-searched phrases among newcomers. This guide covers where you can legally get good ones for free, and what to check before you commit an evening to a download.

## 1. Three legitimate channels for free MOC instructions

Choose the source before you choose the model. These three categories are widely recognized as legitimate:

| Channel | Typical examples | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Official open release | Rebrickable, the free instruction sections of the LEGO website | Builders who want clean files and complete parts lists |
| Community creation | Brick forums, Bilibili creators sharing their builds | Builders who like to follow along and compare notes |
| Author-approved distribution | Designer homepages, author newsletters, design groups | Builders who want a specific designer's work and want to support them |

What all three have in common is that the instructions were released free by the author or the official source, on purpose. The copyright situation is clear and you can download without second-guessing yourself. Compared with files scraped from unknown sites, blueprints from these channels are far more reliable in quality.

## 2. How to judge whether a free blueprint is worth downloading

Once you have a free MOC instruction file in hand, don't start sorting parts yet. Check three things first.

**The parts list.** A blueprint without a complete parts list is a blueprint you'll be building halfway before you discover something is missing. Check that quantities are listed and that the part names or numbers are specific enough to actually order against.

**The step images.** Low-resolution screenshots are the classic beginner trap: zoom in and you still can't tell which direction a piece connects. Look at two or three pages at full size before you download the whole thing. If the connection points are ambiguous at normal zoom, the file will cost you more time than it saves.

**The author's notes.** Scale, difficulty level, and which brands the required parts come from are all information you need before buying anything. A designer who explains that a model is advanced, or that certain parts are only available from specific manufacturers, has saved you a wasted order.

A blueprint that passes all three checks is worth the hours you'll spend building it. One that fails any of them usually isn't — no matter how good the finished photo looks.

## 3. Three traps to avoid when downloading for free

**Sites that are "free" until you try to open the file.** Watch for pages that advertise free MOC instructions but block the actual download behind registration plus a payment. A legitimate free blueprint page does not ask you to pay before you can unlock the file.

**Archives from unknown sources.** Stay away from zip and rar files with no clear origin, and never unpack them on a shared or public computer. Bundled malware is a real risk in file-sharing communities, and a brick model is not worth an infected machine.

**Scanned PDFs of official set instructions.** These are widely circulated and just as widely misunderstood. Reproducing and distributing official set manuals infringes copyright, makes it hard for the original creators to defend their work, and eats into the space available to independent designers. If you want instructions for a set that's still on sale, buy the set or use the manufacturer's own free channel.

Sticking to legitimate sources is what keeps the free side of this hobby alive. Every download from an authorized page is a small vote for the designers who choose to share their work.
