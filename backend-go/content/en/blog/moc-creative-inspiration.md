# Brick MOC Ideas: From Spark to Finished Build

Every MOC (My Own Creation) that makes people stop and look started as something much vaguer — a half-formed idea that had to be carried all the way to a finished model. Whether you have just picked up your first bricks or you are a long-time builder stuck in a dry spell, this collection of idea sources, design workflows and hands-on techniques is meant to be practical. The goal is simple: turn the fuzzy picture in your head into a build that actually holds together.

## 1. Where Ideas Come From: Five Sources

MOC ideas rarely appear out of nowhere. Feed them from the right places and they keep coming.

- **Borrow from real life.** Study the buildings, vehicles, plants and everyday objects around you, then translate real-world proportions and structure into brick language. It is the safest starting point and the one that ages best.
- **Film and animation classics.** Mecha, spaceships and street scenes often begin with a favourite screen design, but add your own take on shaping and colour. Otherwise a "tribute" quietly turns into a copy.
- **History and nature themes.** Ancient architecture, castles, dinosaurs and marine life carry built-in storytelling, which makes it easier for viewers to connect with the model.
- **Modding official LEGO sets.** Making local changes to an existing set (often called modding) is low in difficulty and fast in payoff — an excellent way to train your sense of structure.
- **Cross-pollination in the community.** Browse brick forums and creator platforms, at home and abroad, and watch how other builders solve corners, slopes and overhangs. Your structural vocabulary expands quickly.

## 2. From Idea to Design: A Three-Step Method

Once you have a direction, how do you give it structure and avoid abandoning the project halfway? This three-step process has been tested by plenty of builders.

### Step 1: Sketch and Set the Scale

Start with paper. Draw a front view and a side view to lock in the overall silhouette and the key dimensions. **Scale is the soul of a MOC** — decide up front how many centimetres of the real object one brick represents, then work out length, width and height from there. Skip this and your proportions will drift out of control later.

### Step 2: Break the Build into Modules

Split the model into separate **modules**: base, main body, roof, decoration and so on. Build and refine each one on its own, then assemble. Modular planning makes revisions easy, and it lets you reuse part combinations that worked well.

### Step 3: Colour and Detail Polish

Pick one dominant colour and one or two secondary colours, and keep the accent colour to a small share of the whole. Detail is what creates texture — how a roof eave turns a corner, the curve of a car window, the paving pattern on the ground. These **small details** are usually what separates a build that reads as expert work from one that reads as ordinary.

## 3. Breaking Through a Block: Practical Advice for Dry Spells

Inspiration is not something you wait for. It shows up after you start moving.

- **Set a hard constraint on the theme**, for example "only 30 different part types" or "no taller than 20 cm". Limits force creativity out.
- **Rebuild a familiar shape in unusual colours.** The result is often a surprise.
- **Sort your parts inventory regularly.** Knowing exactly which parts you own and how many of each is useful — a lot of ideas are hiding in the parts themselves.
- **Accept imperfection.** Finish a version that merely "looks okay" first, then iterate and improve it, rather than chasing perfection from the first brick.

## 4. Good Builds Deserve to Be Shared

When a MOC is finished, photograph it from several angles and write down your design thinking and parts list. **Sharing is not just a record — it is how you collect feedback and keep improving.**
