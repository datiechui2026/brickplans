# Police Station MOC Tutorial: From Badge to Interrogation Room

Sooner or later, anyone building a brick city ends up needing a police station. This review of my police station MOC tutorial lays out every mistake I made and every idea I kept, from the badge on the front wall to the last chair in the interrogation room. What separates a police station from an ordinary street building is how much machinery it has inside: garage, office floor and interview room each do a different job, and every level has to look good while telling a story. The build reviewed here is a three-bay frontage with a two-storey tower, sitting on a 48x48 baseplate. Here is what worked and what did not.

## Exterior and Facade: A Building That Looks Disciplined

A police station should read as steady and official. The palette leans on dark blue, grey and white with a little yellow as an accent, which sets it apart from the bright, busy buildings around it. I strongly recommend splitting the facade into three horizontal bands: dark brickwork with floor-to-ceiling windows at street level, light-coloured plates pulling horizontal lines through the middle, then the tower and a rooftop railing on top. Do not leave the walls flat. Alternate studded and smooth bricks to fake a mortar-joint texture, and run a ring of white trim around every window and door opening. That single detail makes the whole thing look sharp. Stand a signboard at the entrance and add a lamp beside it; at night it is the best-looking corner of the block.

Use the minifigure as your ruler. The main door has to be tall enough that a minifig wearing a cap walks through without ducking, and one storey works out at roughly four bricks of height. Go higher and the rooms feel hollow; go lower and everything feels cramped.

## Functional Zones: Garage, Office and Interrogation Room

The most valuable lesson in any police station MOC tutorial is circulation, meaning how people move through the building. The ground floor is garage plus reception: build the garage door from hinge pieces so it opens and closes, park a black-and-white police car inside, and leave an aisle along one side that leads to the reception desk. The second floor is the office, with workstations, filing cabinets and a notice board, plus a water cooler and coffee corner tucked into a corner so the space feels lived in.

The interrogation room deserves the most care. Keep it small and fully enclosed. Face it onto the corridor through one-way glass, made from dark transparent pieces set in a frame. Inside: one table, two chairs, a clock on the wall and a pendant lamp hanging low. The furnishing alone is enough for anyone to imagine the scene. Build the staircase as a module that slides out in one piece, so you can pull it free and rearrange the layout later.

## Detail Work: Badges, Windows and Signage

Details are where a police station MOC review really shows its craft. You do not need printed pieces for the badge: a gold shield-shaped plate with a small star element captures the look, and if the budget stretches, add a few printed badge tiles at the main entrance to anchor it. Use dark glass in white window frames, align the heights of every floor, and add an arched window at the top for a bit of gravitas.

Have three pieces of signage ready: a large sign above the door, a horizontal POLICE strip on the wall, and white thin elements marking out parking bays on the ground. Do not skip street furniture such as manhole covers and fire hydrants, because those small items are what make a street feel alive. Add accessories too: police caps, handcuffs and walkie-talkies. Rotate the display between three scenes, sentry duty, patrol and interrogation, and the storytelling takes care of itself.

## Parts List and Pitfalls: Notes Before You Start

Here is a reference list and a few warnings. Sort the basic parts into bags by colour. Stock plenty of dark blue and grey bricks; lay in white trim plates, transparent glass and dark transparent pieces for the one-way glass, ordering twenty to thirty percent more of those than you think you need; and buy hinge pieces according to how many doors you plan.

There are three main traps. First, when you cut a door opening, keep a beam above and below it, or the wall will sag once weight is stacked on top. Second, do not make the garage ceiling too high; half a brick of clearance above the police car's roof light is enough. Third, the one-way glass is easy to install facing the wrong way, so check the sightline from the minifigure's position before you fix it in place. One more tip: build the interior skeleton first and skin the exterior walls afterwards. You will save yourself a lot of dismantling.
