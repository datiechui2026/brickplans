# Brick Linkage Mechanisms: Make Static Builds Move

A lot of builders hit the same wall after finishing a building or a car: the model looks great, but it does nothing. If you want doors that swing open on their own, driving wheels on a steam locomotive, or a robot arm that actually reaches and lifts, the technique you need is the linkage mechanism. This guide covers the basic principle, the common linkage types, and how to build them with real bricks so your models stop sitting still.

## What Is a Linkage Mechanism?

A linkage is a moving system made of rigid bars joined at pivoting joints. In brick building it is usually assembled from Technic beams (the perforated bars), Technic pins and axles. The point of a linkage is conversion: it takes rotary motion from a motor or a hand crank and turns it into pushing, pulling, swinging or lifting — which is what gives a static model a sense of life.

Compared with gear trains, linkages are visually readable and easy to adjust. Even with no motor at all, a hand crank can demonstrate the full mechanical action, which makes linkages ideal for teaching and for display models where visitors want to see how something works.

## The Four-Bar Linkage: The Workhorse of Mechanics

The four-bar linkage is the simplest and most classic form: four bars joined end to end into a closed, movable loop, with one bar fixed as the frame. Change the relative lengths of the four bars and you get completely different motion.

For example, when the shortest bar is the fixed frame, you get a double-crank linkage and both outer bars rotate through full circles. When the shortest bar is the coupler — the bar between the two moving links — you get a double-rocker, and both outer bars can only swing back and forth. Many well-known brick machines are, at heart, variations on the four-bar linkage.

## Crank-Rod and Crank-Rocker Linkages

The crank-rod (slider-crank) linkage is the mechanism behind piston engine models: the crank rotates through a full circle while the connecting rod drives a piston in a straight reciprocating line. A mini engine needs only a crankshaft, a connecting rod and a crosshead. Turn the crank and the piston travels up and down; add a cylinder housing and the effect is genuinely striking.

The crank-rocker linkage converts full rotation into a back-and-forth swing. It shows up in windshield wipers, mixers and oscillating fans. A simple build: use a gear as the crank, insert a pin off-center, connect the connecting rod to that pin, and attach the other end to a rocker arm. Rotate the gear and the arm swings to and fro.

## Parallel Linkages

A parallel linkage is the general name for parallelogram linkages: opposite bars stay parallel at all times, so the mechanism produces true translation rather than rotation. You will find it in lifting platforms, drying racks, and the main boom of excavator models. Build it by keeping the two pairs of links equal in length, and a platform will rise and fall while staying level. Of all the linkage types, this is the most visually obvious and the friendliest starting point for beginners.

## Parts You Need for a Linkage Build

To keep a linkage running smoothly, it helps to have the following on hand:

- **Technic beams** — stock a range from 3-hole up to 15-hole; these form the linkage bars themselves.
- **Technic pins and axles** — these create the pivoting joints.
- **Gears** — used to make cranks and to change rotation speed.
- **Cross axle connectors** — handy for fixing a crank onto a motor or a hand wheel.

Two assembly rules matter. First, leave a little clearance at each pivot so parts are not clamped tight and jammed. Second, keep the linkage moving within a single plane wherever possible; this reduces twisting and friction so the action stays smooth.

## Project Ideas and Inspiration

Search terms like linkage, mechanism and steam engine on BrickPlans (https://brickplan.cn) and you will find plenty of plans built around linkages: the crank-rod drive on a steam locomotive's wheels, the parallel-linkage boom of an excavator, the flapping mechanism of a mechanical bird. A good learning path is to build one plan exactly as drawn, then work out what each pivot is doing, then start changing bar lengths and fixing points and watch how the motion changes. That is how you develop your own design instincts.

Once you understand linkage mechanisms, you have opened the door to the moving side of brick building. Start with the simplest four-bar and get your hands on the parts — the next model you finish can actually move.
