# LEGO School MOC Tutorial: Campus From Classroom to Playground

A school is one of the most familiar places from childhood, and it makes a wonderfully lively subject for a brick city layout. Compared with a standalone house or shop, a full school brings together several functional zones — teaching building, playground, gym — so the finished model feels rich and full of stories. This MOC tutorial walks you through the whole thing: from the master plan, to classrooms, the sports field, the gym and the perimeter wall, so you end up with a mini campus of your own.

## Step 1: Plan the Overall Campus Layout

Before you place a single brick, sketch the campus on paper or in your MOC software. A standard school MOC usually breaks into three parts: the teaching building (the core structure), the playground (activity space) and the supporting elements (gate, walls, flower beds). A U-shaped or L-shaped plan works well — the classroom block on one side, the playground in the open middle, walls enclosing everything, with walkways and planters filling the gaps. If you are working on 32x32 baseplates, two of them side by side give you a decent campus footprint. Fix the size and position of each zone first; the actual building goes far more smoothly once those boundaries are set.

## Step 2: Build the Teaching Building and Classrooms

The classroom block is the star of the campus. Two or three storeys, built as modules, is a sensible choice — it leaves room for changes later. Fit two or three classrooms per floor, and size each room so the desks actually fit: roughly 8x10 studs is the practical minimum. The build order follows the usual modular building logic: frame first, then walls, then windows and doors. Long strip windows make the classrooms look bright; use light-coloured bricks for the facade with dark window frames, then add a sign plate above the main entrance reading the school name — instantly recognisable. You can top the building with an extra level as a rooftop terrace or multi-purpose hall, which gives the block more depth.

## Step 3: Desks, Chairs and Classroom Interior Details

Interiors are where a build shows real care. Put four to six desk-and-chair sets in each classroom: a 2x2 plate for the desktop, 1x1 bricks for the legs, and a blackboard at the front made from a dark tile with white chalk lines. Raise the teacher's podium on 1x2 bricks and add a bookshelf or storage cabinet beside it. Hang a few "student artworks" on the wall — small coloured bricks arranged into picture frames. If you want the room to feel alive, place a potted plant by the window and a book and minifigure on a desk; looking in through the window, it genuinely reads as a class in session. These interior details are what separate a school MOC from an ordinary building.

## Step 4: The Playground and Sports Facilities

The playground is the most energetic part of the campus. The standard approach is to pave a flat area with grey plates, then lay out the track markings in white or red tiles to form a running track. At one end, build a basketball hoop: two posts plus a backboard, with the three-point line marked on the ground — add minifigures and the game is on. Alongside it you can add a sandpit, a set of horizontal and parallel bars, or a flag-raising platform, with a slim rod element as the pole and a small brick-built flag on top. Plant a few trees made from leaf elements around the edges to bring some greenery in.

## Step 5: Gym, School Gate and Perimeter Walls

The gym can be a separate structure beside the playground: a wide-span roof, either sloped or flat, with double doors at the front and a row of seating and a scoreboard inside. The school gate is the face of the campus — build it with gate posts and a crossbeam, mount the school name sign on the beam, and connect walls to either side. Run 1x2 bricks continuously around the perimeter, leave one opening for the main gate, and add a guard booth and a barrier arm at the entrance for extra detail. Finally, link the teaching building, playground and gym with paths and flower beds, and populate the scene with a few minifigures heading to class, playing ball or raising the flag. The school MOC is complete.

## Common Problems and Layout Optimisation

A few issues come up again and again. First, layouts that are too spread out: large gaps between buildings look empty, so fill them with planters, big trees and pavements. Second, an imbalance between the teaching building and the playground — aim for the building to occupy one third to one half of the baseplate and leave enough room for activities. Third, window orientation: classroom windows should face the playground side where possible, which reads as more realistic in both light and view.
