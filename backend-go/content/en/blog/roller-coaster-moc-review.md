# BrickPlan Roller Coaster MOC Review: 2,450 Parts, 8 Hours

Short version first: this BrickPlan roller coaster MOC understands gravity and inertia very well — the car rolls down the lift hill and completes a full lap and a half. **Around 2,450 parts, roughly 8 hours of building, about 300 CNY in loose parts**, which is around 55% cheaper than an official set. Here is the breakdown across three areas.

## 1. Design and Accuracy

It is a double-helix coaster layout. The main lift section stands about 38 cm tall, and the whole model spreads to roughly 90×70 cm, so it takes up real desk space and you need to plan for that. Blue track with yellow pillars gives a strong contrasting look with plenty of visual punch. The track is built from continuous 2×2 plates, and the curves transition gradually without a stepped feel. The station, guard rails and queue area are all detailed, and the model even recreates the stop block mechanism that launches the car out of the station.

## 2. Building Experience

The build runs in five stages: station base, lift tower, double-helix track, loop section, and the car mechanism. **The instructions total 214 steps at about 11 parts per step on average**, so the difficulty curve stays gentle. The two helices have to be built as mirror images of each other, and it is worth photographing your progress as you go to catch mistakes early. The inertia car is the heart of the model: its center of gravity sits toward the front, and released from the 38 cm hilltop it clears the double helix plus the loop in about 12 seconds. Track seams on third-party parts need a 1 mm shim for fine adjustment; with official-recommended bricks no correction is needed.

## 3. Value and Playability

Loose parts come to about 300 CNY plus roughly 25 CNY for the instructions, so 325 CNY total. Against official sets at 700–900 CNY that saves about 55%–60%. If you already keep a parts stock, you only need to add around 40% of the track-specific pieces, which can push the cost below 200 CNY. The real fun is in the tuning: nudging slope, radius and seams until the car makes it all the way around. Add a motor and it cycles fully automatically — it works as a building challenge and as a hands-on physics teaching tool, and it is well suited to building together with kids.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I get the car to complete the whole circuit on a brick coaster MOC?**
A: It comes down to gravity, inertia and the track gradient. The BrickPlan design's 38 cm climb and double helix give the car enough potential energy; the key is flush track seams and symmetric helices, otherwise the car slows and stalls in the turns.

**Q: How much does the BrickPlan roller coaster plan cost?**
A: The instructions are about 25 CNY, and the loose parts for 2,450 pieces come to roughly 300 CNY, so around 325 CNY all in. If you already have basic parts at home, you only need the track-specific pieces and can bring it under 200 CNY.

**Q: How is this different from a Ferris wheel MOC?**
A: The coaster focuses on track building and inertia tuning, and the play value is in the dynamic run. A Ferris wheel focuses on gear transmission and light-up display. The coaster is a bit harder to build and takes up more space, so it suits builders who want a mechanical structure challenge.

**Q: Can a beginner build this coaster plan?**
A: It is a moderate challenge — 214 steps and about 8 hours, and the mirrored track symmetry is not beginner-friendly. Build something under 1,500 pieces first, and you will get further with a parent-and-child team effort.

**Q: How do I troubleshoot a car that will not finish the run?**
A: First check that the track seams are flush and use a 1 mm shim on joints that are too tight. Next confirm the two helices are true mirror images. Finally check the lift tower actually reaches 38 cm. Those three checks solve about 90% of cases.

The part count is the honest headline here: 2,450 pieces is a weekend-plus project, and the 8-hour estimate assumes you are sorting as you go rather than hunting through bags. The 90×70 cm footprint is the other thing to plan for — this is not a shelf model, and you will want a stable table where the car can run its full 12-second circuit without being nudged.

The five-stage structure helps a lot. Getting the station base and lift tower square early makes the mirrored helices far easier later, and the loop section reads clearly in the instructions. Difficulty stays low thanks to the step density — around 11 parts per step means no long stretches of guessing — but the symmetry requirement is where most mistakes happen, so the advice to photograph the first helix and compare it against the second is worth taking seriously.
