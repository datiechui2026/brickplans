# Brick Hedgehog MOC Review: How to Build the Spiked Back

The verdict up front: a hedgehog model succeeds or fails on its spiked back. Lay the spines in layers using slope and cone pieces — dense at the outer edge, sparser toward the center — and you get a convincing prickly read without clutter. Everything else is a round body plus a nose and a pair of eyes that do the character work.

## Spikes Are the Soul of This Hedgehog MOC

Hedgehogs are one of the most instantly recognizable subjects in animal MOC building: a round body topped by a back full of spines. That back is exactly where this hedgehog blueprint gets hard. The spines have to register clearly as spikes without turning into visual noise.

When reviewing, look at the spike solution first. A good blueprint uses slopes, wedge pieces or dedicated spike elements arranged in a regular arc across the back, so the silhouette reads rounded from the front and only shows the spike layering from the side. If the design simply jabs a handful of spikes in wherever they fit, the finished model tends to look sloppy. This is the first thing to check before you commit to a build.

## Round Body and Color Blocking

A hedgehog only looks cute if it is genuinely round. Blueprints in this style usually build the body volume with curved pieces or half-dome pieces, then assign dark brown or black to the spike zone on the back and light brown or off-white to the face, producing the classic two-tone split.

Two things to evaluate. First, is the body curve natural? The transition from the top of the head into the back should not show a staircase or step effect. Second, are the color zones clean? The boundary line between the spiked area and the face needs to be crisp. Some designs add lighter pieces underneath to suggest a belly, which gives the model more depth. On proportions, a slightly flattened, wider body paired with a smaller head lands closest to a real hedgehog's stumpy charm.

## Face and Expression: Nose and Eyes

The face is where the charm lives. A typical face design covers three features: a small black round nose, round eyes, and small ears.

Check the nose's position and size. A hedgehog's nose sits at the very front of the face and tilts slightly upward, and a black 1x1 round piece gets the job done. Eyes are black round pieces with a highlight, set on either side above the nose. Ears are small curved slopes standing on both sides at the top of the head, in the same color as the face.

A good blueprint places all of these small features clearly so the finished model looks alert and alive. Weak instructions blur the features together and the model ends up with a blank, lifeless expression.

## Limbs and Pose

Short legs are the part of the structure most likely to go wrong. These blueprints normally give the hedgehog four short legs built from small dark brown pieces connected to the underside of the body.

Watch two things. Leg length matters: too short and the body has no support, too long and the animal stops looking like a hedgehog. Leg placement matters too: the front and rear legs need to be separated rather than crammed together.

Some designs also include the curled-up ball pose — the defensive roll a real hedgehog uses when threatened. That version is far more demanding structurally, because the head and all four limbs have to tuck inside the envelope of back spikes. Pulled off, it looks stunning, but the difficulty climbs a lot. Beginners should build the standing or walking pose first.

## Instructions Quality and Beginner Friendliness

Overall, the hedgehog is a beginner-friendly animal MOC subject. Part counts usually fall between 100 and 200 pieces, and the structure is not complicated.

When evaluating this hedgehog blueprint, focus on three things: whether the step diagrams are clear and zoomed in adequately, whether the parts list for the spike section is complete, and whether the finished model is photographed from multiple angles. Spike elements are the core parts of this build, so confirm before you buy that the source for them is convenient.

Once the model is done, a simple grass or fallen-leaf baseplate gives the hedgehog somewhere to stand and completes the piece. All told, this blueprint suits beginners who want to try an animal MOC, and it also works well as a building exercise for children.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose a brick hedgehog MOC blueprint?**

A: Start with the spike solution. A good blueprint layers slopes and cone pieces so the spines are dense at the outer edge and sparser toward the center, with a consistent direction. If the spikes are random parts pushed in wherever they fit, the finished model will almost certainly look messy.

**Q: How do I lay the spikes so they don't look chaotic?**

A: Follow the dense-outside, sparse-inside rule. Use longer cone pieces along the central axis of the back as the main mass, then shorten and tighten them progressively toward both sides, all angled the same way toward the rear. Alternate dark brown and light brown and the layering appears on its own.

**Q: How do I give the hedgehog's face an expression?**

A: The nose tip and eyes carry it. Use a 1x1 black slope or a small round piece for the nose, and 1x1 round plates for the eyes, keeping them symmetrical left and right. Round off the face shape with curved pieces so no hard corners show.

**Q: Is this blueprint suitable for beginners?**

A: Yes. The hedgehog's body is round, the structure is not complex, and the four-legged pose is simple, which makes it an entry-level subject among animal MOCs. As long as you follow the blueprint when laying the spikes and keep everything symmetrical, a beginner can get it right on the first build.
