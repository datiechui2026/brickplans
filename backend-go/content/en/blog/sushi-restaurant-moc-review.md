# Sushi Restaurant MOC Review: Japanese Street Corner Build

Food shops are always the most likeable buildings in a modular street, and a Japanese sushi restaurant sits right at the top of the detail scale. For this **sushi restaurant MOC review** we actually built a Japanese corner sushi shop from a community designer's instructions, scoring it from unboxing to shelf display so you get a clear answer before you buy.

## 1. Packaging and Parts: First Impressions Out of the Box

The instructions use **1:48 street-scene scale**, and the whole shop comes to roughly 1,800 parts split across 6 bags. The palette leans on four families — **off-white, dark brown, dark green and light wood** — so the Japanese colour scheme reads correctly at a glance. The printed parts are a real highlight: the sushi lettering on the shop sign and the price board on the menu are printed rather than stickered, so they hold up to handling and will not curl at the edges. That is a genuine plus. Missing parts were effectively zero and the bag sorting was clean throughout, so this section gets **9/10**.

## 2. Architecture: A Small but Precise Japanese Facade

The shop occupies a 16x16 stud footprint over two floors. The ground floor is an open serving counter; the second floor is a loft plus a terrace. The facade work is genuinely thoughtful:

- **Eaves**: dark brown slope bricks are layered and stacked to form a Japanese karahafu-style gable, and the curve reads as very smooth from the front.
- **Noren curtain**: two 1x3 dark blue plates sit side by side as the doorway curtain, with a gap between them that lets light through — push it apart and you are looking straight at the counter. This is the single most striking design in the whole shop.
- **Shoji windows**: the upper floor uses white frames plus translucent panels as grid windows, and once warm light is added the shadow play is superb.

Structurally, the walls are reinforced with inner plates, the second floor bears weight reliably, the clutch is good and there is no bulging on the outside faces. Architecture scores **9/10**.

## 3. Food Details: The Conveyor Belt Steals the Show

As a **food-themed street build**, the star of this sushi restaurant is unquestionably the **conveyor sushi belt**. A track of tread links loops around the counter, carrying:

- salmon sushi (orange 1x1 round parts on a white rice base);
- tamagoyaki (yellow 2x2 plates stacked two high);
- takoyaki (brown-and-white 1x1 round parts).

The belt can be turned by hand, which makes it far more interactive than most shop interiors. Behind the counter, a minifigure chef is shaping sushi, complete with chef's hat and apron, and the counter is dressed with small pieces of wasabi green and pickled ginger pink. The one shortfall is that the sushi selection is thin — if you want a full feast you will be adding dishes yourself. Details score **8.5/10**.

## 4. Lighting and Overall Atmosphere

With a warm yellow light strip installed, the glow spilling through the noren and the shoji windows is genuinely charming; set it on a late-night street and the everyday warmth of a small Japanese shop comes straight through. Add a red lantern by the door (a transparent red 1x1 sphere plus a light strip) and the mood steps up another level. Lighting compatibility scores **8/10**.

## 5. Overall Score and Buying Advice

**Verdict: worth buying.** This **sushi restaurant MOC** sits at medium-to-high difficulty (around 6 hours), so it is a little unfriendly to newcomers, but experienced builders and modular street collectors should go straight for it. It suits especially:

- builders who want to add a late-night diner mood to their street;
- fans of conveyor-belt sushi who are happy to add extra dishes and expand the build;
- picky builders who value printed parts and hate stickers.

The main weaknesses are the limited range of sushi and the single way up to the second floor (an external ladder only). Those flaws do not outweigh the strengths, though — this is still the most complete **food-themed street build** to come along recently.
