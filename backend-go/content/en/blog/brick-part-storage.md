# How to Sort and Store LEGO Parts So You Can Find Them Fast

Here is the short version: sort by part type first and by colour second, insist on transparent containers, and if you want the best value, go for a 24-60 compartment clear drawer unit plus a label maker.

The worst part of building is hunting for one 2x4 brick and spending ten minutes digging through a full bin. Once your collection grows, sorting stops being optional and becomes a required skill for every MOC builder. This guide covers the sorting logic, the containers, and the habits that keep the system working.

## Why Sorting and Storing Matters

Most beginners reach for colour first: a red box, a blue box. Colour-first is the least efficient method available, because a single colour can span more than a hundred different part types, so you still end up rummaging. The core logic of brick part storage is to sort by part type first and colour second: decide *what it is*, then decide *what colour it is*. Builders who work this way cut their average part-finding time by more than 70%, and breaking down old sets or free-building from mixed parts gets much faster too.

## Sorting by Part Type: Bricks, Plates, Slopes and Special Pieces

Work through three levels:

- **Level one (broad categories):** bricks, plates, slopes, tiles, special pieces (hinges, gears, technic elements and similar), and minifigures with accessories. Six to eight categories is plenty.
- **Level two (part type):** break each category down by size. Plates, for example, split into high-frequency sizes such as 1x1, 1x2 and 2x4, each in its own box.
- **Level three (colour):** only split by colour when a single part type no longer fits in one box.

In practice, special pieces are the most commonly neglected group. Hinges, gears, treads and transparent elements are the parts you need in a hurry but own in small numbers, so keep them together in a small portable tray at the edge of your build area. Store minifigures in five sub-categories: heads, torsos, legs, hair and accessories, so a character can be assembled on the spot.

## Clear Drawers and a Label System

On containers, transparency is non-negotiable. The single most important rule of part storage is that you can only find what you can see; opaque cardboard boxes and fabric bags make all your sorting pointless. Three setups work well: a clear drawer cabinet with 24-60 compartments, clear zip bags inside a storage bin, or multi-layer parts boxes.

The labelling system matters just as much. Use a label maker or write labels by hand, and keep one consistent format: part type plus size, for example "Plate 2x4" or "Slope 1x2x3". Colour does not belong on the label because your eyes can handle that. Put labels on the front of each drawer, and on zip bags place them along the folded top edge so they do not peel off as you open and close the bag.

## Common Storage Setups and Budgets

Three tiers, by budget:

- **Entry level (about ¥50-100):** clear zip bags plus a storage box with dividers, suitable for collections under 5,000 pieces.
- **Intermediate (about ¥300-600):** two or three 24-60 compartment clear drawer units plus a label maker, suitable for 10,000-20,000 pieces. This is the best value tier.
- **Enthusiast (¥1,000 and up):** a multi-compartment drawer wall system combined with a workbench, for heavy builders with very large part counts.

Two things to check before buying: drawer compartments must be deep enough for your tallest brick, and the dividers should be removable so you can re-partition the space whenever your collection changes.

## Maintenance and Part-Finding Habits

Sorting is not a one-time project; it is a system you maintain. A few practical habits:

- Spend 10 minutes putting parts away after every build or teardown. Do not let a "temporary pile" become a parts mountain.
- Sort new parts by type before they go into storage. Never tip a new bag straight into a mixed bin.
- Audit your drawers once a quarter and send any stray pieces back to their correct home.
- Use the two-finger pinch: grip the part from the side of the drawer with thumb and index finger instead of raking through it with your whole hand.

Once the habits stick, sorted storage gives you more than a tidy room — it protects your building momentum, because ideas survive only when parts are quick to find.

## FAQ

**Q: Why doesn't colour-based sorting work well for brick parts?**
A: Because a single colour often covers more than a hundred different part types, so colour-sorted boxes still leave you searching. Sort by part type first and colour second, and average part-finding time drops by more than 70%.

**Q: How many sorting levels should I use?**
A: Three. Level one splits the collection into six to eight categories: bricks, plates, slopes, tiles, special pieces, and minifigures. Level two splits each category by size, for example plates into 1x1, 1x2 and 2x4. Level three only kicks in when one part type overflows its box and needs splitting by colour.

**Q: What is a sensible container and budget setup?**
A: Around ¥50-100 buys clear zip bags plus a storage box with dividers, which suits collections under 5,000 pieces. Around ¥300-600 buys two or three 24-60 compartment clear drawer units plus a label maker, which suits 10,000-20,000 pieces and offers the best value.

**Q: What is the easiest way to store special pieces and minifigures?**
A: Keep hinges, gears, treads and transparent elements together in a small portable tray within reach of your build area, since they are needed urgently and owned in small quantities. Store minifigures in five sub-categories — heads, torsos, legs, hair and accessories — so you can grab whatever you need and build.
