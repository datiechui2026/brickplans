# Lighthouse Brick MOC: Tapered Tower, Stripes and Lamp Room

## Why a Lighthouse Brick MOC Is Worth Building

A lighthouse is the soul of a coastal scene. One tower standing on a rock outcrop pulls the eye harder than an entire expanse of water. What separates a lighthouse brick MOC from an ordinary set is that the architecture itself - the taper, the stripes, the lamp room - has to be built into the bricks. The tower is not a straight cylinder; it narrows as it rises. The colour scheme is not random; it is the classic red and white banding. The top has to light up and rotate. Get those details right and the finished model commands the table the moment you set it down.

## Tapering the Tower So It Stands Right

The taper is the step most lighthouse brick MOCs get wrong. The tower needs a cone profile, wide at the base and narrow at the top, and the way to build it is to pull the circumference inward by one ring every few courses. Start from a 2x2 core column, then every 4 layers step the outer ring in by one stud, easing the transition with 1x1 corner bricks and slope bricks to get a smooth cone. In testing, a taper of 3 to 5 degrees reads most naturally - steeper and it looks like a chimney, flatter and it stops reading as a lighthouse at all. The critical thing is that every setback lines up. On my build one side stepped in before the other and the whole tower leaned; it took three layers of rework to correct. Mark the setback point on each course before you attach anything.

## Red and White Stripes: Colour Swap or Tile-On

The red and white banding is the lighthouse's signature, and there are two ways to do it, each with trade-offs. The **colour-swap** method simply alternates red and white bricks course by course. It is structurally the simplest and the strongest, but stripe width is locked to brick height, so the bands can look clumsy. The **tile-on** method builds the tower entirely in white and then applies a skin of red plates or 1x1 bricks with side studs. The stripes come out finer and you can build banded edging too. My finding: for stripes in the 2 to 3 brick range, the colour swap is enough - quick and clean. If you want the three-dimensional colour separation of a real lighthouse, go tile-on, but remember to cap the corner of each stripe with a corner plate, or the applied skin will lift at the edges.

## The Lamp Room and the Rotating Light

The lamp room at the top is the lighthouse's face. Wrap transparent 1x2 plates around all four sides for the glass, then hold them down with white frame bricks so the light passes through cleanly. For the light itself you can pick one of two approaches. The **rotating beacon** uses a single axle running the full height of the tower, with radiating yellow blades at the top set 45 degrees apart to suggest a sweeping beam. The **lit version** is simpler: hide a light brick or a small LED set beneath the lamp room floor and the whole tower comes alive through the transparent housing. I built both into this model, and with the room lights off the sweeping glow takes the build up a full level. The one rule is that the rotation axle has to run through the base and be fixed there, otherwise it binds after a few turns.

## Base and Coastal Scene: Stability and Atmosphere

However good the lamp room looks, it counts for nothing if the base is not solid. The taller the tower, the higher the centre of gravity, so build the base as a solid mass: lay 2x4 bricks across the full footprint, stack dark grey slope bricks around the outside as rock, and scatter a few trans-light-blue pieces for surf and spray. You do not need to cover a whole baseplate for the coastal scene - one ring of rock plus a couple of small islets is enough, and an old captain minifigure and a rowboat give it a story immediately. In testing the model came to roughly 800 pieces, and the base accounted for a third of them. That is a worthwhile investment: a stable base is the precondition for every detail above it.

## Interior Stairs and Final Verdict

An interior staircase is a bonus that is easy to skip. Once the tower tapers, the inside space is tight and a spiral stair is very hard to fit, so build a single straight flight instead: stack 1x2 plates step by step, leave a four-plate height difference between treads, and add a transparent window for daylight. The build only feels complete if a minifigure can actually climb to the lamp room. Overall this lighthouse brick MOC sits at medium-high difficulty and takes about 7 hours. The taper alignment and the applied stripe skin demand the most attention, but the lighting result far exceeded what I expected - it is the most photogenic thing I have built this year.
