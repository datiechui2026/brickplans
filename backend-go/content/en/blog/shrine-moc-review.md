# Shrine MOC Review: Torii, Eaves and Japanese Proportions

Japanese shrines are one of the most instantly recognizable subjects in brick MOC building, and a **shrine MOC** lives or dies on proportion and colour. A torii gate, vermilion columns, blue-grey tiled eaves — get the balance slightly wrong and the model reads as a generic Chinese pavilion instead. For this **shrine MOC instruction review**, I built a complete shrine plan from start to finish and broke it down section by section: colour guidance, structural stability, and the roof techniques that decide whether the Japanese aesthetic actually survives the translation into bricks.

## 1. Instruction Quality and Colour Guidance

The biggest strength of this set is its **excellent colour guidance**. Vermilion is handled with dedicated red bricks, shadow under the eaves with dark brown, and the tiled roof surface with a deep grey-blue. Every zone carries a colour code, so the finished model holds a single, coherent palette with no stray tones jumping out. The instructions are **split into separate booklets**: one for the torii, one for the main hall, and one for the scenery pieces (lanterns and the temizuya water pavilion). You can build any of them on their own or combine them into one full scene.

The weak point is print quality. Some of the photographs are underexposed, and the detail under the eaves is fiddly to make out on the printed page — keep the digital version open and zoomed in while you build those sections.

## 2. Structural Stability and Proportion

Japanese architecture depends on **upturned corner rafters and deep roof overhang**, and this plan gets there with **slope bricks plus curved corner pieces** rather than flat stacked wedges. The result is a much more natural ridge curve than straight trapezoid pieces can produce. The main hall body is a classic **box mortise-and-tenon structure**: four corner columns run the full height from base to roof, and the wall panels are inset rather than stud-mounted. It is genuinely solid — I shook the finished hall and nothing shifted.

On proportion, the plan holds the torii at roughly **one third the height of the main hall**, which leaves generous open sightlines between the two. Standing them together gives a real sense of spatial layering rather than a single crowded block. The one thing to watch is the ridge junction, where the parts are on the fragile side — do not press hard when seating them.

## 3. Building Experience and Part Usage

This is a slow-and-steady build rather than a quick one, at roughly **1,200 pieces** for the full scene. The most demanding stage is the **layered eave assembly**: you first lay a lining plate along the inside of the roof edge, then stack the tile surface on top. The steps repeat heavily but follow a clear pattern, so once the rhythm clicks the roof goes quickly.

Part usage is where the design really shows its experience. The **lanterns combine transparent elements with small warm-yellow round studs** to fake candlelight, and lit from behind at night they look genuinely atmospheric. The temizuya's stone basin is built from grey smooth tiles to get the surface texture right. The torii crossbeams are held with long smooth tiles plus clip elements, and the **spacing between the two uprights can be fine-tuned**. There are **no stickers anywhere** — every detail is brick-built, which is a real plus in my book.

## 4. Pros, Cons and Who It's For

**Pros of this shrine MOC plan:** professional colour direction, a stable structure, and a booklet split that lets you build at whatever scale you want.

**Cons:** the roof uses a wide variety of part types, so **spare-parts pressure is high**. The plan only provides a total piece list with no per-bag allocation, which means anyone building from loose stock will have to sort everything themselves before starting.

**Who it suits:** intermediate to advanced builders who like Eastern architecture, or who have done a few modular street scenes and want a change of subject. It also works well as a companion piece for a Japanese town diorama. If you are newer to the hobby, start with the torii on its own as a warm-up before committing to the full scene.
