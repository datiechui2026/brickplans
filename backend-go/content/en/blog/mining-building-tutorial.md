# Mining MOC Tutorial: How to Build a Mine Entrance, Rails and a Quarry Site

Want an industrial-looking brick mine but you are not sure where to start? This tutorial walks through the whole build in order: the terrain and rock walls, the mine entrance and tunnel, the haulage track and mine carts, then the equipment and small details. Work through it step by step and you end up with a scene that has real depth instead of a flat pile of grey bricks.

## Terrain and Rock Walls

The terrain is the foundation of the whole model, so build it first. Stack dark grey, light grey and a small amount of dark brown bricks into uneven, rising rock walls, then use slope bricks and curved slopes to create natural fault lines. Do not make the walls too smooth or too regular. Stagger the layers so a few bare "ore seams" show through, and dot those with bricks in a contrasting colour to suggest mineral veins.

Lay dark grey baseplates across the floor, then scatter irregular small pieces as rubble piles — it reads as debris from blasting and makes the ground far more convincing. If you want more depth in photos, split the rock into two rows: a lower front row and a taller back row, with a road running between them. The gap gives you foreground, midground and background, and the camera will pick up that separation immediately.

## The Mine Entrance and Tunnel

The entrance is the visual centre of the scene, so give it the most attention. Build an arched opening from dark bricks and reinforce the top with a horizontal beam. On either side of the opening you can add timber supports or a metal frame structure — either style works, and mixing both suggests an old mine that has been patched up over the years.

Inside the tunnel, use black bricks to create a sense of darkness. Angle the side walls inward slightly as they go back so the tunnel narrows in perspective; even two or three studs of taper makes the opening look much deeper than it is. Leave a small flat area in front of the entrance. That apron is where the track and the mine cart sit, and it also gives you room to add lighting later without the wires fighting the rock work.

## Haulage Tracks and Mine Carts

The track is the part that makes a mining scene read as a working site rather than a diorama. Run two parallel rails from dark grey long bricks or tiles, and use small light-coloured pieces as sleepers between them. Keep the gauge identical along the entire run — a track that widens or narrows at the curve is the fastest way to break the illusion. Curving the track out of the tunnel entrance and away across the scene adds a lot of movement to an otherwise static layout.

For the mine cart, build a dark body with a lighter tub on top and mount four small wheels so it actually sits on the rails. Drop a few "ore" chunks into the tub. That single detail gives the picture a story: the cart is loaded and waiting, or it has just come out of the tunnel.

## Mining Equipment and Scene Details

Place a small excavator at one side of the mine mouth, using yellow and black bricks for the boom and the tracks. Next to it, add a conveyor unit carrying ore away from the entrance — it explains how material leaves the tunnel and ties the entrance to the rest of the site.

Around the floor, add a handful of miner minifigures, a warning sign, oil drums and a tool rack. These small items do more for realism than another layer of rock. This tutorial particularly recommends planting a few dark-coloured trees around the outer edge of the site, or adding a small wooden hut. The contrast between industry and nature is what makes a mining scene memorable rather than merely grey.

At the far end of the track, build a tipping platform or a stockpile area. That completes the transport route: ore comes out of the tunnel, the cart runs along the rails, and the load ends up somewhere. A scene with a finished route feels designed; a scene where the track just stops feels unfinished.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Scale is the mistake builders most often overlook. The track, the mine cart and the minifigures all have to agree with each other, and the rails must be wide enough to physically hold the cart. Check this before you detail anything, because fixing the gauge later means rebuilding the whole track bed.

Rock colour is the second trap. A wall in a single shade of grey looks flat and lifeless — mix at least three greys of different values, and use the darkest ones in the deepest recesses. Do not overload the scene with details either. Build the main structure first, run the track, then add decoration. If you scatter props everywhere from the start, the layout turns messy and the eye has nowhere to rest.
