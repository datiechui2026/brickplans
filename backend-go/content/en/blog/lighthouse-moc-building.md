# Build a Brick Lighthouse MOC: Proportions and Lighting

Start with the conclusion: the first step of a lighthouse build is fixing the tower's proportions — taper it from base to top, either as a cone or in stepped setbacks. Red-and-white striping is the signature palette, and the lantern room plus a light kit is what makes the tower actually glow.

## Set the Tower Proportions First

Lighthouses are one of the most instantly readable subjects in brick building MOCs: striped tower, lantern on top, recognizable at a glance. The first step is deciding proportions. The tower is usually built as a cone or with stepped setbacks, narrowing from the bottom to the top. For a small-scale lighthouse, stacked round bricks are enough. At medium scale, curved pieces make a better shell. At large scale, you can build a faceted, multi-sided structure. A tower height to base width ratio between 3:1 and 5:1 looks balanced — too tall and it feels top-heavy, too squat and it loses the lighthouse's upward thrust. Sketch a simple proportion drawing before you touch a brick; it saves a lot of rework.

## Red and White Stripes: The Signature Color Scheme

The striped body is the defining element. There are two ways to get it. The first is stacking alternating red and white curved bricks layer by layer — simple, but the stripe width is locked in. The second is to build a white tower first and then wrap red pieces around the outside as a decorative band — more flexible, but more steps. To keep stripes tidy, the key is rotating each layer by a consistent angle and aligning stud positions as you stack. Some builders go for spirals, rotating slope pieces as they climb; the result is more refined but harder, and beginners are better off starting with level bands.

## The Lantern Room: Structural Core

The lantern room is the focal point and the technical hard part. It is usually built as an octagon or a circle, ringed with transparent pieces and holding a light-core structure inside. A common approach: form the walls from transparent panel pieces, cap it with a conical or domed piece, and add railing pieces as a fence around the outside. The room has to house the lighting components, so leave at least a 2x2 space inside. Below the lantern room there is normally an overhanging gallery — build it from bracket and railing pieces so the transition from tower to lantern looks natural. Plan that gallery's height and width in advance, or the lantern room's proportions will drift off.

## Lighting: Making the Lighthouse Actually Shine

Lighting is the soul of a lighthouse MOC. There are three tiers of solution. At the lowest cost, drop a light brick straight into the lantern room. One step up, run an LED strip around the inside wall for even light. At the high end, use LED beads with diffuser pieces to produce a rotating beam effect. Two things to watch when choosing lights. First, cable routing: the wires must run from the lantern room down through the tower to the base, so the tower interior needs a channel — it can't be solid all the way through. Second, heat: for long sessions, pick low-power beads. Builders who want to skip wiring can fake the glow with transparent yellow pieces, which read fine in daylight, though they can't match the mood of a real light after dark.

## Coastal Base and Scene Pairing

A lighthouse rarely stands alone — a coastal base gives it a story. The base can be a rocky shore, a breakwater or a dock. Stack dark grey and brown irregular pieces for rock, dab in white and light blue pieces for surf, and put a small boat or a few fisherman minifigures out front. The base is also your counterweight: build it wide enough to keep the tower stable. That matters especially once lighting is installed, since the center of gravity sits high and the base has to hold it down. Keep the base height in proportion with the tower — typically a quarter to a third of the overall height.

## Beginner Advice and Where to Go Next

For a first lighthouse, build a small version 20-30 cm tall to learn the striping and lantern room basics; once that feels comfortable, take on a large lighthouse over 40 cm. Beyond classic red and white, black and white or blue and white also work. Advanced builders can add a rotating beam, an openable interior with a full room inside, or a whole coastal diorama with lighthouse, reef and boats. Put the finished model on a desk or windowsill and switch the lights on at night, and the atmosphere does the rest.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I taper a lighthouse tower?**

A: Use a cone or stepped setbacks: at small scale, reduce the diameter of round bricks layer by layer; at large scale, offset plates inward. Narrowing from bottom to top is what gives the tower its upward stretch.

**Q: How do I split the red and white stripes naturally?**

A: Divide the height into equal bands, usually three or four alternating red and white groups. Wrap each band with bricks of a single color all the way around so no seam breaks the color, and start the first stripe just above the base for proportions closer to a real lighthouse.

**Q: How is the lantern room built?**

A: The lantern room is the structural core: ring transparent pieces to make the glazing, add a conical roof and railings on top, place one transparent warm-colored light piece inside, and leave wiring space in case you add lights later.

**Q: How do I add lighting?**

A: Leave a cable channel inside the tower and mount the light unit at the floor of the lantern room, aimed upward. Warm white or warm yellow beads read best as a beam — a lower color temperature suits the look of an old lighthouse, so avoid cool white.
