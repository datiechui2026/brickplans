# Street Hotel MOC Build Guide: Lobby, Facade, Lighting

**The short version:** a street hotel wins on three things — a double-height lobby, a deliberate window-and-wall rhythm, and signage lighting. Every load-bearing point in that open lobby has to be reinforced with technic pieces, or the whole thing sags into a plain grid apartment block.

## Why Every Street Module Needs a Hotel

In a finished brick street, the hotel is usually the visual peak. Next to low modules like a coffee shop or a flower shop, a three- or four-storey hotel lifts the whole skyline and gives the build depth and layering. The catch is that hotels are big and full of repeated units, so beginners tend to end up with something stiff and flat. This guide breaks a satisfying street hotel down from structure to detail, step by step.

## Lobby Height and the Glass Curtain Wall

The lobby is the face of the building, so go for a two-storey void. The method: float the lobby floor slab and carry it on columns at the two sides only, leaving a full-height open interior. Then drop in a reception desk, sofas and a chandelier — the space feels luxurious the moment you walk in.

The front of that void is the natural place for a glass curtain wall. Stack transparent 1x2 bricks row by row and interleave door frame elements at intervals to get full-height glazing. To suggest the metal mullions, lay a silver tile along the seam of every course — the layering reads instantly. And reinforce the load-bearing points of the open structure with technic pieces, otherwise the upper floor slab will sag. This is exactly the detail worth checking when you buy a hotel blueprint.

## Window and Wall Rhythm on the Guest Floors

The middle and upper floors are guest rooms, and this is where builds turn into identical grid blocks. The fix is rhythm: you don't need every window perfectly aligned. Group two or three windows together, then offset each group by half a brick in height to imitate the panel joints on a real hotel facade. Keep window frames in a single dark colour, use a lighter tone on the sills for contrast, and run a decorative band around each floor — the facade suddenly has a beat. Balconies can be built from low railing pieces: they don't block interior light and they add detail to the wall.

## Signage and Canopy Details

The soul of the hotel is its sign. Put a large sign directly above the lobby, tiled as a base with letter tiles spelling HOTEL, and frame it with warm light strips so it pops at night. Don't skip the canopy either: span the entrance with plates and hang a row of small round lights or drop elements along the lower edge — shade and decoration in one. Two planters and a doorman minifigure either side of the door give the entrance some life.

## Lighting Design

Lighting is what finishes a street hotel. Fit light panels to the inside of the glass curtain wall and pair them with pale interior walls so the lobby glows as a whole. On the guest floors, add a warm light every few windows to suggest occupancy. Use cool white for the sign and canopy lights so they read differently. If you can, extend the street lamp module across the hotel frontage so it joins the lighting of the neighbouring shops into one continuous warm band.

## Joining the Neighbouring Modules

A street is judged as a whole, and the hotel can't stand in isolation. Put a three-storey residential building on its left and a two-storey shop on its right to create a stepped transition in height. Leave roughly a one-stud gap between buildings — it makes cable runs easier and reads more like a real street. Share the same pavement tiles and curb across modules, and dot small scene elements like a street sign or a post box in front of the hotel. When you join modules, line them up on the studs of the baseplate so there's no height mismatch.

## Final Thoughts

A good street hotel doesn't come from expensive parts — it comes from the combination of a double-height lobby, window-and-wall rhythm and signage lighting. Plan before you build: mark the function and height of every floor clearly and you'll stay confident as you go.
