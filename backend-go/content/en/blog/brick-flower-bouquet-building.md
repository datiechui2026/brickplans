# Brick Flower Bouquet MOC: Parts, Colors and Stem Guide

The short version: a brick bouquet is not a difficult build. What makes or breaks it is part choice and color planning. Each flower type — rose, tulip, sunflower, hydrangea — has parts that read as that flower at a glance, a palette built on one main color plus one support color plus an accent stays clean, and flexible hose keeps the stems where you put them.

## Why brick bouquets are so popular

Brick bouquets have taken off fast because you get the fun of building plus a finished piece that never wilts — on a desk it often holds up better than the real thing. The barrier to entry for **brick flower bouquet building** is genuinely low; the core skills are picking the right flower parts and getting the colors right. Most bouquet MOCs center on roses, tulips, sunflowers, hydrangeas and small daisies, because those shapes are distinctive and stay recognizable when rebuilt in brick. If it is your first bouquet, start with a single variety — build a bunch of tulips first, then move on to a mixed bouquet once you are comfortable.

## Flower parts: petals, centers and leaves

**Brick flower bouquet building** depends on a handful of key part families. Petals usually come from curved pieces, petal elements and slopes; a rose reads most naturally when you layer curved panels one on top of another. Centers are made with round plates, studs or printed tiles — a sunflower's dark center works well as a dense bed of 1x1 plates. Leaves and calyxes use green slopes, leaf elements and connectors. The connection is the part people underestimate: stems run on axles or long bars, and the flower head is fixed with a cross-hole or round-hole connector so the bloom can rotate slightly for angle adjustments. Sort your parts by color before you start and the build goes much faster.

## Color schemes that look considered, not cheap

Color is about seventy percent of whether a bouquet looks good. There are two approaches to **brick flower bouquet building**: same-family gradients and contrast accents. Gradients suit a soft look — a pink bouquet running from pale pink up to magenta stays harmonious and easy on the eye. Contrast accents suit a livelier look: main flowers in white or cream, with a few bright yellow or orange small blooms as accents, gives the eye a focal point. Do not neglect the greens — leaves in two or three different shades, interleaved, are what give the bouquet depth. Beginners should avoid too many colors at once; keep it to three main colors and the finished piece looks far more refined.

## Stem structure and wrapping

Stems and wrapping decide whether the bouquet can stand up on its own. When **brick flower bouquet building**, run stems on long axles or extended bar elements, and leave enough length to account for the height of the wrapping. Once each flower is done, gather the bottoms with connectors into a single stable handle structure. The wrapping can be a cone of curved panels, or transparent elements standing in for cellophane, with a "ribbon" band added on the outside. To let the bouquet stand upright, the base needs weight and stability — tuck a few weight bricks into the bottom of the wrapping. If you are going the vase route instead, match stem length to the depth of the vase neck, and stagger the flower heights so the blooms sit at different levels once inserted.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I keep a brick bouquet from looking cheap?**
A: Color comes first. Pick one main color (mid pink or cream white, say), add one or two support colors, then a small amount of a bright color as an accent. Keep the whole bouquet to three colors or fewer and it will look refined.

**Q: Which flower parts do I need?**
A: Roses, tulips, sunflowers and hydrangeas are the common flower types, each with its own petal and center elements. Leaves combine slopes and curved pieces; stems use bendable flexible hose so you can adjust the pose.

**Q: How do I fix the stems so the bouquet does not fall over?**
A: Run the flexible hose stems down into a vase or stand, and fix the bottom with perforated bricks acting as floral foam. Keep the center of gravity low, and for bouquets with lots of branches, add weight bricks at the bottom of the vase.

**Q: Is a brick bouquet easy to maintain?**
A: Very. Nothing wilts and nothing needs watering. Just keep it out of direct light, dust it, and brush it gently with a soft brush now and then. For long-term display, a display case is best — direct sunlight will yellow light-colored parts.

Beginners doing **brick flower bouquet building** hit three common problems. First, flowers that sit loose because the petal elements were not clipped in properly, so the whole thing falls apart at a touch. Second, stems that are too soft for a large head, so the flower droops. Third, too many colors with no focal point. The fixes: pin each petal layer with a connector, thicken the stem or run two bars side by side, and decide the main color before you build anything. More advanced builders can try a gradient bouquet, add transparent elements as dew drops, or build the bouquet and its vase together as one complete scene. A finished bouquet works as a decoration in an entryway, on a dining table or on a shelf.

## Care and display for your bouquet MOC

A brick bouquet will not wilt, but dust still needs dealing with. Display it in a covered display box, or brush the petals gently with a soft brush on a regular basis. Avoid long exposure to direct sunlight or some colors will fade. If you want better photos of the bouquet, shoot in natural light against a simple background, with the bouquet on a light-colored tabletop — the image quality goes up noticeably. A bouquet also makes a good gift: compared with real flowers, it lasts far longer, and the thought behind it feels more particular.
