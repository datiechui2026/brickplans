# Tunnel MOC Tutorial: Build a Realistic Tunnel Scene From Scratch

A tunnel is the most atmospheric part of any railway, road or mountain scene. The dark mouth of the bore, the light falling away into the interior, the mottled rock face — all of it lifts the quality of a build instantly. This **tunnel tutorial** walks you through it from the baseplate up, so you end up with a **tunnel MOC** that holds up to a long look.

## 1. Decide the structure first

Before you pick up a single brick, settle the size and shape of your **tunnel bricks**. Are you building a road tunnel, a rail tunnel, or a bore that cuts through a mountain? Is the portal a single opening or a twin bore? These choices decide how many parts you need.

The most reliable approach — and the one that shows up in almost every scenery tutorial — is to build the basic frame from ordinary bricks first, laying out the route the tunnel will take, and only then fill in the surfaces. Roughing out the path in plain brick lets you test the curve and the clearance before you commit expensive curved parts or detail work to it. It also makes it easy to pull a section apart when the proportions feel wrong.

## 2. Baseplate and arch: the core of the tunnel tutorial

The soul of a **tunnel MOC** is the arch. Build the portal vault from curved bricks, or from bricks that step inward one stud at a time, and you get a natural-looking curve instead of a flat-topped hole in a wall. The stepped method is slower but gives you control over the radius, which matters if the tunnel has to match a specific track curve or a road width.

The base has to be solid. Lay the floor bricks inside the tunnel first, then raise the walls on both sides, and crown the whole thing last. At every stage, leave enough strength in the assembly — a tunnel that is thin at the haunches will sag or deform after a few months on display, especially if it sits in a warm room. This is the point the **tunnel tutorial** keeps coming back to: the arch is what people look at, but the base is what keeps it standing.

Work in modules if the tunnel is long. Two or three short sections that join with technic pins are far easier to move, photograph and store than one rigid tube, and the joints disappear once the rock work is on top.

## 3. Detail and atmosphere: making it believable

With a good **tunnel brick** structure in place, detail decides whether the build reads as real. A few things that pay off quickly:

- **Rock texture:** mix several shades of grey across the rock face rather than one flat colour. Scatter dark grey and light bluish grey plates and slopes so the surface has variation the eye can follow.
- **Vegetation:** moss green and brown elements tucked into crevices and along the top edge sell the idea that the rock has been there a while.
- **Weathering at the portal:** add a few irregular stone elements around the rim of the opening to imitate natural erosion. Round tiles and small slopes work well here.
- **Layering:** build the rock face in two or three depths — a front skin, a recessed band, an overhang — so the wall casts its own small shadows.

Light matters even more than texture. Leave a gap for a light position inside the tunnel and run an LED in from the back, so the **tunnel MOC** goes from bright at the portal to dark deep inside. That gradient is what creates the sense of depth; a tunnel lit evenly from the front just looks like a painted box. Keep the LED wiring tucked under the floor bricks so it stays hidden, and check the colour temperature against the rest of your scene before you fix anything down.

## 4. Combining the tunnel with other scenery

Once you have worked through this **tunnel tutorial**, try combining the tunnel with rail track, a mountain mass or a lake to form a complete natural scene. A tunnel works as the passage and the transition in a layout, joining two functional areas that would otherwise sit awkwardly side by side — the visible station on one side, the hidden fiddle yard on the other. That connection is what gives a build a sense of narrative, and it is the standard pattern in scenery tutorials for a reason.

## Summary

Get three things right — the arch structure, the layered detail, and the light falloff — and your **tunnel MOC** moves from merely presentable to genuinely satisfying to look at.
