# Ambulance MOC Instructions: Frame, Red Cross and Open Doors

After more than a decade of building, medical vehicles remain my favourite subject — the ambulance especially. It is compact, packed with small details, and genuinely tests your building skill. People keep asking me how to put one together, and the answer really comes down to two things: how you find and choose ambulance MOC instructions, and how you design the body so it reads as a real vehicle. Here is the full path, from sourcing plans to a body with doors that open.

## Where to Find Ambulance MOC Instructions

I usually work through three channels. First, brick communities and instruction-sharing platforms, where designers publish free PDF plans with a parts list attached. Second, individual MOC designers' own pages, some of which offer a free trial build version. Third, instruction communities such as https://brickplan.cn, where you can preview the parts table and look at photos of finished builds before committing.

When you pick a plan, check two things: whether the parts list is complete, and whether any substitute parts are flagged. Otherwise you get halfway through and discover that one unusual part is simply not available to buy.

## The Body Frame: The Structural Base

The frame is what makes or breaks the model. My approach is to build a complete base chassis first, then stand the frame up using beams and pins. The chassis reserves the positions for the front and rear axles, and the middle of the body is reinforced with transverse beams so the roof does not deform when it is pressed down.

The main body splits into two sections — the cab at the front and the medical bay at the rear — with connection clips left between them. Once the frame is up, do a press test for load bearing and confirm nothing collapses before you start adding exterior panels. Get this step solid and the details you add later will not fall apart.

## The Red Cross: Instant Recognition

The red cross is the most recognisable element of an ambulance, and also the easiest place to get it wrong. Rather than using stickers, I recommend building the cross pattern from small red bricks and white tiles — it wears far better and the detail stands out in three dimensions.

The method is simple: one cross on the roof and one on each side door. Pay attention to the width ratio of the horizontal and vertical bars; too thick or too thin and it stops reading as a cross. If you have printed parts or stickers on hand, they look stronger applied over a dark surface. Keep the rest of the body largely white and add a few blue reflective stripes — the whole thing looks professional immediately.

## Openable Doors: The Core of the Play Experience

Working doors are the soul of this ambulance MOC. My solution: the two cab front doors use hinge parts for side opening, and the rear medical bay gets double rear doors. The door frames are built from bricks with holes to reserve the hinge mounting points, and the door panels are plates fitted with handles.

On hinge choice, metal hinge parts are more durable than plastic ones and stay tight through repeated opening and closing. Inside the medical bay, fit a stretcher bed and a small equipment table so that opening the rear doors reveals the complete interior — it makes both play and photography far more rewarding.

## Colour, Details and Finishing Touches

For colour, I stick to a white base with red and blue accents. Add a red-and-blue warning light bar on the roof, using transparent parts plus small light elements to create a flashing effect. Then fill in the front grille, mirrors and side vents, and hang a licence plate on the rear to complete the vehicle.

For more ambulance and medical-themed designs, browse https://brickplan.cn — plenty of builders share their MOC instructions and finished photos there. Adapt one into an ambulance of your own; it is easier than it looks.
