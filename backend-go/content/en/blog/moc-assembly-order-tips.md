# MOC Build Order Tips: Sort Parts, Build Skeleton First

When a new set of MOC instructions lands in your hands, the instinct is to start clicking bricks immediately. After a few years of building, I've become convinced that build order is the first thing that decides whether a project goes well. Get the sequence right and a mech that should take four or five hours wraps up in a little over two. Get it wrong and you'll hit a structural conflict halfway through, then tear everything apart to start again. This review pulls together what I've learned from assembling and disassembling several sets of instructions, and lays out the order that actually works.

## Sort Parts and Verify Numbers First

Whether you're working from a PDF or from official or third-party instructions, step one is always the same: sort the parts and check them against the numbered list. Official part lists usually give both colour IDs and part numbers, and third-party plans often include a homemade inventory of their own. I bag similar parts together by colour and size, then tick each line off the list one by one. Don't skip this. Missing or wrong parts are far easier to deal with the earlier you spot them — discovering halfway through a build that a key technic pin is absent is a very expensive kind of rework. Labelling bags by number while you sort means you barely have to dig through boxes later, and the efficiency gain is obvious.

## Skeleton First or Details First?

This is the classic argument in MOC building. From my own testing, the vast majority of large MOCs should follow a skeleton-first, details-later order: build the main frame from load-bearing beams, technic pins and axles, confirm the connection points are solid and symmetrical, then come back to add exterior decoration, stickers and finishing pieces. The big advantage of putting the skeleton up front is that it exposes hidden problems in the instructions early — an area that's too weak, or an axle that interferes with something else. Fixing that at an early stage costs almost nothing. Details first does let you see a finished-looking model quickly, but if those details cover a critical interface, you'll usually have to pull apart a whole section to get the skeleton in afterwards. Not worth it.

## Build in Layers, Module by Module

For architecture, street scenes or large vehicle MOCs, I strongly recommend breaking the instructions into layers or modules and completing each one before joining them together. After every layer, check that the mating surfaces are flat and that nothing is loose, then stack the next one on top. This keeps the load spread evenly through the structure and makes it easy to trace your way back through the instructions. If the plan is already divided into step bags, follow it exactly and don't reshuffle it for your own convenience. Counting the leftover parts for the current layer as you go is also a useful early warning that you've used too many or too few pieces.

## Common Order Mistakes and How Rework Damages Structure

There are three traps that catch most people. The first is skipping the inventory and building straight away, only to find a missing part mid-build and be stuck waiting. The second is rushing ahead with the exterior, so the skeleton and the shell end up fighting each other. The third is tipping out an entire bag of instructions and parts at once, which mixes everything together and actually slows you down. More important than any of these is the fact that rework damages structure far more than most builders expect. Repeatedly inserting and pulling technic pins loosens the connections, and pulling parts apart with too much force can leave stress marks or even cracks. That directly affects how solid the finished model feels and how it looks. Better to build a little slower in a steadier order than to rely on fixing things afterwards.

In the end, good MOC build order comes down to a simple formula: structure before appearance, layer by layer, verify before you build. Turn that sequence into a habit and you'll get it right the first time with far fewer detours.
