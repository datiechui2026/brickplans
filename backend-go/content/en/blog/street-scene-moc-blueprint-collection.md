# Street Scene MOC Instructions: The Complete Collection Guide

Street scenes are probably the most addictive subject in brick MOC building. Finish one small building and you immediately want to give it a neighbour; once the corner is standing, you start wondering whether a whole street is within reach. Plenty of builders' MOC careers begin with collecting a full set of street scene instructions. This guide draws on my own collecting and building experience to explain how to gather a street scene instruction library that is both complete and genuinely good.

## Where to Source a Full Set of Instructions

Street scene instructions are scattered across community forums and instruction sites, and quality varies wildly. My approach is to build the system first: decide whether you want a European-style side street, a Chinese old-town lane, or a modern city block, then collect by building type. Small shopfronts such as cafés, bookshops and florists, combined with larger structures like a town hall, bank or theatre, are what give a district its sense of layering. Brick communities such as https://brickplan.cn sort instructions by theme, which is far more efficient than digging through endless forum threads.

The trap is collecting one-off buildings that never sit together. Before you buy anything, sketch the street you are aiming for: how many corner buildings, how many mid-block shops, where the taller landmark goes. A corner building needs two finished facades, while a mid-block unit only needs one, and that difference should shape your shopping list from day one.

## How to Judge Whether a Set of Instructions Is Worth It

The biggest risk when collecting a full street scene set is ending up with a half-finished file. A few hard criteria tell you most of what you need to know. First, are the step diagrams clear and complete, with critical substructures broken down step by step? Second, is the parts list accurate? A missing or miscounted part will stall your build halfway through. Third, is the scale consistent? A street scene depends on everything standing on the same street, so neighbouring buildings at mismatched proportions look wrong immediately.

Prioritise instructions that other builders have actually built and reviewed, and do not judge from renders alone. A beautiful render can hide a fragile facade, an impossible connection, or a roof that collapses the moment you pick the model up. Look for photos of the finished build from several angles, and check whether the designer has published a parts count and a difficulty rating. If the instructions mix in rare or discontinued parts without warning, that is another red flag, because the parts list will be far more expensive than the sticker price suggests.

## Planning Colors and Budget Across the Street

How a street scene reads depends heavily on color. When planning a full set, give the whole street a dominant palette, such as warm brick walls under dark roofs, then let each building carry its own accent color. The result is coherent but not monotonous. Pick two or three core colors first, buy those bricks in bulk, and reserve the bright accents for details like awnings, signage and door frames.

On budget, a street scene is a classic case of small amounts adding up. A single building's parts count is modest, but a whole street multiplies the variety of parts enormously. My advice is to stock up on shared foundations first: baseplates, standard bricks for walls, and common plates and tiles for pavements. Buy special decorative parts per instruction set, one building at a time, and you avoid overspending on pieces you only need twice. Sorting your spare parts by color and type before you start also cuts build time noticeably.

## Build Order and Interlocking Details

Once the instructions are in hand, build order matters. Start with the ground floor foundation, then work upward level by level, checking structural stability on each building individually before moving on. Only when every building is finished should you lay the shared elements: pavement, street lamps, greenery, benches and signage. That is the step that turns separate buildings into one street.

Builders who care about playability can add openable doors and windows, or lighting modules so the whole street glows at night. Wire the lights before you close up the upper floors, because retrofitting them later means dismantling the model. Keep a small margin of space between buildings so you can lift one out without disturbing its neighbours, and standardise the depth of your pavement strip so future additions slot in without rework.

## Closing Notes

Collecting a full street scene instruction set is a hobby that deepens as you go: the files need to be precise, the palette needs to be consistent, and the budget needs planning ahead of purchase. If you want to find high-quality street scene instructions in one place, spend time in brick instruction communities such as https://brickplan.cn and file the good ones into your own district plan. A miniature town is closer than it looks.
