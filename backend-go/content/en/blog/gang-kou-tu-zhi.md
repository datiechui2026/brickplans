# Where to Find Port MOC Blueprints: A Full Build Guide

After more than a decade of building, the port is the theme I keep coming back to in city layouts. A container terminal, shore cranes, ships coming and going, plus a stretch of water — put it together and you have a large scene that actually tells a story. But finding blueprints for a port and building one both take some know-how: there are many modules, it eats up floor space, and the details are fiddly, so beginners often stall halfway. Here is everything I have learned about sourcing blueprints and putting a dock together.

## Where to Find Port Blueprints: Channels and Selection Tips

There are a few main places to get port blueprints: community blueprint libraries, builder forums and blueprint marketplaces. Communities like BrickPlans have the advantage of sorting blueprints clearly by module — containers, cranes and ships each have their own separate blueprints, so you can mix and match them into your own terminal. When choosing a blueprint I apply a few standards. First, check whether the parts list is complete; a port is a large scene and the piece count often runs into the thousands, so a missing list is genuinely painful. Second, check whether it supports modular building — ideally it can be split into a container zone, a loading zone and a water zone. Third, look at feedback from builders who have actually finished it, since that makes it obvious whether the build sequence flows well.

## Modular Design: Split the Port into Sections First

For a port scene I strongly recommend going modular. Divide the whole dock into four modules — the container yard, the shore crane area, the dock platform and the water surface — fix each one to its own baseplate, and join them at the end. The benefit is that it is easy to move and easy to upgrade: if you want to add a ship or a warehouse, you just build one extra module rather than tearing everything down. Align the baseplates with studs and reinforce the seams with technic pins, and the whole thing stays solid enough that kids can play with it without it falling apart.

## Containers and Cranes: The Visual Stars of the Port

Containers are the soul of a port. A standard container can be built as a block from small bricks and then finished with printed parts, which makes producing them in bulk painless. Keep the colours varied — red, blue, green and white mixed together is what makes it read as a real terminal. Cranes are the finishing touch: for a ship-to-shore gantry crane I suggest a twin-column structure with a crossbeam, a slide rail mounted on the beam, and a gear rack driving the trolley back and forth; combined with a hoist line you get a loading and unloading action, and playability jumps immediately. This section is a little more complex, so practise with an existing blueprint before modifying it yourself — there is plenty of room for your own changes.

## Ships and Water Structure: Bringing the Scene to Life

Ships are the bonus element of a port scene. Cargo ships, tugboats and pilot boats each have their own character. Build a cargo ship in three sections — hull, deck and wheelhouse — and keep the superstructure small so the proportions read correctly. Tugboats are small but detail-heavy, which makes them good accents. For the water surface, lay transparent light-blue bricks as the base, add white wave studs to create a sense of swell, then dot in a breakwater and a lighthouse and the dock instantly gains depth. Remember to offset the water baseplate and the dock platform in height, which looks more natural and gives your photos more depth.

## Pitfalls and Blueprint Resources

A few warnings to finish. A port scene has very mixed parts, so sort everything into boxes by colour and purpose before you start. Large baseplates tend to warp, so press them flat under something heavy before building. Tall parts like cranes need attention to their centre of gravity — weight the base a little more so it stays stable and does not topple when a child knocks it. Following this modular workflow, a complete port blueprint scene takes shape in two to three weeks.
