# Brick Subway MOC Blueprints: Themes, Techniques, Where to Find

After ten years of building, I have put together trains and trucks, but nothing grabs me like subway models. Steam locomotives have that retro romance, while a metro carries the order of a modern city: a streamlined nose, a silver-grey body, hurried crowds on the platform. Line a few cars up on a shelf and you get the feeling of a city that never stops running. This guide focuses on brick subway MOC blueprints, from picking a theme to building techniques to where to find plans, so anyone thinking of starting has something practical to work from.

## What themes do subway MOCs cover

Subway MOC subjects are more varied than most people expect, and they fall into three groups.

The first is the modern commuter metro, which is the mainstream direction. Reference cars are usually the classic looks of Chinese A and B type cars, Japanese commuter trains or European city rail, with the emphasis on a streamlined nose and a colored band running along the windows. Plenty of builders like to make a long three-car consist, which gives the model real presence.

The second is the retro tram. Strictly speaking it is not a subway, but it is rail transit on the same track system, so it often gets folded into subway scenes. Its wood-tone color scheme and articulated body are instantly recognizable, and it is actually easier to build, which makes it a good warm-up for beginners.

The third is the extended scene, meaning subway plus everything around it. Drop the brick subway MOC blueprints into a full setting: an underground platform, ticket gates, station signs, a tunnel cross-section, a transfer corridor. Then you are not just building a train, you are building a slice of a city. The most striking build I have seen turned an entire line into a picture hanging on a wall, with the train still able to run along the track. It was genuinely impressive.

## Building the car body, platform and track

Start with the car body. Subway cars want to look square and long, and the trick is to build an equal-width, equal-height body frame from standard bricks first, then skin the outside with tiles and curved slopes to pull the corners into a rounded shape. Line up transparent parts as a continuous band for the windows, transition the nose with slope bricks or rounded parts, and add a black-on-white destination sign, and the car reads as a metro immediately. Make sure the articulation between cars keeps some play in it, or the consist will come apart on curves.

The platform comes next, and it is the heart of a subway scene. What matters most is the height difference: the platform surface should sit one or two bricks above the rail surface. Lay dark grey plates as the floor, build the safety line from light grey bricks, then add ticket gates, light boxes and waiting minifigures, and the picture comes alive at once. For the track, use standard straight track for the straights and curve track to set your turning radius, then raise the ground with black or dark blue plates to suggest ballast, which makes the whole thing sit together better.

## Which special parts you need

A few part groups matter more than others for subway MOCs, so check your parts pool before you go looking for plans.

- **Transparent window parts**, such as 1x4 and 1x6 transparent bricks and curved windscreen pieces. These decide how much the model reads as glass.
- **Curved slopes and wedge slopes.** Nose fairings and rounded body corners both depend on them to close the shape.
- **Articulation and wheel-steering structure parts.** Long consists need these to get around curves.
- **Light parts and stickers.** Headlights and route-map stickers add a lot of accuracy for very little effort.

If your parts pool is thin on these, favor brick subway MOC blueprints that are mostly standard bricks, so you are not hunting everywhere for a few rare pieces.

## Where to find blueprints

When it comes to finding brick subway MOC blueprints, the channels come down to three kinds. First, overseas blueprint sites: huge volume, but mostly in English and usually designed around parts from foreign sets. Second, builds shared by players on various platforms: authentic, but uneven in quality and often just loose images. Third, dedicated blueprint communities: clear categories, parts lists and build instructions, which makes them the best option if you actually want to build from the plan.

My own experience is to put the community that has all three, clear categories, a parts list and player reviews, at the top of the list. It saves a lot of dead ends. If you are thinking about getting into subway MOCs, take a look at brickplan.cn (https://brickplan.cn). The site has collected a good number of subway MOCs, from single commuter cars to complete platform scenes, and you can check parts lists and feedback from players who have built them, which helps you pick the one that is genuinely worth your time.
