# Brick Alpaca MOC: SNOT Fur, Color & Cute Face Guide

Short version first: an alpaca MOC gets its fleece look from SNOT combined with slope pieces, tan paired with cream white holds up best over time, and the long neck ratio plus the eyes are what sell the character.

Anyone who builds animal MOCs knows the alpaca is a forgiving subject with a big payoff: rounded body, long neck, goofy expression, and a natural fluffy charm baked in. But recreating that soft, puffy texture with ordinary stacked bricks alone won't get you there. This breakdown of a brick alpaca MOC walks through the body, neck, head and legs step by step so you can apply the approach to your own build.

## 1. Body and Neck: Building Fleece with SNOT and Slopes

The fleece is the alpaca's whole personality. My rule: don't try to replicate individual strands of wool brick by brick. Instead, alternate **slope pieces** with **inverted slopes** so the surface picks up natural rises and dips. Start by using SNOT (Studs Not On Top, i.e. sideways building) to hang plates off both flanks of the body, then attach curved slope elements onto those side-facing planes. The body ends up round and three-dimensional rather than a boxy brick cube.

The neck works the same way. Offset several groups of 1x1 round-corner bricks as you stack upward, and use wedge pieces connected on the sides to taper gradually, so the long neck flows naturally from body to head. Good instructions tend to emphasize the curve in this section — leave yourself two or three extra layers of internal core structure so you can adjust the shape later without rebuilding.

## 2. Color Scheme: Tan and Cream White Ages Best

The classic alpaca palette comes down to **tan with white/cream**. Use tan for the main body, white for the belly, the underside of the neck and the face, and the layers separate naturally. A small amount of dark brown on ear tips, hooves and around the eye sockets outlines the silhouette and keeps the overall tone from looking flat.

If you want the popular internet-meme alpaca look, grey-and-white or a touch of milk-coffee brown also work — but keep the dominant color above roughly 60% of the build or it starts to look busy. Once the palette is locked, buy the parts in a single batch where possible to avoid color mismatch between production runs.

## 3. Long Neck and Head Proportions: The Key to the Goofy Look

Alpaca proportions are fussier than they appear. The head should be on the small side, the neck long and tilted slightly forward — that's what produces the elegant-but-dopey character. Build the head core from 1x2 bricks and wrap it in smooth tiles so the cheeks read as full and rounded. A common move in brick alpaca MOC designs is a small stepped taper under the jaw; seen from the side, the face gains depth instead of looking like a flat panel.

## 4. Cute Expression: Eyes, Ears and Mouth

The expression is the soul of the build. For eyes, use 1x1 round printed tiles or sticker tiles, placed slightly high and slightly forward, and add light-colored brow pieces for alertness. Push two 1x1 wedge pieces into the top of the head at an angle, tipped slightly outward, and the goofiness appears instantly. Keep the mouth simple: a shallow dark dot at the lower edge of the white face, or a single inverted 1x1 slope to form a faint upturned mouth line. Both read well.

## 5. Leg Structure: Four Stable, Good-Looking "Fur Legs"

Alpaca legs are thin, and the contrast against the puffy body is exactly what makes them cute. Use 1x1 round bars or cylindrical pieces with half-round bumps for the lower legs, then wrap a ring of slope pieces around them as cuffs — this mimics leg fleece and hides the joint gaps. Spread the four contact points outward as far as you can for stable support, and don't let the center of gravity sit under that tall neck. This matters especially if the model will be on display long term.

## 6. Display and Photography Tips

Once it's finished, give it a simple light-colored display base or a small grass plate — the fleece colors will look cleaner against it. Shoot with soft side lighting to accent the slope-built undulations and bring out the layered texture. If you plan to share it with the community, take several 45-degree close-ups; fidelity to the brick alpaca MOC design and the expression details are usually what people look at most.

Hopefully this gives you enough to build your own little alpaca on top of whatever instructions you're working from. Start modifying — the fluffy joy is right there between the bricks.

## FAQ

**Q: How do you actually build the fluffy texture on a brick alpaca MOC?**
A: Alternate slope and inverted slope pieces. First hang plates off both sides of the body with SNOT, then attach curved slope elements to those planes, and the surface picks up natural fleece-like rises and dips.

**Q: What color scheme looks best on an alpaca MOC?**
A: Tan with cream white. Tan for the main body, white for the belly and face, dark brown accents on ear tips and hooves, and keep the dominant color above roughly 60%.

**Q: Which parts make stable alpaca legs?**
A: Use 1x1 round bars or cylindrical pieces with half-round bumps for the lower legs, wrap slope pieces around them as cuffs, and spread the four contact points wide so the tall neck doesn't pull the center of gravity off balance.

**Q: What mistakes do beginners make most often?**
A: Trying to replicate individual strands of wool instead of letting alternating slopes create the texture, and buying main-color parts across different batches, where color mismatch can ruin the whole look.
