# LEGO Hinge Parts Guide: Types and MOC Techniques

In brick building, hinges are the quiet workhorses nobody praises. The moment your MOC needs a door that swings, a wing that folds, a head that turns or a joint that bends, a hinge is what turns a static pile of bricks into something that moves. This guide walks through the part families, the design techniques that get the most out of them, and a few real build cases you can copy.

## Meet the hinge part family

LEGO hinges fall roughly into three groups, each suited to different jobs.

**Classic hinge (hinge brick)**: a rotating part with a stud-topped pin that seats into a slotted receiver. You will find it in 1x2 and 1x4 sizes, and it turns through 90 or 180 degrees. Simple, cheap and the most widely used entry-level hinge.

**Knuckle hinge (hinge cylinder / ball joint)**: the part builders call a "ball joint." A ball head sits inside a slotted clip and rotates on multiple axes. Common in Technic-style models and articulated MOCs, it gives you far more freedom when posing limbs and heads.

**Technic hinge**: built from Technic pins and cross axles, it comes in two flavours, the gear-meshing type and the friction type. These handle higher torque, so they suit folding doors, tipper truck beds and other load-bearing structures.

## Hinge techniques for MOC design

Knowing the parts is only half of it. The rest is knowing how to make them earn their place.

**Use friction to your advantage**: some hinges carry teeth or a rubber friction pad, which lets them hold at any angle. When you design a mechanical arm or a turret, pick this type and the structure will stay in position without any extra bracing.

**Chain multiple hinges together**: one hinge gives you a single axis of rotation. For more complex folding motion, run several in series. An aircraft's folding wing, for instance, only needs two or three hinge segments to go from stowed to deployed.

**Hide them and clean them up**: exposed hinges spoil the look of a finished model. Layer plates, bricks or stickers over the outside face so the mechanism stays functional without breaking the silhouette.

**Balance the load and mirror the layout**: a large door panel or canopy hanging from a single hinge point will sag. Place hinges symmetrically on both sides and reinforce around the pivot so repeated opening and closing does not loosen the assembly over time.

## Common connection methods and examples

**Example 1: opening container doors.** Two pairs of classic hinges, fitted at the top and bottom of the door panels, give you a convincing double-door effect. This shows up constantly on truck, building and military MOCs.

**Example 2: folding sails or ladders.** Use knuckle hinges to link each section of sail or ladder rung. The sections collapse for storage and still line up straight when extended, a standard trick on ship and fire engine builds.

**Example 3: articulated animals and mechs.** Ball joint hinges at the legs and neck let a model strike natural, lively poses, which lifts both playability and accuracy.

## Advanced: pairing hinges with gears

Link a hinge to a gear system and rotation stops being something you do with your fingers and becomes mechanically driven. Fit a geared hinge at the base of a turret, connect a Technic axle through to a hand crank, and the barrel turns remotely. This is a favourite challenge among more experienced MOC builders.

Once you have these hinge techniques down, your model stops being a static sculpture and becomes something with life and interaction in it. Try things, take things apart, rebuild them, and you will develop your own feel for when a hinge is the right answer.
