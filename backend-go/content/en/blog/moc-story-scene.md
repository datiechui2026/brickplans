# How to Build a MOC Story Scene: A 6-Step Beginner Guide

The short version: a MOC story scene is not about piling on detail, it is about writing the story first and building the stage second. Follow six steps — sum up the plot, break it into frames, pick a viewpoint, build the base, place the minifigures, then tune the light — and a 20 cm square desktop scene can tell a complete episode for roughly 80-150 CNY in parts.

What separates a story scene from an ordinary MOC is narrative: a viewer has to grasp who, where and what happened within three seconds. The top-scoring entries in the BrickPlan story-scene category almost all follow the same method. Here it is, step by step.

Step 1: Write the story summary. Use one sentence built from character + place + conflict. For example: a delivery rider brings a lighthouse keeper a birthday cake in a downpour. The more specific the summary, the more recognisable the scene. Keep it to about 20 words; if you cannot write it in one sentence, pick a different subject.

Step 2: Break it into frames. Split the sentence into 3-5 key moments and choose the one with the most tension as your main scene. In the example above you could pick the instant the cake is blown away and both people burst out laughing, which is far more dramatic than a calm, successful delivery.

Step 3: Choose viewpoint and composition. For a desktop scene, a 45-degree downward angle or eye level works best; place the subject on one of the intersections of the rule-of-thirds grid. Foreground props hint at the environment, the midground holds the main figures, and 1-2 large plates in the background establish the wider setting. The clearer the layers, the clearer the story reads.

Step 4: Build the stage base. Use a baseplate plus terrain pieces 2-4 cm tall to create height differences, and whenever a step is over 3 cm high, add a slope as a transition. For a 20 cm x 20 cm desktop scene, keep the base to about 150-300 pieces and save your budget for figures and props.

Step 5: Place the figures and set the poses. Minifigures are the soul of the narrative: one running figure with a leaning torso beats ten standing ones. In a two-person scene, have their eyes meet at a distance of 3-5 cm and the image instantly gains a sense of conversation. Expression heads and handheld props such as an umbrella, a cake or a letter are your cheapest high-value narrative tools.

Step 6: Tune the lighting and details. Warm light elements suggest dusk or a desk lamp, while cool tones suit a rainy night. Details follow the three-second rule: only what a viewer can catch in three seconds counts, and everything else is noise. Finish by shooting nine photos — 1 full view, 4 at 45 degrees, 4 detail close-ups — and lay them out in that order when you post to BrickPlan.

Time and cost reference: a complete story scene takes about 6-10 hours to build and roughly 80-150 CNY in parts, excluding minifigures. The base accounts for about 20%, the structure 30%, and props and details 50%. To keep costs down, reuse baseplates and terrain pieces from old sets first; that saves around 30%.

## FAQ

**Can I build a good MOC story scene with no writing background?**
Yes. You do not need a full script — the formula who + where + what happened is enough to start, for example an astronaut watering plants on Mars. Simple, clear stories are often easier to make work.

**How is a MOC story scene different from a normal street scene MOC?**
A street-scene MOC is about recreating a building; a story scene is about conveying a plot. Story scenes need posed figures, props and lighting working together, while a street scene is a static environment with no readable-in-three-seconds requirement.

**What does a MOC story scene cost?**
A desktop-size scene of around 20 cm runs about 80-150 CNY in parts; a large scene runs 300-500 CNY. Minifigures and special props take the biggest share, so do a trial build with parts you already own and only order the gaps once the composition is settled.

**How do I give a MOC story scene more atmosphere?**
Three moves work best: use light elements to contrast warm and cool tones, use height differences in the base to create spatial layers, and aim the figure's pose at the heart of the story. Do all three and even a 200-piece scene gains a level of mood.

**Is a MOC story scene worth sharing with the community?**
Very much so. Work with a plot invites discussion, and BrickPlan's story-scene category runs themed prompts every week. Post it with a one-line summary and a note on the frame you chose; interaction typically runs about 50% higher than for pure architecture builds.
