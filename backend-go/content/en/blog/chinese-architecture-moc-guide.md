# Chinese Architecture MOC: Core Structural Units Explained

Short version first: the soul of a Chinese building lives in its structural units. Keep the eaves flat on the inside and lifted at the corners, stagger the bracket sets layer by layer, and pair grey brick and grey tile with vermilion columns — hold those three lines and you are most of the way there.

Plenty of builders finish an entire courtyard complex and still feel it "doesn't read as Chinese". The problem is rarely the overall proportions; it is the detail units. What carries the character of traditional Chinese architecture is a fixed vocabulary of parts — flying eaves, bracket sets, gate towers, lattice windows — and each one has its own building logic. This guide takes them one at a time.

## 1. Flying Eaves and Upturned Corners: Corner Plates Plus Slope Bricks

The upturned eave is the most recognisable symbol of a Chinese roof, and the easiest place for a Chinese architecture MOC to fall apart. The core method is **corner plates as the base, slope bricks as the edge**: at each of the four roof corners, start with 1x1 corner plates to build the outward overhang and the step down, then stack 2x3 slope bricks layer by layer, moving from shallow to steep so the line sweeps upward into the upturn.

To make the tip livelier, add a studded special element at the outermost point as the "flip", and close the gaps with small slope bricks. The rule of thumb: flat inside, lifted outside, each layer smaller than the one below. Get the joints clean and the roof instantly gains depth.

## 2. Bracket Sets in Brick: Stacking and Offsetting Are the Whole Trick

Dougong brackets are what carries the load from roof to column in a traditional structure, and the brick version depends on **stacking and offsetting**. Use 1x1 grooved elements as the "blocks" and 2x2 plates as the "arms", deliberately offset the upper layer by half a stud, then extend sideways — the result reads as tiers cantilevered outward and interlocking in both directions.

Don't chase an exact historical bracket profile. A Chinese architecture MOC is going for the spirit, not the survey drawing. Two or three offset layers are enough, and dark brown or natural wood tone keeps the brackets distinct from the red columns. Leaving a dark shadow gap at the bracket eyes adds a lot of depth.

## 3. Gate Towers and Spirit Screens: The Face of the Building

The gate tower is the face of a Chinese building, and the spirit screen in front of it is a key unit for both feng shui and decoration. For the gate tower, two sets of columns plus a cross beam build the matched door frame, capped with a small hip-and-gable roof that echoes the main roof.

Build the spirit screen as a standalone vignette: smooth tiles for the face, a round or diamond motif set into the centre (a printed element or sticker can carry the auspicious pattern), and a brick-coloured border all the way round. It does not need to be big — two or three bricks thick is plenty — and standing it directly opposite the main gate gives the scene immediate spatial depth.

## 4. Lattice Windows and Railings: The Details That Sell It

Lattice windows test your patience and reward it. The usual approach in a Chinese architecture MOC is 1x1 openwork elements, thin strip plates and cross pieces assembled into a diamond lattice or meander pattern, with transparent elements behind as glazing. Outline the frame in a dark colour so it contrasts sharply against a white wall.

Railings are all about **spacing**: run 1x2 fence elements as a series of posts, top each post with a round dot element, and keep the gaps even. Wrap a courtyard corridor in this kind of railing and the Chinese feel jumps immediately.

## 5. Grey Brick, Grey Tile, Red Columns: The Palette

Chinese architecture is highly consistent in colour, and a MOC only has to hold one line — **grey brick, grey tile, vermilion columns** — to stay coherent. Roofs take dark grey tile elements throughout; walls use light grey or mid grey laid in an offset pattern to suggest grey brick; columns, beams, doors and window frames all go red to lift the composition.

A little off-white is fine as wall blank space so the building does not turn into a grey blob. Lay a dark grey baseplate for the ground, then pave it with light grey floor tiles: clear separation, and it reads clean from any angle.

## Final Thoughts

Master these five structural units and your Chinese architecture MOC moves from "looks like a house" to "has presence". Practise on the small parts first, then combine them into a full courtyard — the beauty of the style lives entirely in the details. ## FAQ

**Q: How do I build upturned flying eaves in a Chinese architecture MOC?**
A: Corner plates as the base, slope bricks as the edge. Use 1x1 corner plates at the four roof corners to create the overhang and the step, then stack 2x3 slope bricks from shallow to steep so the line sweeps upward. The rule is flat inside, lifted outside, each layer smaller than the one below.

**Q: How do you reproduce dougong brackets in brick?**
A: Stack and offset. 1x1 grooved elements form the blocks, 2x2 plates form the arms; offset each layer by half a stud and extend sideways to get the tiered cantilever. Two or three layers is enough, and dark brown or natural wood tone works best.

**Q: Which parts work best for lattice windows?**
A: 1x1 openwork elements, thin strip plates and cross pieces make the diamond lattice or meander pattern, with transparent elements behind as glazing. Frame them in a dark colour against a white wall. For railings, line up 1x2 fence elements and top them with round dot elements.

**Q: How should I set the colour scheme?**
A: Hold grey brick, grey tile and vermilion columns. Dark grey tile elements on every roof, light or mid grey walls in an offset pattern to mimic grey brick, red on all columns, beams, doors and windows, and a little off-white for blank wall.
