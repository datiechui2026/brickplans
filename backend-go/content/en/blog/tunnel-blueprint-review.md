# Tunnel MOC Blueprint Review: Arches, Portals, Lighting, Terrain

Tunnels are a theme plenty of brick builders love and dread at the same time. What they love is the built-in sense of depth, the way a tunnel ties a city, a rail line and a mountain view into one continuous story. What they dread is monotony: get it wrong and you have assembled a hollow concrete pipe. I lined up several **tunnel blueprints** side by side and took them apart across four dimensions, structure, portals, lighting and terrain integration, to work out which tunnel MOCs are actually worth building.

## Tunnel Structure: Arch vs. Straight Wall

The first dividing line in any tunnel blueprint is the cross-section. There are two mainstream approaches. The first is an arched profile, where curved slopes and wedge pieces close off a rounded crown that sits close to a real tunnel's outline, though it demands tighter interlocking between courses. The second is straight walls plus a top plate: a rectangular section built from standard bricks, the easiest and most rigid option, but it gives up the curvature that makes a tunnel read as a tunnel. Across the blueprints I reviewed, the strong ones almost all go hybrid: arch for the main run so the effect lands, then straight walls where the tunnel meets the terrain so the difficulty drops at the joins. Also watch how the joints are staggered in the crown. If every course lines up perfectly, the crown tends to collapse when you move the model; good instructions call out exactly where to offset the seams.

## Portal Treatment: The Tunnel's Front Door

The portal is where a tunnel looks its best, and where it most often falls apart. Blueprints at the mid-to-upper level put real effort in here: a wedge-shaped stone wall with decorative moulding above it to suggest a lintel, slopes on both sides flaring outward into a bell-mouth so the perspective feels right. Some go further and place track spikes, signal lights or even a small attendant hut at the opening, turning "a hole" into "a scene". Weaker blueprints, by contrast, simply truncate the arch with no transition pieces at all, as if someone sliced it off. When you are choosing what to build, check first whether the portal has its own transition structure. It is the fastest single test of how much care went into a blueprint.

## Lighting: The Soul of Tunnel Atmosphere

The inside of a tunnel is dark by nature, which makes lighting the deciding factor for atmosphere. Blueprints that understand light reserve a channel for a light strip or trans-clear bricks in the crown, and pair it with dark grey interior walls to create contrast between lit and unlit surfaces. Railway tunnels go further, spacing signal lights along the side walls to reproduce realistic clearance lighting. Builders who like movement can cut a slot in the crown and run an LED strip through it, and many blueprints already leave the routing space for the wiring. One warning: lighting parts push the cost up noticeably. If the model is strictly for static display, a design that works with or without lights is the more flexible buy.

## Fitting Into Terrain and Display Tips

A tunnel's biggest advantage is that it joins two worlds: highway on this side of the mountain, mining country on the other. My favourite of the whole batch embedded the tunnel into a rolling brick mountain range, with foliage plates across the crown, a stream bridge and a lookout tower, so that from above it reads as pure landscape. For display, give the tunnel a baseplate with enough depth and leave two or three studs of buffer at the front and back; otherwise a vehicle enters the bore and immediately leaves the frame. If you have the space, run it along a wall and use the longitudinal perspective to emphasise how deep it goes. Mine-style tunnels are a different mood entirely: swap to warm-toned interior walls, add a mine cart and some minifigures, and the atmosphere changes at once.

## Final Thoughts

A tunnel blueprint worth recommending should hit four points: reliable structure, a portal with a real transition, lighting you can extend, and terrain you can blend it into. What you are building is not a pipe but a stretch of light and shadow that keeps a scene moving, and it deserves the care.
