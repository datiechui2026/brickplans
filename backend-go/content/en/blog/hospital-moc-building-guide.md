# Brick Hospital MOC: ER Entrance, Wards and Operating Room

The hospital is the building that needs the most sense of order in a street-scene town. It is not romantic like a flower shop and not flashy like a cinema, but a **brick hospital MOC** with the right details makes the whole street read as complete and believable. This tutorial breaks the build down piece by piece, from the emergency entrance and the wards to the operating room and the rooftop cross, so you can put together a mini hospital that looks like a hospital from the outside in.

## 1. Facade and Emergency Entrance: It Has to Read as a Hospital at First Glance

A hospital facade gets its identity from three things: a white main tone, long horizontal windows, and an unmistakable emergency entrance. Use off-white or light gray basic bricks for the walls and run a dark belt line between floors to get that clean, clinical look. The entrance is where the effort pays off: the emergency entrance should be an **accessible route with a ramp**, built from slope plates to form a gentle incline, ending in a sliding door (a transparent glass door panel plus sliding rail parts). Mount an indicator light box above the door along with an "Emergency" sign. Park an ambulance outside and the busy atmosphere of a working hospital appears immediately.

## 2. The Ward Area: Beds, Nightstands and IV Stands

Wards are the main event inside a hospital. The standard approach is two to three patient rooms per floor, each holding one bed: build the frame from light-colored plates and bricks, use white curved pieces for the mattress, and make the nightstand from 1x2 dark brown bricks with a small cup and a vase of flowers on top. Above the bed, rig an IV stand: a thin round bar part with a hook, holding a small transparent round piece as the IV bag. It is a tiny detail, but it is the soul of the room. Add a bed number plate on the wall (a printed part or a sticker) and a plant on the windowsill, and the ward suddenly feels warm. In the corridor, lay a center line of dark floor tiles to give the interior more depth.

## 3. The Operating Room: The Surgical Light Is the Star

The operating room is often what makes a hospital MOC really stand out. It needs a sterile mood: white bricks overall with pale green or pale blue accents, and an operating table in the middle (a white flat plate with slightly raised side plates). On top, install a **surgical light** by stacking transparent round pieces onto bendable joint parts so it hangs down from above, then clip a few white round studs into the head to suggest the lamps. Once the surgical light is in, the room instantly looks professional. Add an instrument table, an ECG monitor (a dark plate with a round stud screen) and a rotatable floor lamp beside the table, then leave a ring of light troughs around the ceiling. The realism of the whole room jumps at once.

## 4. Rooftop Cross and Interior Circulation

The rooftop sign is often overlooked, yet it is the key to someone in the distance recognizing the building as a hospital. Stand a small tower frame or a large plate in the center of the roof and build a **red cross** horizontally with SNOT technique, then add a ring of small light pieces at the four corners. If you are building a corner hospital, swap the cross for three-dimensional pieces with slopes so the sign can be seen from two directions. For circulation, place the emergency entrance on the ground floor facing the street, put the wards on the quiet inner side, and put the operating room on the second floor toward the rear. Then the routes for minifigures and "patients" are obvious, and the building actually works.

## 5. Build Order and Common Pitfalls

Recommended order: baseplate and floor zoning, then wards and beds, then the operating room and surgical light, then the emergency entrance and ramp, then the facade and long windows, then the rooftop cross and lighting runs. Three frequent failure points: first, building only the facade and no interior, so the roof comes off and there is nothing inside; second, making the emergency entrance a set of ordinary steps with no accessible ramp, which badly undercuts the hospital feel; third, laying the cross flat against the roof, where nobody can see it from a distance. Remember these three rules, **interior before exterior, a ramped entrance, and a three-dimensional cross**, and your **brick hospital MOC** will hold its own in the town.

Once the single building is done, try joining it with an outpatient block and an ambulance station to form a whole medical street corner, complete with an ambulance and paramedic minifigures, and the scene comes alive.
