# Brick Mecha Building Guide: Joints, Proportions, and Color

The short version: the first lesson in mecha building is the joints. Small-scale builds use ball joints for flexibility, while mid- and large-scale builds use hinge parts for load bearing. Then comes proportion design, so the model can both stand up and hold a pose.

## Start With the Joint Problem

Mecha are one of the most structurally demanding subjects in brick MOC building, because the model has to satisfy two things at once: standing up and holding a pose. The first lesson of brick mecha building is the joint. There are three common joint approaches. Ball joints are flexible but have limited load capacity, so they suit small-scale mecha. Hinge parts are structurally stable and produce clear posing angles, making them the mainstay of mid-scale mecha. Technic friction pins combined with beams give the strongest load bearing, suited to large-scale and heavy mecha. For a first mecha, start with a combination of ball joints and hinges: use load-bearing hinges or friction pins at the shoulders and hips, and ball joints at the wrists and ankles, balancing articulation and stability.

## Proportion Design: The Key to Whether a Mecha Looks Good

In brick mecha building, proportion decides success or failure. Common mecha proportions include the humanoid ratio (head-to-body ratio of 1:6 to 1:7) and the Gundam-style ratio (1:8 to 1:9, which looks more slender). Before designing, fix the size of the head first, then work backward to the lengths of the torso and limbs. One practical trick: build a skeleton draft from your smallest parts to lock in segment lengths and joint positions, then add armor plating on top of that skeleton. Leg proportions matter most; the thigh, lower leg and foot should be roughly 3:3:1.5. A knee placed too low makes the mecha look short and stocky, while too high makes it unstable. Shoulder width is usually about 2.5 times the head width; shoulders that are too narrow look weak.

## Torso and Core Structure

The torso is the hub that connects everything in brick mecha building. The inside of the torso needs a main frame built from beams and pins, with armor plating attached on the outside. The cockpit is a highlight of many mecha: use transparent parts for the canopy and place a micro figure or pilot inside. If you want an articulated waist, connect the upper and lower body with hinges or ball parts, but note that waist articulation costs some stability, so combat poses and standing poses both need to work. The shoulder mounts on both sides of the torso must be strong, because a mechanical arm plus a weapon carries real weight, and a weak mount will sag. It is also worth reserving a connection point in the core so you can add a backpack or flight wings later.

## Limbs: Balancing Articulation and Looks

The limbs are the biggest workload in brick mecha building. Arms usually get three joints at the shoulder, elbow and wrist, with hinge parts at the elbow for clear bending angles; legs get three joints at the hip, knee and ankle, with a double-hinge structure at the knee for better load bearing. Armor coverage must leave room for the joints to move. Do not seal the joints completely, or the armor will block them when you pose the model. Feet should be wide, large and flat, with ground-contact parts or claw-shaped parts for grip; this is the key to a mecha standing firmly. For weapon systems such as beam sabers and cannon barrels, combine transparent parts with Technic parts and make them plug-in so you can swap loadouts easily.

## Color and Finish Planning

The color scheme of a brick mecha build directly affects how good it looks in the end. The classic approach is a main color plus a secondary color plus an accent color, with the main color at 60 percent, the secondary at 30 percent and the accent at 10 percent. Gundam-style builds often use white, blue, red and yellow, while realistic mecha look more grounded in gray, military green and dark blue. Watch the light-dark contrast when choosing colors; a large area of one shade looks flat, so use dark parts to break up color at joints and intakes. Stickers and printed parts greatly boost detail, but do not overdo it or the model looks busy. For a quick quality lift, add a simple stand or base and set the mecha in a flying or sword-wielding pose; the build instantly reads as more finished.

## Common Beginner Mistakes and Next Steps

Beginners at brick mecha building make three common mistakes. First, a weak skeleton with armor piled straight on, so the model falls apart at a touch. Second, off proportions, with a big head and small body or legs that are too short. Third, joints that cannot carry the load, so the model collapses as soon as you pose it. The fixes: build the skeleton before the armor, sketch the proportions before you start, and use load-bearing parts at the joints. More advanced builders can try transformable structures such as a jet that becomes a mecha, a half-mechanical design with exposed frame, or a full weapon set and display base for the mecha. Start with a small-scale practice build and work your way up to your first mecha MOC that both stands and poses.

## FAQ

**Q: What do I do if my brick mecha will not stand up?**
A: The problem is usually joint load bearing. Small-scale builds can use ball joints for flexibility; mid- and large-scale builds should switch to hinge parts plus axle sleeves for load bearing. The knee and hip joints need two-point support, and with the center of gravity between the feet, the model will not fall over.

**Q: How do I design mecha proportions that look good?**
A: Use human proportions as a reference: a head-to-body ratio of about 1:7 to 1:8, shoulder width about 3 times the head width, and thighs slightly longer than lower legs. Keep the torso short rather than long and the limbs thick rather than thin, and the mecha will look powerful.

**Q: What is the most common beginner mistake in mecha building?**
A: Three of them: chasing articulation until the model cannot stand, limbs too thin to support the torso, and more than three colors making the build look messy. Get it standing with good proportions first, then add articulation and detail step by step.

**Q: How do I choose a mecha color scheme?**
A: Pick one main, one secondary and an accent: main color at 70 percent (dark gray, military green or white), secondary at 30 percent (black or blue), and a small amount of accent (red, yellow or metallic). Keep joints and frame in a consistent dark gray for a more unified look.
