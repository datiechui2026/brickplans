# Brick Bridge MOC Guide: Piers, Arches and Cable-Stays

A brick bridge succeeds or fails on its mechanics, not its styling. Nail three principles — dense piers, an arch that works in compression, and a reinforced deck — and plain standard 2x4 bricks will span 60 cm while carrying more than 2 kg. Here is the build broken down step by step.

## Step 1: Plan the Dimensions and Scale

Before you touch a brick, fix three numbers: total span, deck width and clearance underneath. Using the most popular tutorial on BrickPlan as a reference, the recommended proportions are a span of 48–60 cm, a deck width of 8–10 studs (roughly 6.4–8 cm) and at least 12 bricks of clearance so a small boat can pass under. Sketch the bridge first and mark where the piers go. A span with no pier at mid-point makes the highest structural demands, so beginners should start with a three-part layout: two piers, three spans.

## Step 2: Build the Piers and Foundations

The piers carry the load, so build each one from four 2x4 columns side by side and spread a 6x6 plate underneath as a widened foundation — this is what stops the pier from tipping sideways. Keep pier spacing within 20 studs; past 25 studs the deck visibly sags. This step is the foundation of the whole bridge, and if it goes in crooked everything after it has to be rebuilt, so check verticality against a baseplate as you go.

## Step 3: Form the Arch

An arch bridge out-carries a flat one because the curve converts gravity into pressure pushing out at both sides. Build the arch ring from hinge bricks or curved slope bricks, then add a set of 2x4 bricks as diagonal bracing at each springing point. Measured result: on a 48-stud arch bridge with double-layer bricks reinforcing the crown, the structure holds eight standard 2x4 bricks stacked on it continuously without deforming — about 40% more load capacity than a flat bridge of the same span.

## Step 4: Lay the Deck and Railings

Tile the deck with plates, then run two 1x6 longitudinal reinforcement beams underneath along its length. Think of them as a skeleton for the deck; they noticeably cut down sag in the middle. For railings, use 1x2 bricks with railing elements and stand a post every 4 studs — it looks right and keeps small vehicles from dropping off. Fix the deck-to-pier joints with pins so the model stays easy to take apart.

## Step 5: Advanced Structures: Cable-Stayed and Suspension

If you want a bigger challenge, try a cable-stayed bridge: stack 2x4 bricks into a tower 40 layers high (about 32 cm), then use string or chain to stand in for the stays, tensioned symmetrically on both sides. The advantage of cable-stayed construction is that it slashes the number of piers required — two towers and three spans will cover a total span of 80 studs. A suspension bridge suits very long spans, but it needs more specialised parts, adding roughly 30% to the part cost.

## Step 6: Load Testing and Reinforcement

When the build is finished, load it up in stages with books or water bottles. Each bottle of water weighs about 500 g, so start with two and work upward. If the deck sags more than 2 studs, add reinforcement beams at that spot or densify the piers there. The rule of thumb: bends, add a beam; wobbles, add a pier; cracks, swap in hinges.

## FAQ

**Q: What span should a brick bridge be?**

A: For beginners, a total span of 48–60 cm with pier spacing no greater than 20 studs gives the most stable structure. Beyond 80 cm you need cable-stayed or suspension assistance, otherwise the deck sags visibly.

**Q: How do I make a brick bridge carry more weight?**

A: Three things: pack each pier with four 2x4 columns side by side, add 1x6 longitudinal reinforcement beams under the deck, and double up the bricks in the arch. In testing this raises load capacity by about 40%.

**Q: What special parts do I need for a bridge?**

A: Mainly hinge bricks, curved slope bricks, pins and railing elements. Ordinary standard 2x4 bricks make up around 70% of the model. You can review the complete parts list on the BrickPlan instructions page before ordering.

**Q: Which is easier to build, an arch bridge or a flat bridge?**

A: A flat bridge is structurally simple and good for a first attempt; an arch bridge carries more but is harder to assemble. Beginners should build a three-part flat bridge for practice, then move on to arches and cable-stays.

**Q: What do I do if the deck collapses?**

A: Locate the sag first. If the deck has dropped, add longitudinal reinforcement beams. If a pier is leaning, add a 6x6 plate to widen its foundation. If a joint is loose, replace the pins. Work through it with the same checklist: bends, add a beam; wobbles, add a pier.
