# MOC Blueprint Guide: Read Plans and Build Your First MOC

Almost every new builder hears the term MOC early on, but the idea of an MOC blueprint can be confusing: isn't a MOC supposed to be your own original design? In practice, the vast majority of builders start out by following someone else's plan. This guide covers what an MOC blueprint actually is, how it differs from an official instruction booklet, and how to choose and read your first one.

## What Exactly Is an MOC Blueprint?

MOC stands for "My Own Creation" — a brick model designed by a fan rather than by a set designer. An MOC blueprint is that design packaged so other people can build it, usually released as a PDF or a 3D file and containing step-by-step drawings, a parts list and a color scheme. For a beginner it is the smoothest entry point into the MOC world: you do not have to design anything from zero, yet you still get to experience what it feels like to build another builder's model.

## How MOC Blueprints Differ From Official Instructions

Official instructions hold your hand. Every step adds only a few parts, and each one is marked clearly. An MOC blueprint is closer to a designer's working drawing. Here is how the two compare:

| Item | Official instructions | MOC blueprint |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| Step size | Very few new parts per step | A single step may add a dozen or more parts |
| Part labeling | Part numbers and names are given | Often only renders, so you cross-check the parts list |
| Orientation | Clear placement direction | Some plans give no direction cues at all |
| Fixing mistakes | Backtrack step by step | You usually have to diagnose and disassemble yourself |
| Part sourcing | Included in the box | You gather the parts yourself from the list |
| Color freedom | Fixed | Colors can be swapped freely |

Once you understand this gap, it becomes obvious why so many first MOC builds go wrong. The plan is rarely at fault — the builder simply is not used to a document that leans much harder on spatial thinking.

## Four Things to Check When Reading a Plan

When you open an MOC blueprint, work through it in this order. First, read the whole thing once from start to finish and confirm whether the model is a single structure or a modular, split-part build, so you have a rough silhouette of the finished model in your head before you touch a brick. Second, sort your loose parts by color against the parts list — MOC blueprints often call for many same-colored parts in one step, and good sorting can cut your build time in half. Third, whenever a 3D perspective view is too ambiguous to read, look for an exploded view or a zoomed-in detail inset in the plan rather than guessing and forcing the part in. Fourth, check each large module against the finished-model photo as soon as you complete it; fixing a problem early is easy, while reworking it later gets progressively harder.

## Choosing Your First Blueprint

When picking a starter plan, check three things. Keep the part count between 300 and 800 pieces — demanding enough to be interesting, but not overwhelming. Favor authors who include a complete parts list and step-by-step drawings. And choose a subject you genuinely like, whether that is vehicles, architecture or mechs, because interest is the best fuel for finishing the build. Once your first model is done, try swapping the color scheme or adding small details, and work your way gradually toward a true original design of your own.

If you want a reliable starter plan, head to [brickplan.cn](https://brickplan.cn): every blueprint there comes with high-resolution step-by-step drawings and a downloadable parts list, and the community has plenty of beginner write-ups about the mistakes they ran into. Start by building from one blueprint, and after enough of them you will feel ready to start modifying designs yourself.
