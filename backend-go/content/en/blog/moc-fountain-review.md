# Brick MOC Fountain Review: Clear Parts for Realistic Water

The short verdict first: this is a 900-piece brick MOC fountain that looks far more finished than its piece count suggests, takes about 6 hours to build, and gets its entire water effect from stacked trans-light-blue parts. Difficulty sits at low to medium, it works beautifully as a desk display, and it is worth picking up.

## Water Jets and How the Clear Parts Are Used

The water involves no motor and no lighting — it is all parts. The builder alternates 2x2 trans-light-blue round plates with trans-light-blue elements, stacking the column out of three readable segments: the splash at the base, the body of the jet through the middle, and the spray at the top.

Trans-light-blue reads differently depending on how much of it you stack and at what angle light hits it, so the column does not look like an even glass rod. It looks like moving water rising, which is the whole trick.

The top is where the effect is won. About 40 1x1 transparent parts fan outward as scattered droplets, with a small number of white parts mixed in to suggest mist. That thin layer of mist is what makes the model read as wet — without it, the top spray would just look like bits stuck on. It is also why the build lives or dies on colour: it only works if your parts bin has enough trans-light-blue to keep the density even.

## Basin Structure and the Fountain's Center Column

The basin is a circular pool built from curved bricks into three tiers of steps, and that stepped outline is what makes it read as a fountain at a glance. The inside of the pool wall is lined with trans-light-blue elements to fake the reflection on the water surface — much better than a dark interior, since a single course of clear parts is enough to look like water has risen to the lip.

The center column is the cleverer part. It is built as a double-tube offset: an inner and an outer transparent cylinder rotated 45 degrees against each other so they interleave. Rather than reading as two concentric rings, the eye interprets it as a spiralling jet climbing the column. At the bottom, dark blue parts create a shadow under the water, which gives the pool floor real depth instead of a flat tiled look. Underwater shadow, water surface and spray each sit at their own level, and the layering is very clear.

## Difficulty, Cost, and Whether It's Worth Building

Loose parts cost roughly 150–220 CNY, with transparent parts making up about 30 percent of the total. That sounds like a lot, but they are common generic pieces — ordinary 1x1 and 2x2 transparent elements and curved bricks — not hard-to-find parts you have to hunt down. If you already have trans-light-blue in stock, the cost drops a lot; if you are starting from zero, the price is still reasonable for the size.

Nothing in the build involves mechanics or lighting, so the logic is straightforward: lay the basin, run the water column up, then dress the surface with transparent parts. That makes it a good transition project — simple enough for a first MOC after finishing standard sets, and still a quick weekend build for more experienced builders. The overall difficulty is low to medium.

Compared with a traditional architecture MOC, the fountain is small. A finished tower or street-corner facade takes up far more shelf space, while this delivers a strong display effect for much less money — that is the value argument, and it is why the recommendation holds. ## FAQ

**Q: How do you get the splash effect on a brick MOC fountain?**
A: Stack 2x2 trans-light-blue round plates alternately with trans-light-blue elements, split the water column into three segments — base splash, mid column and top droplets — then add about 40 1x1 transparent parts at the top to scatter the droplets. That is enough for a convincing spray.

**Q: How much does it cost to build a brick MOC fountain?**
A: Loose parts run about 150–220 CNY, with trans-light-blue making up around 30 percent. If you already have transparent parts at home, the cost can fall below 100 CNY. It is an entry-level build with a parts list that is not complicated.

**Q: The base is wobbly and keeps falling apart — what should I do?**
A: Start the basin with a solid baseplate and raise it about 4 layers, then circle the outside with curved bricks that interlock with each other, and finally clip the bottom of the center column onto studs on the pool floor. Once the base has enough weight, it will sit on a desk without wobbling.
