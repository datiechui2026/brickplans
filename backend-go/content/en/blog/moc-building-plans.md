# MOC Building Plans Reviewed: How to Pick the Right One

## What MOC Building Plans Actually Are

MOC building plans are the core tool of any My Own Creation project. They record a designer's process of building a model from nothing, and a good one does far more than chop the model into numbered steps. It carries the designer's structural reasoning, colour choices and shaping tricks, which makes it excellent study material for anyone who wants to build better models of their own.

What separates a real plan from a folder of photos is the way it handles assembly order: sub-assemblies are built separately and joined later, fragile sections are handled before they become hard to reach, and the order of steps reflects how the model actually wants to be put together. As MOC culture has grown, plans have appeared across architecture, vehicles, mecha, animals and sci-fi. This review looks at the dimensions that matter when choosing one, so you end up with a plan that suits how you actually build.

## Difficulty Levels

The difficulty of MOC building plans falls into four clear tiers:

- **Easy entry (★★☆☆☆)**: micro scenes or simple vehicles, 80-200 pieces, good for building with kids or for first practice.
- **Steady progress (★★★☆☆)**: mid-size buildings or vehicles, 200-500 pieces, requires basic brick building technique.
- **Advanced challenge (★★★★☆)**: large buildings or complex mechanisms, 500-1200 pieces, needs patience and spatial imagination.
- **Master level (★★★★★)**: very large scenes or fully articulated models, 1200-3000+ pieces, aimed at experienced MOC builders.

Before choosing, assess your own experience and the time you have available, then start with a plan that matches your level. The jump from three stars to four is usually not about piece count but about hidden structure: large builds rely on internal frames and offset connections that are easy to get wrong if you have not met them before. Working through a two-star and then a three-star plan first is the cheapest way to learn those habits.

## Parts Breakdown

The parts a plan calls for directly shape the experience, and this is where a plan earns or loses its money:

- **Parts list**: a quality plan provides a complete parts list with part numbers, colours and quantities. Plans on BrickPlans all come with a detailed parts list, so you can buy pieces directly from it rather than guessing from step images.
- **Recommended brands**: for MOC plans, Gobricks is the best value choice, with high precision, good compatibility and well-controlled colour variation. If budget allows, LEGO parts are best for quality and feel. SEMBO's technic parts are strong value and well suited to mechanical MOCs.
- **Piece count range**: MOC plans run from a few dozen pieces to several thousand. 500-1000 pieces is the most comfortable range for most builders.

Read the list before you commit, not after. A plan calling for a handful of unusual parts in a specific colour can cost more in sourcing time than the rest of the model combined, while a plan built mostly from common bricks, plates, tiles and slopes comes together quickly. Checking quantities of small parts early also tells you whether the design leans on heavy greebling, which is slow and fiddly work even when the piece count looks modest.

## The Building Experience

Experience varies with plan quality. Plans on [brickplan.cn](https://brickplan.cn) are reviewed by the community, so quality is dependable. A build usually starts with the base or internal frame, expands outward, and finishes with detail work.

A good plan marks the orientation of key parts at each step, so you do not discover a reversed piece halfway through. It also flags the points where the structure needs reinforcing, so the finished model stays solid rather than sagging under its own weight. Sorting parts into trays before you start is worth the ten minutes; nearly every stall in a long build comes from hunting a single small piece. For collectors of building plans, the BrickPlans library updates regularly, with new designs added every week.

## The Finished Model

The finished result is the best test of a plan's quality. A good plan produces a model that performs well on structural stability, accuracy to the original design and level of detail. Whether it is a street scene destined for the display cabinet or a vehicle model you can actually pick up and play with, the finished build is the clearest proof of the designer's skill — and the clearest sign that the plan will hold up if you build it a second time.
