# Brick Harbor MOC Guide: Dock, Crane and Cargo Ship

Short answer first: build a brick harbor MOC as three separate modules — dock, crane, cargo ship — and combine them at the end. Keep support pillars under 8 studs apart, add counterweight to every crane base, and pick a cargo ship with a detachable hull so the scene's scale stays consistent.

A harbor is one of the most layered subjects in brick transport building: a static dock, a moving crane and a moored cargo ship. Put the three together and the scene tells a story on its own. The hard part isn't the building — it's keeping the proportions coherent and the traffic flow readable. This guide works through the dock, crane and ship modules in order, with parts guidance and the mistakes worth avoiding.

## Choosing Instructions and Planning the Layout

When picking a harbor MOC plan, check first whether the scene is split into modules. A good plan separates the dock platform, the loading area, the crane and the cargo ship so each can be built on its own and merged later. Confirm the plan includes a parts list for containers and racking — that's the bulk of a harbor's detail. On https://brickplan.cn you can filter by transport theme and read builders' notes on crane articulation and dock proportions, which helps you avoid a plan where the modules are visibly out of scale with each other.

## Building the Dock Platform

The dock is the skeleton of the whole harbor. Start with large plates as the base, then run dark grey bricks along the outer edge to suggest the impact bumper strip. Support the platform from underneath with pillars spaced no more than 8 studs apart, and leave a height step between the water surface and the dock deck so the waterline reads properly. For the jetty section, cantilever long plates outward with diagonal bracing and connect them to the main platform at an offset rather than flush — the staggered edge adds a lot of realism. On the shore side, add mooring bollards and fender pieces; they state the subject immediately and raise the detail density.

## Crane and Cargo Handling Structure

The crane is the technical centerpiece. For a gantry crane, use a two-leg structure that straddles the rails, mount gears and a turntable on the crossbeam for slewing, and add a pulley and hook at the end of the boom. A tower crane needs attention to counterweight and base reinforcement; when the jib span is long, add cable-stayed supports. Keep the two rails parallel so container trucks can pass between them. If you want the crane to actually be playable, leave a rotating joint at the crossbeam — that way it works for display and for handling.

## Cargo Ship and Container Scene

The cargo ship comes down to hull and deck. Build the hull with curved bricks to shape a streamlined bow, then stack containers across the deck in layers, alternating red, blue, yellow and green so the ship becomes the visual highlight of the harbor. Use dark bricks for the hull and bridge to sharpen the silhouette, and pick a warm color for the funnel as an accent. Keep the shore container yard echoing the stacks on the ship, and place truck and forklift pieces so the whole loading route reads at a glance.

## Color Scheme and Scene Styling

A useful rule is cool water, warm dock: lay blue-to-green gradient plates for the sea, then lift the dock and containers with high-saturation warm colors. For styling, add a seagull, a lighthouse and a few palm trees — a harbor shouldn't be purely machinery, it should feel lived in. If you have the space, add an approach bridge connecting to the water so the cargo ship and the dock genuinely connect.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose a harbor MOC plan?**
A: Check whether it's modular first. A good plan separates dock, loading area, crane and cargo ship so you can build them individually and merge them later. Confirm it lists container and racking parts, and filter by the transport theme when browsing.

**Q: My crane keeps tipping forward. What's wrong?**
A: It's almost always an underweight base. Fill the base interior with counterweight pieces and extend the outriggers to widen the support footprint. The longer the jib, the more counterweight you need — and add diagonal bracing at the jib root if necessary.

**Q: Stacked containers keep coming loose. How do I fix that?**
A: Stacks without interlocking will slide. Use plates with studs as a connection between layers, or add small locking pieces at the container corners. Above 3 layers, a retaining wall behind the stack noticeably improves stability.

**Q: How many parts does a harbor MOC need?**
A: Dock and crane alone run roughly 800–1500 pieces. A medium scene including the cargo ship, container yard and trucks is usually 2000–4000 pieces. Buy and store by module so moving the finished build doesn't shake it apart.
