# Space Station Brick MOC Guide: Scale, Modules and Parts

Among sci-fi brick subjects, space stations are my favourite. Unlike warships or mechs, a space station scene isn't about looking cool — it's about being plausible. It should read like a small city floating in orbit, with lab modules, docking ports and solar arrays unfolded to the sun. Here's the full breakdown, from design thinking to parts planning, for building your own station MOC.

## Decide the scale first

Before you place a single brick, fix the size of the build. A mini scale (a few tens of centimetres) works as a desk display and rewards clean, simplified lines. Minifig scale — roughly 40 cm and up — lets you build detailed interiors and working mechanisms, which is why most MOC builders go that way. My advice: for your first attempt, build just the core module at minifig scale, around 20-something centimetres long, then splice on extensions once you have some experience. Once the scale is locked, you can estimate both your parts budget and your build time accurately.

## Modular sections: the skeleton of the station

The one thing to avoid is building the station as a single long stick. Real stations are assembled from multiple functional modules docked together, and I strongly recommend the same modular approach in a MOC: build each section as an independent module with standard cross axles or technic pins as the docking interface, so you can take it apart and add new modules later. A common layout puts the core module in the centre, with lab and habitat modules extending symmetrically to either side, and a node module acting as the traffic hub connecting each direction. Leave interior space in every module for a living area — bunks, consoles, a few minifigures — and the detail density jumps immediately.

## Solar panels and robotic arms: the sci-fi accent

Solar arrays are the part of a space station build that delivers the biggest visual payoff. The method isn't complicated: build a rectangular wing from a row of hinges or technic beams with holes, clip on trans-clear thin plates to suggest the cell surface, and connect the root with a ball joint or axle pin so the array can be shown folded or deployed. If you want more life in it, add a small gear train so it rotates by hand. Robotic arms work the same way — chain technic arms with several ball joints, then fit a claw or a camera at the tip. One arm that can hold a range of poses makes the whole scene feel alive.

## Docking mechanisms and ports

The docking port is the station's front door. Look at real designs and you'll see a ring plus petal-style locking structure: use an octagonal or circular base, arrange a ring of small pieces around the circumference as locking claws, and keep the central passage wider than a minifigure's shoulders. Don't forget an alignment target tower with an arrow and some navigation lights beside it. Get the docking mechanism right and a small shuttle parked against it looks completely natural — the logic of the whole space station scene closes up.

## Colour scheme and parts list planning

For the colour scheme, go for restrained tech: white and light grey for the main body, orange-yellow warning accents and dark blue hatches, and avoid bright reds and greens that break the look of a spacecraft. Plan the parts list in three blocks: structural elements (beams, plates, pins) take the largest share, exterior elements (panels, curved bricks) come second, and functional elements (gears, hinges, trans pieces) last. For uncommon specialised pieces such as large curved hull panels, check whether you already own them before deciding to substitute ordinary plates — that keeps the budget under control and stops the build stalling.

## Build order and pitfalls to avoid

My habit is inside first, outside second, skeleton before skin: build the main beam frame of each module, confirm the interior living-area layout, then lay on the outer wall panels, and finally attach the solar arrays and robotic arms as a single pass. Three pitfalls to watch for. First, always use standard connectors for docking interfaces — a bespoke non-standard port will make later expansion miserable. Second, calculate the folded and deployed proportions of your solar arrays in advance, because an array that overshoots the base once unfolded is a very common embarrassment. Third, leave a little friction margin in moving joints so nothing goes slack the moment you touch it. Get these right and the finished model is both good to look at and good to handle.

There's no single correct answer to building a space station out of bricks — the important thing is to start.
