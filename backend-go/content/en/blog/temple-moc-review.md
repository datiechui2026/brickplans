# Temple MOC Review: Eastern Architecture in Bricks

A temple is the high-water mark of Eastern classical architecture: upswept eaves, carved beams, bracket sets and the rhythm of morning bells and evening drums, all governed by rules. Squeezing one into the brick world is a challenge plenty of MOC builders keep coming back to. I recently built and compared several **temple MOC** instructions, taking each one apart to see how the structure and the mood hold up. Here is what I found.

## Eaves and Brackets: The Roof Is Where Skill Shows

The first test any **temple MOC** has to pass is the roof. Chinese classical roofs sweep in a long, relaxed curve with corners that tilt upward, and ordinary slope pieces struggle to capture that light, lifted quality. Good instructions use curved slopes and corner pieces in offset, staggered layers to build a natural arc and thickness into the eave line, then lay dark tile pieces to imitate the rows of round ridge tiles. In testing, the strongest builds gave dedicated treatment even to the ridge-end beasts and the dougong bracket clusters under the eaves, with details that survive close inspection. That is the gap between a cheap plan and a serious one.

## Hall Structure: Timber Framing in Brick

A temple's main hall is designed so that "if the walls fall, the house still stands" — the load runs through beams and columns, not masonry. A strong **temple MOC** plan puts down a solid grid of columns and a stone platform first, then adds the beam framework and only then the roof, rather than simply stacking up walls. Thin cylindrical pieces stand in for the round columns, lattice pieces reproduce window tracery, and horizontal beam pieces inside trace out the coffered ceiling and purlins, so the sense of an internal skeleton appears immediately. One plan in this review even worked the traditional column-reduction method into its structure, leaving the interior of the hall strikingly open and tall — and very much in the Zen spirit.

## Buddha Figures and Offering Tables: Details That Carry the Spirit

A temple without a Buddha figure is missing its soul. Most **temple MOC** designs place a throne, offering table and incense burner inside the hall, using gold and transparent pieces to suggest solemnity and a halo of light. These details are small, but they do the most to lift the overall finish: a cushion in front of the offering table, prayer banners hanging from the beams, even a small curl of "incense smoke" built from a few pieces can upgrade the whole thing from an architectural model to a scene with a story. Check the parts list before you start and confirm the gold pieces, transparent pieces and printed elements are all present, so a late trip for missing parts does not kill the momentum.

## Color and Atmosphere: Restraint with a Touch of Zen

Nothing ruins a temple build faster than a busy palette. Mature **temple MOC** plans work mainly in vermilion red, dark blue-green and natural wood tones, with off-white walls and dark gray tiles, accented sparingly with gold and green so the whole reads calm and dignified. The piece that moved me most in this review went further, adding moss-green bricks at the wall corners to suggest age and weathering; combined with stone steps, an incense burner and an old tree, it immediately evoked a mountain temple in deep forest. Add one warm light and you have a small Zen world on the desk.

## Build Experience and Suggested Improvements

On build experience: good instructions put the base platform and load-bearing structure in the early stages and save the roof eaves and fine decoration for later, which keeps the sequence logical and rework rare. The mild disappointment is that many temple plans currently lean on a wide range of specialized parts for the dougong brackets and coffered ceiling, which hurts versatility and pushes part costs up. Some builds also neglect the rear and the interior, so they only work viewed head-on. As for improvements, the authors should supply a substitute list of common parts and finish the interior furnishings so the model can be appreciated from all four sides.

All told, the **temple MOC** theme is one of the best ways to express the character of Eastern classical architecture in brick, and with the right instructions the upswept eaves, bracket sets and Zen atmosphere can all be reproduced under your fingertips.
