# Brick Maglev Train MOC: Levitation and Track Design Guide

Here is the conclusion up front: what sells a maglev train model is a gap of only a few millimeters between car and track. Tuck the support wheels inside the body skirts, let those wheels run in a channel on the outside of the rails, and from the front the train reads as genuinely floating.

## Levitation Structure: Making the Train Actually Float

The most eye-catching thing about a brick maglev train MOC is that thin sliver of air between the train and the track. Bricks have no real magnetic levitation parts, so the float has to be faked with structure: hide the support wheels inside skirt panels on both sides of the body, run the wheels in a channel on the outer face of the rail, and from a head-on view the train appears to hover above the track.

The method: build two parallel support beams under the body from technic pieces, mount small-diameter wheels at both ends of each beam (for example 6x2 wheel hubs with rubber tires), and seat those wheels in the guide channel of the track. Keep roughly a 4-plate gap between the body and the running surface. That gap decides whether the whole build works. Too small and it scrapes the track; too large and the illusion collapses.

## Track Design: Straights, Curves and Switches

The track is the other half of a brick maglev MOC's soul. Build a continuous running surface from 2x2 tiles, then add guide walls on both sides using 1x2 bricks with side studs, fixed at a width of 6 studs so the support wheels on both sides of the body drop into place precisely.

Straight sections are simple. Curves are the real difficulty: because the support wheels sit on both sides, a curve needs a turning radius of at least 8 studs, or the body will jam. My advice is to build a test curve from a 4x4 turntable section first, push the car through by hand, and only lay the permanent track once it rolls smoothly. For a switch, use hinge pieces to make a moving tongue rail operated by a manual lever. It will not change tracks automatically like the real thing, but it looks spectacular on display.

## Streamlined Body: Slopes and Curved Pieces Together

The nose of a maglev train is its signature streamlined shape, and you carve it layer by layer with slope bricks and curved pieces. A recommended approach: step 6x1 curved slope bricks outward from the top at the front of the nose, wrap both sides with 4x1 curved pieces to form a rounded cockpit outline, run 2x4 curved plates along the roof as a continuous arc, and embed a row of 1x2 transparent pieces in the body for windows to create that modern high-speed-train look. Do not forget a waistline running from nose to tail along the sides of the body: lay 1x2 printed tiles or 1x1 side-stud pieces horizontally, which visually stretches the whole train and gives it leaner proportions.

## Color Scheme and Detail Work

Classic maglev trains are mostly white, blue and gray. Use white for the main body, dark blue for the waistline and nose accents, and light gray for the skirts; the result is clean and suitably high-tech. For details, add three things. First, doors: build openable doors from 1x2 hinge pieces, or simply suggest the door seams with 2x2 tiles. Second, a car number: use a 1x2 printed tile or a sticker to make a marking such as "CR400". Third, end stops at the track ends: finish with 2x2 slope pieces with studs so the train cannot slide off the rails. If you have light pieces on hand, drop a trans-yellow 1x1 brick into the headlight position and the night display effect doubles instantly.

## FAQ

**Q: How do you create the floating look on a brick maglev train?**
A: Hide the support wheels inside the skirt panels on both sides of the body and let the wheels run in a channel on the outside of the rail. From the front the wheels are masked by the skirts, leaving only a few millimeters of visible gap, so it looks like real levitation.

**Q: How do you build the straights and the curves?**
A: Use long straight beams on straight sections to keep the run smooth, and on curves break the track into several short, shallow-angle segments that approximate an arc. Add a turntable or swing-arm piece at the switch point to change tracks, and never let the curve radius fall below twice the length of the car.

**Q: How do you build the streamlined body?**
A: Combine slope bricks with curved pieces: give the nose a forward-leaning angled face, keep the body sides flat with large tiles, and round the transitions at the top. Avoid right angles throughout, and you get the high-speed-train feel.

**Q: What if the levitation height is uneven?**
A: Check whether the lower edges of the skirts are level and whether all the support wheels sit on the same horizontal plane. Adjusting the bushings at the axles lets you fine-tune it, and the gap between skirt and running surface should be kept uniformly at one plate thickness.

The three most common problems when building a brick maglev train MOC are uneven levitation gaps, stalling on curves and off-center weight. An uneven gap usually means the running surface is not flat: check that every track section sits at the same plate height, using a baseplate as an alignment tool or simply eyeballing the seams between tiles. If the car stalls on a curve, check the width of the guide channel first, and widen the curve section by half a stud if needed. Off-center weight tips the body to one side and causes scraping; fix it by placing the battery box or ballast pieces in the exact center of the body and balancing the load on the support wheels on both sides. One last reminder: once the main build is done, push it around the loop several times to test. The joy of a maglev MOC is exactly that moment when it runs smoothly.
