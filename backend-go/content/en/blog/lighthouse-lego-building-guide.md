# How to Build a LEGO Lighthouse: Tower, Lamp Room and Lighting

A lighthouse is almost always the star of a seascape or harbor MOC. The red-and-white banded tower, the transparent lamp room and the beam sweeping the water at night — nobody mistakes it for anything else on a display table. This guide breaks the build down step by step: tower structure, lamp room, rotating light and wiring. It isn't tied to a specific set, only to general building approaches you can adapt.

## Tower Body: How to Build the Taper and Setbacks

A real lighthouse tower is wide at the base and narrow at the top. The easiest way to get that in brick is stepped tapering: build four or five courses of constant-diameter cylinder, then every so often step the wall inward by one brick's width so the circumference shrinks gradually. At each step, use plates to clamp the inner and outer rings together — otherwise the courses drift out of alignment and the tower leans more with every layer you add.

Inside the tower, reserve a vertical core column. It stiffens the structure and doubles as the axle for a spiral staircase. Plan the window frames in advance and keep them aligned on every level; only then does the outside read as orderly.

Tower height is typically about 70% of the whole build. A mini desk model can stay under 30 cm, while a large centerpiece can go past half a meter. Decide the scale before you start and work backward from your baseplate size to the tower diameter — too wide looks bloated, too narrow leaves no room for stairs and light cables.

## Lamp Room and Rotating Light Structure

The lamp room is the soul of the lighthouse. It is usually a little narrower than the tower, with transparent parts forming a ring of windows to imitate a glass house. Cap it with a dark rain eave ring, then a cone or dome shade, and the silhouette is complete. If you want the light to actually turn, leave the bottom of the lamp room open and use a simple gear train to connect a rotation motor or hand crank to the lamp base. Fix the gears firmly, or the assembly falls apart after two rotations.

A more advanced version stacks two or three lamps side by side on a rotating bracket; as it turns, the light sweeps across the "sea" and photographs beautifully at night. If you'd rather skip the motor, lock the lamp in place and cover it with a transparent hemisphere shell for a calm static look. Add a transition ring between the lamp room and the tower, otherwise the top looks abrupt — a detail many builders miss.

## Lighting System: Choosing Transparent and Light-Emitting Parts

Lighting is the most rewarding part of a lighthouse build. For the lamp room walls, favor transparent bricks, transparent hemispheres and transparent plates; light transmission decides how convincing the glow is. For the light source, use official light elements, or a removable mini light kit or light strip. Route the cables through the channel in the internal core column, and run them before you close the top — reworking afterward is painful.

To get a beam that sweeps the water, add a transparent fan-shaped piece in front of the rotating lamp base as a refractor. Don't aim the light straight into people's eyes just for brightness; warm white or warm yellow suits a lighthouse far better. Adding a fill light at the bottom that leaks through the window gaps lifts the tower's sense of depth immediately.

## Seascape and Harbor Scene Pairing

The build is only finished once the tower sits in a scene. Lay out the sea with baseplates in several shades of blue, stack a few irregular pieces as rocks, and dot white small pieces for foam. For a harbor, add a stretch of pier, a small boat, a few seagulls and minifigures in trench coats; a small door at the tower base adds lived-in character. The red-and-white tower against the blue water becomes the visual anchor of the whole display.

## Common Pitfalls and Parts Prep

Beginners hit three snags most often: irregular setbacks that make the tower lean; stairs, core column and light cables competing for the same interior space so nothing fits once you're halfway up; and not enough transparent parts for the lamp room, leaving a half-transparent compromise. Lay the interior out on the desk first, then close the tower around it. Cylindrical towers eat 1x1 and 1x2 bricks, so sort same-color parts before you start, and keep a spare bag of transparent parts and gear sets on hand.
