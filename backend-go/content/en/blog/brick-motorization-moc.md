# Brick Motorization MOC Guide: From Motor to Remote Control

Every builder knows the moment: you finish a good-looking sports car or excavator from the instructions, put it on the shelf, and something still feels missing. What is missing is motion. Motorizing a static MOC means adding motors, gears and a remote control so the model can drive, turn, lift and lower — and the payoff feels like a completely different hobby. This beginner tutorial starts from the most basic components and walks you through your first powered conversion.

## Step 1: Learn the Three Core Components

Before anything else, get to know the three parts every motorized build depends on. The motor is the power source: the M motor is a good fit for driving small vehicles, while the L motor delivers more torque and suits heavier or more complex mechanisms. The battery box supplies power, and it comes in two flavors — with a remote receiver built in, or without. Beginners should buy the version with remote control included so they do not have to upgrade twice. Third are the gears, cross axles and connectors that act as the bridge between the motor's output and the parts that actually move. At entry level, a single combo pack of one motor, one battery box and one remote is enough, and those parts can be reused across future builds.

## Step 2: Build the Chassis Before Anything Else

The first move in a powered conversion is not cramming a motor into a finished model — it is planning from the chassis up. The standard approach: build a flat 2x2 or 4x2 chassis, leave a mounting space for the motor in the middle, and connect the motor's output to the drive axle with a cross axle. Link the front and rear axles with a universal joint or a differential so the wheels turn freely. For a first attempt, keep it simple: a four-wheel car with a steering mechanism (a worm gear driving a steering arm), the motor dropped in, then test runs in every direction. This step teaches you how power travels from the motor to the wheels, and every later conversion builds on that understanding.

## Step 3: Gearboxes and the Speed-vs-Torque Trade-off

Gearing is the most interesting part of a motorized MOC. The same motor, paired with gears of different tooth counts, can produce either high speed with low torque or low speed with high torque. A sports car needs taller gearing for speed; a crane needs shorter gearing for lifting force. During a conversion, build a simple gear set as a test "gearbox" and measure how each combination actually performs. The classic beginner errors are meshing gears too tightly, which binds and jams the drivetrain, or leaving too much slack, which makes gears slip and spin without driving anything. The spacing should be just right: teeth meshing firmly but turning smoothly. Use different cross axle lengths to fine-tune positions when needed.

## Step 4: Wiring the Remote and Battery Box

With the chassis and gears in place, it is time to connect power. Plug the motor's cable into the motor output port on the battery box, and watch the polarity — reversed wiring makes the motor run backwards. If that happens, swap the two leads or flip the reverse switch on the remote. Mount the battery box near the model's center of gravity: it keeps the weight balanced and still lets you remove it for battery changes. Pair the remote according to the channel settings in the instructions; it usually takes a few seconds. For the first power-up, prop the model up so it runs in the air, confirm the direction of rotation is correct and there are no strange noises, and only then put it on the floor. This prevents you from dragging a jammed gear train and damaging the motor.

## Step 5: Convert an Official Static Set

Once the basics feel natural, try the more advanced version: turning a static official set into a powered model. Study the instructions first to map the structure, and separate pure decoration from joints that could actually move — doors, tracks, the crane arm, the steering wheel. Prioritize the technic connection points the set already provides, hide the motors inside the body or chassis, and route axles out to the existing joints. It helps to look up motorized MOCs of the same subject in the BrickPlans (brickplan.cn) community, where builders share their conversion approaches and parts lists; following their lead saves a lot of trial and error.

## Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

A few pitfalls catch almost every beginner. First, chasing looks and ignoring the center of gravity: mount the motor and battery box too far off-center and the model will pop wheelies or pull to one side. Second, underestimating gear strength: under high torque, plastic gears strip their teeth, so use reinforced parts where the load is heaviest. Third, forgetting a service opening: if the battery box and motor go in, they have to come out again, so design a removable top cover or chassis plate from the start. Keep those three points in mind and your motorized builds will go far more smoothly. For more technic plans and conversion inspiration, the BrickPlans (brickplan.cn) community has a large library of MOC instructions to work from, ranging from simple starter cars to complex linked mechanisms — study, rebuild, repeat, and you will improve fast.
