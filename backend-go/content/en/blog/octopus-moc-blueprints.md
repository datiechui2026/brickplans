# Brick Octopus MOC: Head, Tentacles and Sucker Build Guide

The short version first: an octopus MOC lives or dies on its tentacles. When you choose instructions, check the tentacle bending plan before anything else. Build the head core first and step it inward layer by layer, and stagger the eight arm poses so no two match.

Octopuses are one of the easiest sea-life subjects to make look cinematic in bricks: a round head, eight spreading arms, dense rows of suckers, and the deep-sea mood comes almost for free. The build itself is not so forgiving. The head has to be round, the arms have to curve, and all eight arms need different poses. Get it wrong and you end up with eight straight sticks jammed into a ball. This guide covers picking a plan, shaping the dome and locking in natural arm poses.

## What to Check Before You Pick a Plan

Look at the tentacles first. A reliable plan states the bending scheme for every arm: which segments use hinge parts, which use curved parts, and which direction each arm turns. Next, confirm whether the head is a single-block build or a layered build. A layered dome comes out rounder but takes more steps; a single-block head is quicker and friendlier for a first MOC. Finally, check the finished size. Anything under 10 cm makes a good desk piece, while a 20 cm-plus octopus works as the centerpiece of a scene. The part count difference between the two is large, so pick according to your budget and patience.

## The Head Dome: Start With a Bowl-Shaped Core

The octopus head is an upside-down bowl, and the key is to build the core first. Lay a bowl-shaped base out of 2x4 plates, then step inward one layer at a time, edging each layer with curved bricks and capping the top with a round tile. If you want a rounder head, wrap a skin over the bowl core: run 1x2 slope bricks around the curve in a staggered pattern so the seams do not line up, and no hard corners show on the surface.

Leave room for the eyes at the front and slightly below the widest point. Octopus eyes are large and bulging, so raise them on black 1x1 round plates and ring each one with dark curved parts. The goofy charm shows up immediately.

## Eight Tentacles: Stagger the Poses

The eight arms are the hard part of any brick octopus MOC and the part that sells the model. Use a segment-and-set approach: connect two to three hinge sections per arm, thick at the base and narrowing toward the tip, and finish each one with a cone part. The critical rule is that all eight poses must differ. Curl the front two forward, send the middle two out to the sides, and let the rear two trail backward, with a different angle on every arm. That is what gives the model a swimming motion instead of a symmetrical surrender pose.

Build the suckers from 1x1 round plates or small round tiles, spaced along the underside of each arm. You do not need to cover every arm completely; a few suckers every so often reads as texture just fine.

## A Seabed Base Gives It a Home

A lone octopus looks a bit stranded, and a seabed base lifts the whole display considerably. Use blue transparent plates for the water, lay sand-colored plates for the floor, then dot coral and seaweed parts around. Pose the octopus half-hidden behind coral with only part of its face showing, or wrap a few arms around a coral rock, and the scene immediately tells a story. ## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose an octopus MOC plan, and what matters most?**

A: Check the tentacles first. A reliable plan marks the bending scheme for each arm, whether it uses hinge or curved parts, and which way it turns. Then confirm whether the head is single-block or layered; layered is rounder but takes more steps.

**Q: How do I get the octopus head properly round?**

A: Build the core first. Lay a bowl-shaped base out of 2x4 plates, step inward layer by layer, edge each layer with curved bricks, and cap the top with a round tile. For extra roundness, wrap the core in a layer of 1x2 slope bricks with staggered seams.

**Q: How do I keep the eight arms natural instead of stiff?**

A: Use segmented posing. Connect two to three hinge sections per arm, keep the base thick, and finish the tip with a cone part. Every arm needs a different angle, with front arms curling, middle arms reaching sideways and rear arms trailing, to get a swimming look.

**Q: How much does part count differ between small and large octopus plans?**

A: A great deal. An octopus under 10 cm suits a desk display, uses fewer parts and can be finished in a day. A 20 cm-plus octopus works as a scene centerpiece and multiplies the part count, so choose by budget and patience.
