# Ultraman MOC Blueprint Review: Tiga, Zero and the Original

Ultraman replica blueprints are everywhere right now. Almost every popular character has at least one builder selling a parts list and instruction file, from the original Ultraman through Tiga to Zero. The problem is that quality varies wildly. Some builds are instantly recognizable across the room; others end up looking like a robot wearing a helmet. I built three of them — Tiga, Zero and the original Ultraman — and compared them on the things that actually matter: how well the head reads, how much the figure can move, and how painful the build is.

## Head Sculpts: Does the Face Read?

The head is where an Ultraman blueprint succeeds or fails, and all three of these handle it differently.

The Tiga version I built uses layered plates to build up the silver outline around the eyes, finished with red stripe stickers. The likeness lands at roughly eighty percent — good enough that anyone who knows the character will name him immediately, and the profile even picks up some of that streamlined shape from the show. The original Ultraman blueprint takes a squarer approach to the face, with transparent elements over a dark inner lining so the eyes actually catch and glow under a lamp. It reads well on a shelf.

Zero is the standout on the head. The two head sluggers are separate pieces that can be removed and reattached, and the sticker sheet labels them clearly enough that you won't mix up the placement.

Across all three, the head sculpt is the strongest part of the design. But each creator has a noticeably different style — one leans smooth and stylized, another chunky and blocky — so look at finished photos from a few angles before you commit to a file.

## Articulation: Can It Hit the Signature Poses?

A good-looking Ultraman brick figure still needs to throw the poses people remember. This is where the three builds split apart.

The Tiga blueprint gives you ball joints at the shoulders, elbows and knees, plus two hand options — a closed fist and an open palm — that swap out. Getting him into the Zepellion Ray stance is no trouble at all, and the range in the arms means you can fine-tune the angle instead of settling for one fixed position.

Zero is weaker on the body. The waist is essentially locked, so anything that depends on a twist or a lean is off the table. What he does offer is plenty of head slugger and arm movement, which suits the more aggressive poses he is known for.

The original Ultraman is the simplest of the three by a wide margin: fewer joints overall and limited range. Treat him as a static display piece rather than something you reposition every week.

If posing matters to you, count the joint elements in the parts list before anything else. More joints almost always means more expressive posing.

## Build Difficulty and Instruction Quality

The difficulty tiers here are clear. The original Ultraman is entry level — under one hundred parts, and you can finish him in a single evening. That makes him the obvious first build if you are new to replica blueprints.

Tiga sits at medium-to-high. The parts list approaches two hundred pieces and the instructions run past forty steps. The upside is that those steps are clear, the diagrams are well laid out, and nothing gets skipped, so the extra length is honest work rather than confusion.

Zero is the messiest build. The parts are the most varied, with a lot of special pieces, and you will want to sort everything by color before you start or you will spend half the session hunting for a single element.

All three sets use high-resolution rendered instructions with clear callouts. A beginner can follow along without prior MOC experience, and the documentation is noticeably better than what you get with a lot of off-brand sets.

## Parts and Price Comparison

Replica Ultraman blueprints generally run from a few tens of yuan up to around one or two hundred, with the final number driven by parts count and joint count. Against officially licensed finished sets they are considerably cheaper, and the real advantage is flexibility: you can swap in pieces from your own collection and change the design as you go. The trade-off is that you source the parts and do all the building yourself.

If you already own a decent stock of common bricks, putting together the original Ultraman is very cheap — most of what he needs is standard. For Tiga and Zero, where the special pieces pile up, buying a complete parts pack is the lower-stress option.

## What to Pick

Start with the head sculpt and the joint count, then match the file to your experience level. The original Ultraman is the right entry point for newcomers and for anyone who wants a clean display piece. Tiga and Zero are for builders who care about articulation and screen accuracy and don't mind a longer parts hunt.
