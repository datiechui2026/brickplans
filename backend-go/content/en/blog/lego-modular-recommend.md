# LEGO Modular Buildings Guide: From First Set to Collection

The short version first: LEGO Modular Buildings are widely treated as the safest holds in the hobby. Every building shares a 32x32 baseplate and a uniform connection system, so you can join them into a street in any order; each floor lifts off as a complete unit; sets retire roughly two years after launch; and secondhand prices stay firm year after year. The line started with Café Corner in 2007 and has now run for close to two decades. This guide is here to help you pick the one building that genuinely suits you out of several dozen annual releases.

## Brand positioning: the collector culture of modular buildings

The series' biggest draw is expandability. The structures use a separable floor system, so the roof, the upper floor and the ground floor can each be lifted off in one piece, and the interiors — counters, furniture, staircases — are modeled fully to scale. These sets do not chase play features; they push detailed miniature living as far as it will go. Each model is discontinued about two years after release, which keeps resale prices solid over the long term.

## Representative sets: from entry level to collector pieces

Beginners should start with sets that are more structurally regular and use a moderate brick count, such as Detective's Office (10246) and Boutique Hotel (10297). Detective's Office is a New York-style red-brick building with lower floors and a comfortable building rhythm, and it hides a secret-passage function inside — a good balance of fun and difficulty. Boutique Hotel is known for its curved corner facade and is one of the few recent releases where the look outweighs the size.

Once you have some experience, you can take on the older classics, such as Town Hall (10224) and Brick Bank (10251). Town Hall is known for its domed tower and banquet hall, and at over 2,700 pieces it is the big one in the series. Brick Bank, with its vault and safe plus a corner colonnade facade, is regarded as the detail lover's favorite. Both have long been retired and their prices have climbed steadily, so they suit collectors with a budget.

## Build experience and part quality

The best part of the modular line is that there is no repetitive work — every floor has a different interior fit-out, so the build almost never drags. Part tolerances are excellent and bricks clutch tightly, and the generous number of printed elements (signboards, murals, telephone) saves you the trouble of lining up stickers. The downsides: some older sets show slight color variation from brick-batch differences, and retired sets cost far more than their original retail price.

## Price and value analysis

Current releases sit fairly steadily at roughly CNY 500 to 700 per 1,000 pieces. Given the density of interior detail, that puts them in the upper-middle range of the LEGO catalog for value. Retired sets follow a completely different logic: the premium usually runs two to three times the original price, so unless you are a hardcore collector, it makes more sense to watch popular sets that are close to retirement and buy while they are still in production.

## Who it suits, and the pros and cons

This guide is aimed at three kinds of people: builders who love miniature architecture and enjoy watching a city slowly grow, hobbyists who want collectible value, and anyone who wants to build their own small town from a single series. The drawbacks are obvious too — they take up a lot of space, the unit price is high, and retired models are hard to backfill. ## FAQ

**Q: Are LEGO Modular Buildings worth collecting?**
A: Their value-holding is outstanding. Each building is discontinued about two years after launch, yet decades on they still share a uniform connection system and can be joined into a miniature city. Secondhand prices stay firm, which is why players treat them as the safest holds in the hobby.

**Q: Which modular should a beginner start with?**
A: Pick something structurally regular with a moderate brick count, such as Detective's Office or Boutique Hotel. Detective's Office has lower floors and a comfortable building rhythm, plus a hidden secret passage; the hotel is known for its curved corner facade.

**Q: What exactly makes the series "expandable"?**
A: Two things. All buildings share a 32x32 baseplate and a uniform connection system, so they can be joined into a street in any order. And floors lift off as whole units, separating the roof from the ground floor, with the interior furniture and counters modeled to scale.

**Q: How is this different from the LEGO City line?**
A: Modular Buildings are aimed at adult collectors, with realistic proportions, high detail density and rising value after retirement. LEGO City is aimed at families and scene starters, with basic parts and lower prices. On a tight budget, you can practice on City first.
