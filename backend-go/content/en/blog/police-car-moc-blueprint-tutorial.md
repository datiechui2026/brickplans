# Brick Police Car MOC: Parts List, Build Order and Color Guide

The police car is one of the most classic and most rewarding subjects in vehicle MOC building. The black-and-white livery, the swept front end, and that one bright light bar do a lot of work — park the finished model on a desk and it holds the room. I have been building vehicle MOCs for years and have revised my own police car three times, collecting plenty of lessons along the way. This guide covers the whole process: finding a blueprint, sourcing parts, the build order, and how to handle the livery, whether you are a first-time builder or an experienced one looking to improve an existing model.

## Where to Find a Police Car MOC Blueprint and How to Read It

When I hunt for police car MOC plans, I start by searching keywords in blueprint communities and brick forums. Many designers share PDF instructions with a parts list attached, free of charge. Do not rush to order parts the moment you find a plan — check three things first.

One, confirm whether the design is brick-built or technic-based. That single detail decides whether you are buying basic bricks or beams and pins. Two, scan the parts list for rare or unusual elements and think about substitutes before you commit. Three, look at the finished-model photos and the in-progress build shots the designer posted; many hidden structural tricks only show up in those images. Communities like https://brickplan.cn let you preview the parts table directly and compare different designers' styles side by side, which saves a lot of time when you are choosing.

## Putting Together the Parts List

I sort parts into five modules: chassis, body, wheel assemblies, light bar, and livery pieces. Grouping them this way makes any gap obvious at a glance.

- **Chassis:** baseplates, axle elements, and suspension plates.
- **Body:** plates, tiles, and sloped elements for the nose, with hinge positions reserved around the door area.
- **Wheel assemblies:** buy a wheel set that already includes tires — far less hassle than sourcing tires separately.
- **Light bar:** transparent elements plus small red and blue light studs.
- **Livery pieces:** black and white bricks in large quantities. White tiles in particular get used up fast when you are laying down a police livery.

If your budget is tight, you can delay the specialty pieces. Get the basic structural parts in hand first and you can start building.

## Build Order: From Chassis to Roof

The build order is what decides whether everything lines up flush at the end. My sequence:

1. **Chassis first.** Fix the front and rear axles and the steering structure in place. If this stage is crooked, everything after it is crooked too.
2. **Frame up.** Use beams to connect the chassis to the body framework, and add lateral reinforcement through the middle.
3. **Close the body panels.** Work from nose to tail, section by section. Do not try to panel the whole thing in one pass.
4. **Wheels, light bar, details last.** Mount the wheel assemblies, install the light bar, then fill in the small details.

After each section, press-test the connections. Coming back to redo work after the whole model is assembled is a genuine headache.

## Black-and-White Livery: Color and Detail Tips

The black-and-white police livery is where patience gets tested. Use large areas of white tile on the roof, hood, and trunk. Black carries the lower half of the body and the front-end lines. A narrow black-and-white stripe section between them creates the visual layering.

When you lay the stripes, watch the width alignment of the bricks — being half a stud out is immediately visible. For the light bar, sandwich small red light studs inside transparent elements. Add a "badge" or reflective strip to each door and one on the roof, then finish with the small character pieces: license plate, mirrors, and front grille.

For more ideas on how other builders handle police car structure, browse the photos and plans at https://brickplan.cn. Plenty of inspiration comes from simply looking at other people's work.

## Mistakes to Avoid

A few things I learned the hard way:

- **Do not leave the window and door areas too thin.** The doors especially need to hold hinges; if the structure is not strong enough, a few open-and-close cycles will loosen it.
- **Buy white elements from the same production batch where possible.** Whites shift slightly between batches, and a model that ends up patchy white and off-white looks wrong.
- **Do not build the light bar solid.** Transparent elements that let light through are what give it the "lit up" look.

Get the blueprint right, gather the parts in full, and keep the build order clean, and a sharp brick police car is not hard to pull off. Start building — I suspect yours will come out better than my third version.
