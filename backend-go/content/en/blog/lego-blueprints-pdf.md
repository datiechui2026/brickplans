# LEGO Blueprint PDFs: Format, Printing and Tablet Reading

If you build MOCs regularly, you have probably learned the hard way that not every LEGO blueprint PDF is created equal. The first time you open a downloaded instruction file, the options pile up fast: vector or raster? A4 or A3? What resolution is actually sharp enough? This guide covers the format question, downloading, printing, reading and file management in one pass, so you can skip most of the trial and error.

## The Two Formats Behind a LEGO Blueprint PDF

Instruction PDFs come in two main types. The first is a **vector PDF**, built from clean lines and solid color blocks. It stays crisp no matter how far you zoom, which makes it the best choice for printing and the default format on most MOC platforms today. The second is a **raster PDF**, which is essentially a compressed image, typically from a phone scan or a photo of a printed page. It looks fine on a monitor, but the edges break into jagged stair-steps as soon as you zoom in.

Telling them apart takes about five seconds: zoom past 200%. If the lines stay sharp, you have a vector file. If pixel grain appears, it is a raster file.

## What to Check Before a High-Resolution Download

When you want a genuinely high-resolution PDF download, start with the resolution itself. **300 DPI** is the usual dividing line; anything below that will show fuzzy or ragged edges when printed. Next, confirm the parts list matches the instructions. Many files bundle the building steps and the parts inventory into one PDF, so remember to pull the parts-list pages out separately when you print. Finally, watch the file size. A PDF for a thousand-piece build typically runs **20-50 MB**, so make sure your connection is stable before downloading. A dropped transfer can leave you with a corrupted file and missing pages.

## Tips for Printing Paper Blueprints

Print on **A4 paper at 100% scale**, and leave "fit to page" unchecked. Scaling distorts the dimensions and any size callouts on the page. For pages packed with dark-colored parts, switch on ink-saver mode: it uses less ink and keeps heavy color fills from swallowing the outline detail.

Binding matters too. Instead of binding the whole stack, use paper clips or document wallets to split the pages into step batches. You only pull out the batch you are currently building, which is far more manageable than flipping through a single thick booklet on the table.

## Reading on a Tablet Is Easier

For anyone actually building, a tablet beats paper. Import the PDF into an app that supports layer annotation, such as GoodNotes or Notability. You can then use a highlighter to mark off the rows and columns you have already completed, which makes it much faster to find your place again if you have to disassemble and rebuild a section.

On a large-screen tablet, rotate the blueprint to landscape and use split screen: instructions on one side, parts bins on the other. The back-and-forth disappears and your build speed goes up noticeably.

## Build Your Own Blueprint Library

One last habit worth adopting: name and sort your folders by **theme - blueprint name - version number**, for example `Military-J-20 MOC-1.0.pdf`. Scan your printed copies and archive them, then sync everything to a cloud drive. Your LEGO blueprint PDF collection then becomes searchable at any moment and effectively impossible to lose.
