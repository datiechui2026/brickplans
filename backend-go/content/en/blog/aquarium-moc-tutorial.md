# Brick Aquarium MOC Tutorial: Build a Glowing Reef Tank

An aquarium is the ceiling of the "small but beautiful" category of builds: you don't need a full building like a modular street, because one small tank can hold a complete underwater world. The problem is that most beginners end up with a plain blue box — flat and lifeless. This brick aquarium MOC tutorial walks through the tank structure, the scaping, the fish and the lighting step by step, so you can turn a limited parts stash into an underwater scene with real depth and real light.

## 1. Tank and Base: Build the Aquarium Frame First

Decide the format before you pick up a brick: a single display tank, or a whole aquarium hall with several tanks? Beginners should start with a **single upright tank**, roughly 16×16 studs and about 12 bricks tall. Build the "glass walls" on three sides from clear bricks or clear plates, and use dark blue solid plates for the back panel — that saves clear bricks, and gives you a flat surface for seabed stickers or hanging coral. Run a ring of dark gray plates and bricks as the base, sit the tank on top, and leave slots at all four corners for light strips. When you join clear bricks, use small clear parts or thin plates as the connectors, never dark-colored pieces, or the "glass seams" will stand out badly.

## 2. Water Wall Texture: Using Clear Bricks Correctly

Clear bricks are the soul of a brick aquarium MOC, but there is a right way to use them. For a rippling water look, make the front wall of the tank from transparent blue plates and back it with a second layer of light blue plates, sandwiching a few white transparent elements between the layers as bubbles. Lay dark blue plates across the tank floor and edge them with light blue slope bricks to build a gentle underwater slope — that instantly creates a sense of depth. For the surface, run a layer of clear plate across the top of the tank, leave a strip of "air" above it, and put a few seagulls or a lighthouse tip up there: the boundary between inside and outside the water becomes obvious at a glance. Clear bricks scratch easily, so check them before building and turn any scratched ones toward the back.

## 3. Coral Reef and Rock Scaping: Layering Is Everything

Whether an aquarium looks good comes down to the front, middle and back layers of the scaping. **Back layer:** work against the back panel, using pink and purple plates to block out the large silhouette of the reef. **Middle layer:** pile brown and gray slope bricks into rocks, staggered at different heights. **Front layer:** small coral and plants — stack 1×1 round plates into coral heads and use green vine pieces as seaweed, choosing high-saturation pink, purple and orange that contrast hard against the blue water. Leave negative space: keep a clear patch of sand (tan plates) in the middle and place a couple of small fish figures and some shipwreck debris there, so the composition can breathe. Every rock must be fixed to the tank floor — don't just stack loose pieces, or the whole reef collapses the moment someone touches it.

## 4. Fish and Details: Bring the Seabed to Life

Fish are the finishing touch of the whole scene. At minifigure scale, use small fish elements or build your own: a 1×2 curved slope for the body, a 1×1 wedge plate inserted backwards for the tail, a 1×1 round plate for the eye — two or three color schemes are plenty. Don't line the fish up flat against the wall. Build a school instead: hang several fish at different heights on transparent rods or thin axles to suggest swimming paths. Add a shipwreck (a hull wreck built from brown slope bricks), a treasure chest (gold plus brown plates and tiles) and a bubble column (white transparent elements strung along a transparent rod), and the sense of story underwater jumps immediately.

## 5. Lighting: Make the Aquarium Glow

Lighting is what gives an aquarium its premium feel. The simplest approach: clip an LED strip into the bottom or the top of the tank and light it upward from below, so the clear bricks glow with a soft blue. For something more dramatic, switch to a strip with both blue and white, and the projection across the tank floor looks far more convincing. Plan the cable channel before you install anything — leave a vertical passage between the base and the back panel so the strip can run from bottom to top without wires showing on the front. Keep the strip about 1 stud away from the front clear wall rather than pressing it against the plastic, so the light spreads out instead of showing as a row of individual LED dots.

## 6. Build Order and Common Pitfalls

Recommended order: base and light channel → back panel → tank floor and the two side water walls → layered scaping (back rocks first, then middle rocks, then front coral) → fish and props → front clear wall and top cover.

Three things go wrong again and again:

- Clear bricks joined with dark-colored connectors, which makes the glass seams obvious.
- All the scaping piled against the back wall, leaving a huge empty space at the front.
- No light channel planned, so the LED strip ends up taped to the outside.

Remember these three rules — front, middle and back layers of scaping; never mix clear bricks with dark pieces; reserve the light channel — and your brick aquarium MOC will look genuinely display-ready.

Once the single tank is finished, try expanding it into a whole aquarium hall: three or four tanks side by side, an aisle down the middle, and an "Aquarium" sign across the top, and you have a miniature underwater world.
