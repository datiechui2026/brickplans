# Brick Pagoda MOC Tutorial: Tapering Tiers and Flying Eaves

Among Chinese traditional buildings, the pagoda is the hardest subject to get right. A city wall can hide a lot behind sheer length, but a pagoda has to taper at every tier and cantilever eaves at every tier. Miss those two moves and you end up with a chimney. This tutorial works from overall proportion down to the flying eaves, covering the tower shaft, the roof rings and the finial, so you can build a multi-story pavilion-style pagoda that actually reads as traditional architecture.

## 1. Fix the tier count and taper first: the pagoda's silhouette

The beauty of a pagoda comes from progressive tapering: each tier is narrower than the one below, which is what gives the shaft its rising curve. Take a five-tier pagoda: the ground-floor footprint is 10x10 studs, each tier steps in 1 stud on all four sides, and the top tier lands at 6x6. Tier heights shrink too: the ground floor is 6 brick courses, each level above loses 1 course, and the top tier stays at 3 to 4 courses.

Before you touch a brick, draw a plan view of every tier on paper and write the side length and height next to it. If this step is solid, you build each level straight off the drawing and you will never get halfway up and discover the taper no longer closes.

## 2. Tower walls: hollow beats solid

Build the shaft as a hollow tube. Each tier is four walls closing a square tube, with corner posts (or two stacked 1x1 bricks) reinforcing the four corners and the interior left open so you can add lighting or an elevator minifig later.

Keep the walls in the beige family, light tan and ivory white, to suggest brick masonry. Between tiers, run a ring of dark brown plates as a wooden belt course. That single dark line visually slices one floor from the next and the layering becomes instantly readable.

Do not put openings on every face. Cut one door on each of the four cardinal faces and windows on the remaining walls, otherwise the facade looks shredded. And remember: every tier's walls must be offset and interlocked with the tier below. This is the single thing that keeps the pagoda from leaning.

## 3. Flying eaves: SNOT does the heavy lifting

The upturned eaves are the hardest and the most rewarding part of a pagoda MOC. To build a lifted corner, start with SNOT bricks (studs facing sideways) to reverse the eave line outward, letting the eave plate cantilever 1 to 2 studs past the wall. Then at each corner, prop up an upward tilt with a 1x1 slope or a wedge plate, and the corner lifts.

Build the eave body from dark brown plates stepping outward from the inside, and finish the outer edge with a row of dark slopes standing in for tube tiles. The key to the upturn is high corners, low middle: all four corners sit about half a brick course above the midpoint of the eave. That is what gives the roofline its traditional snap.

## 4. Eaves under pressure: how each tier sits on the one below

After a tier's eave plates are done, the next tier has to sit on top of them, and this joint is the one most builders overlook. Cap the inner ring of the eave with plates to form a platform, then raise the next tier's walls from that platform. The eave plates are now pinned in place by the weight above them.

Keep the capping platform slightly lower than the outer eave edge so that, visually, rain would run outward. Tier pressing on tier, eave stepping out over eave: that is how the whole silhouette ends up layered like a real pavilion-style pagoda.

## 5. Finial and details: a clean finish

Once the top tier is closed, the roof needs its finial, the ornamental spire. Stand a thin rod piece on the central axis and thread gold or dark brown round pieces and slope pieces onto it to make a gourd shape or a pearl shape. Run a cross axle down through the centre holes of the top tiers so the whole spire stands straight instead of wobbling.

Hang a 1x1 ring piece at each of the four eave corners as a wind bell. At the base, lay a few steps in front of the door and plant two pine trees (dark green slopes on brown trunks), and the temple atmosphere arrives immediately.

## 6. Build order and pitfalls to avoid

Recommended order: base platform and steps, then the first tier's walls, then the first eave ring and its corners, then the eave capping platform, then repeat upward tier by tier, and finish with the finial.

Three failures come up again and again. First, the taper is not built to the drawing, and the finished top tier is wider than the base. Second, eave plates cantilever too far without support and drop off at the first touch. Third, the tiers are not interlocked, so the whole shaft twists like a wrung towel. The countermeasures are simple: press-test each tier by hand as you finish it, and add bracket pieces anywhere an eave overhangs by more than 2 studs.

Get taper, interlock and overhang right and your pagoda will be both stable and beautiful.
