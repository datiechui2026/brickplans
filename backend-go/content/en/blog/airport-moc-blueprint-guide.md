# Brick Airport MOC Blueprint: Terminal, Runway and Tower

An airport is a dream subject for a lot of brick builders: a sprawling terminal, a long straight runway, a tower rising above it all, and together they form a busy air hub on your table. But building one that is both impressive and structurally sound takes a clear blueprint and a sensible build order. This guide walks you through planning, structure and detailing so you can finish your own miniature airport without rebuilding it three times.

## 1. Plan First: One Good Blueprint Beats Ten Rebuilds

Many beginners dive straight into the terminal, then discover halfway through that the runway no longer fits and the tower is standing in an awkward corner. The right approach is to work out the whole layout on paper (or in software) first. The runway and apron take up a huge footprint, so fix the size and orientation of your baseplates before anything else, then divide the rest into four zones: terminal, boarding bridges, tower and parking area.

Keep scale consistent while you plan. Airport MOCs usually work at minifigure scale, where one standard baseplate covers what would be several dozen metres of a real airport. Terminal height generally sits at two to three storeys — tall enough to look impressive, low enough that it never blocks the tower's sightlines. Sketch the zones, note dimensions and colour scheme, and every later step has a target to hit.

## 2. The Terminal: Heart and Front Door of the Airport

Terminals are the showpiece of the whole build. Structurally, go modular: build the check-in hall, security area and departure lounge as separate modules, then connect them at the end. Modules are easier to move, and much easier to remodel later.

The roof is what gives the building its character. A curved glass roof can be made by stacking angled transparent pieces layer by layer for a light, modern feel; a flat roof is simpler and gives you room for air-conditioning units and antennas. For the facade, floor-to-ceiling window pieces combined with white or light grey bricks quickly create the big glass curtain wall that every airport lounge seems to have. Don't forget small parts on the check-in desks — keyboards, monitors, a luggage scale — those tiny details are what make the scene feel alive.

## 3. Runway and Apron: Laying the Big Flat Areas

The runway looks easy but it is where most problems start. Because the area is so large, one uneven baseplate joint throws every building that follows out of alignment. Clamp your baseplates together on a flat table first, then run the centreline markings down the runway, using alternating dark grey and light grey long bars.

The apron deserves more depth. Start with light grey plates, then add stand markings, jet bridge connection points and one or two aircraft models. If you want a stronger sense of place, lay a short taxiway along the edge of the apron and park a fuel truck and baggage carts on it. These supporting actors keep the runway from looking empty and lift the finish level of the whole blueprint considerably.

## 4. Tower and Details: The Finishing Structure

The control tower is the visual high point of the airport, and the part beginners find trickiest. The challenge is the combination of a slender column and a cantilevered observation cab. Use solid column pieces or a widened base to keep it stable, close the cab in with transparent pieces, and seat a controller minifigure inside. A turntable or gear piece under the roof disc lets the top rotate slowly, which is a lot of fun.

Then come the atmosphere details: minifigures queueing in the departure hall, a windsock at the end of the runway, lighting masts on the apron, a taxi rank outside the terminal. Fill every corner of the blueprint with signs of life. Add direction signs and departure information screens at the entrances and exits so the airport actually feels operational.

## 5. Beginner Tips and a Parts Stockpile List

A few suggestions for a first airport MOC: start with a single module — just a tower, or just a departure lounge — and expand to the full scene later. Favour common parts you already have in bulk and be realistic about how many specialised shaped pieces you can afford. Photograph the build as you go, so you can review it and share the process.

A general parts list looks roughly like this: several standard baseplates for runway and apron, plenty of white and light grey bricks and plates, transparent window pieces and large glass panels, small printed elements (markings, signs, keyboards), turntable pieces, lighting mast parts, plus a handful of minifigures and a mini aircraft. Sort everything into boxes by colour and function beforehand and your build speed goes up dramatically.

An airport MOC is a project about patience and imagination. Hold on to your blueprint as the construction plan, work from layout to structure to detail one step at a time, and you will end up with a busy miniature air hub of your own, running day and night.
