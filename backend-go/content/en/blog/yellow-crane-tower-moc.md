# Yellow Crane Tower MOC: Layered Roof and Upturned Eaves

The short version first: a Yellow Crane Tower MOC stands or falls on layers and lift. Every storey of the pyramidal roof pulls inward by 2 to 3 studs, every eave corner rises 2 to 3 brick layers on hinge pieces, and the palette stays red columns, yellow tiles, white railings.

The Yellow Crane Tower is the high point of Chinese pavilion architecture. Building it in brick comes down to those same two ideas: layers and lift. A roof that steps inward storey by storey, eave corners that kick upward, and a base that suggests the river below — get all three right and the presence takes care of itself.

Start with the pyramidal roof. The tower has five storeys, and that converging roof is its most recognisable silhouette. The build logic is "step in as you go up": each level's footprint shrinks by roughly 2 to 3 studs on every side, and the storey height shrinks too, which produces the tapering tower profile. Close the top with cone pieces, or use SNOT to run four slopes that converge on the centre point, then finish with a 1x1 round plate plus a gold element as the finial. Keep the rate of inward step even. Sketch the outer outline of each level on paper before you touch parts, otherwise the whole tower body will look twisted.

Upturned eave corners are the tower's expression. On a Chinese pavilion the corners have to flip up, and the most practical trick in a MOC is hinge pieces: fit one hinge at each end of the eave line, lift the corner 2 to 3 brick layers, then fill the triangular gap the lift creates with wedge bricks. That reads as a natural flick. All four corners on every storey need to lift, and the angle has to match (roughly 30 to 45 degrees), or the building looks like it has a crooked mouth.

The colour scheme follows the imperial-pavilion route. Use dark brown and red for the main body — columns, doors, windows — golden yellow or orange-yellow slope bricks for the tile surface, dark red under the eaves to build shadow layers, and white as an accent on the railings. Red columns, yellow tiles, white railing: that combination carries the tower's richness without turning gaudy.

Do not skip the riverside base. Lay dark blue and transparent light blue plates for the water surface, and edge it with white wave plates for the spray. Add a short stretch of bank and a few trees to one side of the building — brown bars with green plates for foliage — and you get the picture from the old poem about sunny trees across the river. Make the base slightly larger than the tower footprint so it reads as visually stable.

The five-storey interior can be handled with smart shortcuts. You do not need a complete room inside each level; a single gallery ring is enough — 1x2 plates cantilevered out with white railing on top. The real focus is the rhythm of the bracket sets under the eaves. Run a ring of 1x2 bricks with studs on the side along the eave line, spacing one every other stud, and the layering shows up beautifully in raking light. For railings use white 1x1 railing elements or 1x2 fence pieces, and transition at the corners with hinge pieces so you avoid hard right angles. On the back of the tower you can leave a small door and a set of steps — a hint that the building can be climbed, which gives the model more story.

Common pitfalls. First, an inconsistent inward step per storey, which makes the tower body look like a wrung-out towel. Second, lifting the corners on the front face only — all four directions need the flick. Third, a decrease in storey height that is too gentle, so a five-storey tower ends up looking like a five-tier cake. Test the silhouette at small scale with a throwaway sub-assembly before you commit real parts.

## FAQ

**Q: How do you build the pyramidal roof of the Yellow Crane Tower?**

A: Step in as you go up. Each level's footprint pulls inward by about 2 to 3 studs, and the storey height decreases to create the tapering profile. Close the top with cone pieces, or use SNOT to run four slopes converging on the centre, then add a finial made from a 1x1 round plate plus a gold element.

**Q: How do you keep the upturned eave corners from looking crooked?**

A: Fit a hinge piece at each end of the eave line, raise the corner 2 to 3 brick layers, then fill the resulting triangular gap with wedge bricks. Every storey needs all four corners lifted, at a consistent angle of roughly 30 to 45 degrees — doing only the front face is what makes the building look lopsided.

**Q: How should the colour scheme be decided?**

A: Go imperial pavilion. Dark brown and red for the columns, doors and windows; golden yellow or orange-yellow slope bricks for the tiles; dark red under the eaves for shadow depth; white as an accent on the railings. Red columns, yellow tiles and white railing give you the grandeur without the clutter.

**Q: Does the five-storey interior need to be fully built out?**

A: No. One gallery ring per level — 1x2 plates cantilevered out with white railing — is enough. Put your effort into the bracket-set rhythm under the eaves: 1x2 bricks with side studs run along the eave line, one every other stud, which reads with clear depth in light and shadow.
