# Brick Airliner MOC Plans: Fuselage, Wings, Landing Gear

Airliners are the most recognizable subject in aviation MOC building, and the one that tests your fundamentals hardest. There's a long tube for a fuselage, wings that need a slight angle from root to tip, engines hanging under the wing, and landing gear that ought to retract — every stage hides a small trap. This guide to choosing a brick airliner MOC plan walks through the fuselage, wings, tail, cockpit and landing gear, plus the spots where new builders most often go wrong.

## Check the proportions first: if length and diameter don't match, skip it

The first thing to judge in a plan isn't whether the shape looks cool — it's the proportions. On a real airliner the fuselage length-to-diameter ratio lands roughly between 8:1 and 10:1, and a plan's fuselage length and diameter have to convert back into that range. Some airliner MOC plans build the body short and fat, so from across the room it reads like a tin can; that's a proportion problem, not a parts problem. Before you buy, count the fuselage segments and the width of each one, then run a scale check over length, wingspan and height. Plans that include all three views and state the finished dimensions are the ones worth trusting.

## Fuselage curvature: the cylinder is the hard part, don't expect it in one pass

An airliner fuselage is a smooth cylinder, and getting a circle out of square bricks is the hardest piece of the job. A reliable plan gives you a stepped curvature transition at each fuselage joint and treats the nose radome as its own subassembly, rather than forcing ring bricks straight onto a straight tube. As you build, add transverse connections on the inside — otherwise the body will twist apart halfway through. Keep an eye on the window layout as well: cabin windows should follow the fuselage curve at even spacing, and one or two sitting crooked spoils the look of the whole model.

## Wings and engines: the wing angle comes from two small tricks

Wings are the showpiece of an airliner MOC. On the real aircraft the wing rises slightly from root to tip instead of sitting flat, and plans usually get there in one of two ways: thin plates stacked at the wing root to build the angle in, or hinge pieces at the root so the angle stays adjustable after assembly. The classic beginner mistake is building the wing dead level, which looks stiff and fake. Engine nacelles should hang below the wing and sit slightly forward of it, stay symmetrical with the winglets, and match each other in height and position — a single brick of difference is instantly visible.

## Tail, cockpit and landing gear: details decide the likeness

Build the vertical and horizontal stabilizers to the proportions of the real aircraft. The sweep angle of the fin matters especially, since a fin standing too upright reads as a freighter rather than an airliner. Cockpit windshields are usually assembled from trans-clear parts — two to four curved glass pieces are enough to suggest the windscreen, and more isn't automatically better. Landing gear is where airliner MOC plans cut corners most often: basic plans hand you three fixed wheel assemblies, while thorough plans include a retractable mechanism that folds the nose gear up into the belly with hinge pieces. For a display model, fixed gear is fine; if you want the feel of an aircraft taxiing and rotating, look for a plan with retraction.

## Parts list and common pitfalls: check before you start

Always check the parts list before you open the first bag. Plans with many fuselage segments and a long wingspan need a lot of Technic beams, pins and gears plus long bricks, and one missing part stalls the build halfway through. A few frequent traps are worth heading off in advance: wings that aren't strong enough, where a long span with no internal spar sags over time; a nose that's under-weighted, so the finished model tips back on the shelf; and landing gear placed without accounting for the center of gravity, so the fuselage won't stand up. Favor plans with step-by-step instructions, marked load-bearing reinforcement points and common part sizes — you'll save yourself plenty of detours.
