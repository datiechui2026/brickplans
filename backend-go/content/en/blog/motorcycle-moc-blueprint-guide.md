# Brick Motorcycle MOC Plans Reviewed: Street, Race and Retro

After ten years of building with bricks, I honestly think motorcycles are the best-value MOC subject out there: they take up almost no shelf space, they photograph beautifully, and you can finish one quickly. Over the years I have built more than twenty brick motorcycle MOC plans, working through street bikes, race bikes and retro classics. Here is what they actually feel like under the fingers — which ones are worth your time, and where the traps are.

## Street Bike Plans: The Best Entry Point, but the Front Wheel Is a Hurdle

Street bikes are the friendliest place to start. They usually land around 300 pieces, so an afternoon is enough, and the design range runs from Honda-style commuters to Ducati-style streetfighters. Most street plans handle the frame-to-fuel-tank transition smoothly, and the seats are often built with soft rubber parts, which is a nice touch in the hand.

There is one very common trap, though: front wheel steering. A lot of plans treat steering as decoration — the handlebars turn, but they bind, or the fork scrapes the mudguard as it swings. The version I built actually required removing two parts before it would steer freely. If working steering matters to you, watch a video of the finished model before you buy.

## Race Bike Plans: Perfect Stance, Fiddly Details

Race bike plans are the ceiling for visual impact: full fairings, a low, aggressive stance, large-diameter wheels. Put one in a display case and it looks genuinely impressive. Reproduction accuracy on these brick motorcycle MOC plans is generally strong, and the color schemes lean on bright red and fluorescent yellow race livery, so they photograph well.

The trade-off is that the details are hard on the hands. The curved fairing panels have to meet cleanly, the transparent windscreen parts need precise placement, and the rider minifig's crouched pose has to be locked in — every step needs small adjustments, and beginners can get frustrated halfway through. Race bikes also sacrifice movement for stance: the rear wheel is essentially fixed. They suit builders who want to build it once and look at it.

## Retro Classic Plans: The Most Character, the Pickiest Parts

If we are talking about character, retro classics are my favorite category. A brown leather-style seat, large spoked wheels and softly rounded tank lines add up to an old-movie atmosphere once the model is finished. These are the rarest of the brick motorcycle MOC plans, and finding one with a complete parts list is genuinely difficult.

The trap here is special parts. Curved mudguards and two-tone spoked wheel hubs are uncommon elements, and you often have to break down several sets to gather them, so the cost adds up. Check your own parts pool before you commit.

## How to Choose Between the Three

| Type | Part count | Difficulty | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Street bike | Around 300 pieces | ★ | Beginners, quick builders |
| Race bike | 400–600 pieces | ★★★ | Display builders, detail obsessives |
| Retro classic | Around 400 pieces | ★★★ | Nostalgia builders with a deep parts pool |

## Closing Notes

Street bikes are for practice, race bikes are for photographs, retro classics are for the collection — all three kinds of brick motorcycle MOC plans have their own way to play. The key is being honest about whether you are a "roll it across the desk" builder or a "put it on the shelf" builder.
