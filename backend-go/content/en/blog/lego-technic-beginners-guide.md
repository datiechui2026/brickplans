# LEGO Technic Beginner's Guide: Parts, Gears and First Sets

Start with the short answer: before you buy anything, get comfortable with the four core part families — beams, pins, gears and axles — and with how gear ratios work. Then spend under 100 yuan on a small set to practice connections, or 200-500 yuan on a set with a gearbox, steering or suspension, which delivers the most learning per yuan.

## First, Understand How Technic Differs from Regular LEGO

Many builders hit LEGO Technic for the first time and wonder: it is still a brick system, so why does a Technic model actually move? The answer is that the part system is completely different. Regular LEGO is built from bricks that stack via studs on top. Technic is built from beams, pins and gears, and it works through sideways connections and rotating drive. Technic beams have a standard hole spacing, so once pins are pushed in you can build a frame that flexes and moves, and gears carry rotation from a motor out to wheels or arms.

The second difference is the measuring system. Regular bricks are counted in studs; Technic is designed on hole spacing. Mixing the two systems requires adapter parts. So be clear before you start: what you are buying into with Technic is a whole transmission logic, not just a shape to display. Understand that and you will not pick the wrong first set.

## The Core Parts: Beams, Pins, Gears, Axles

The parts look complicated but fall into four groups. **Beams** are the skeleton. Common lengths are odd numbers — 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 and 13 — with holes at both ends and along the middle, and they carry load and make connections. **Pins** are the rivets. There are friction pins and frictionless axle pins: the first locks a structure, the second makes a moving joint. Mix them up and your build will either wobble or lock solid.

**Gears** are the heart of the drive. Common tooth counts are 8T, 12T, 16T, 24T and 40T, and the gear ratio sets speed and torque — a small gear driving a large one slows things down and adds torque, which is what you want for climbing; a large gear driving a small one adds speed. **Axles** are the shafts that run through gears and mesh with the cross-shaped hole in the gear centre to transmit rotation. Learn the names, sizes and connection methods for these four families before you start building.

## Choosing a First Set: Three Budget Tiers

Do not jump straight to a flagship. Under 100 yuan you can get a small car or motorcycle with roughly 100-200 pieces, which is perfect for learning how pins and beams connect. The 200-400 yuan range is the sweet spot: look for a car with steering or suspension, such as an entry-level rally car, where you get gear reduction and damper behaviour in one build. Above 500 yuan you can consider a mid-range set with motors or pneumatic parts, but be aware a first-time build can take 10-15 hours.

Buying tips: check whether the set description mentions a gearbox, steering system or suspension — those sets teach the most. Technic sets built purely for display teach you no transmission at all. Sets with A and B build modes are better value, since one box gives you two practice builds.

## Common Drive Systems: From Direct Drive to Differential

Work through three structures in order of difficulty. **Direct drive** is simplest: gears mesh directly, and one reverses the rotation of the other, which is how you learn basic ratios. **Two-stage reduction** uses two gear pairs to drop motor speed and raise torque, the usual approach for vehicle drivetrains. The **differential** lets the left and right wheels turn at different speeds so nothing skids in a corner; it is one of the most classic Technic mechanisms and appears in almost every car model.

Start practice with the smallest closed loop — motor, gear, wheel — and ignore the bodywork at first, focusing only on getting the drive to turn. Then add steering links and suspension arms step by step. When gears jam, it is usually an axle not pushed fully home or a pin inserted the wrong way round, so build the habit of rotating the mechanism and hunting for the binding point.

## A Build Practice Order for Beginners

What order should a newcomer actually practice in? Five steps. First, build an official small set to learn the parts and how instructions work. Second, take it apart and rebuild it, working out why each part sits where it does. Third, run a minimum-drive experiment, free-building a rotating mechanism from loose parts. Fourth, modify an existing set, for example adding dampers to a car. Only fifth, try an original MOC. Each stage has a clear goal, so you are less likely to get frustrated. When you finish, sort parts into boxes by beams, pins, gears and axles — loose-part builders work far faster.

## Budget and Next Steps

Overall, 300-500 yuan is enough to buy 2-3 sets with different structures at the beginner stage. There is no need to chase out-of-production sets; secondhand marketplaces often have cheap loose-part bags that are ideal for topping up wear items like gears and axles. Once you can independently finish a car with a differential and steering, you are ready for powered components — motors and battery boxes — and remote-controlled MOCs. Remember: the point of starting Technic is understanding drive, not owning the most sets. Nail gear ratios and structure and you get more value than from ten extra boxes.
