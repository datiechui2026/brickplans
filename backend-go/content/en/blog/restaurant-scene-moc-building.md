# Restaurant Brick MOC Guide: Layout, Bar, Signs and Lighting

The short version first: divide the ground floor into four zones before you lay a single wall, build a visible height step into the bar, hide warm light bricks inside the fixtures, and give every minifigure a pose and a direction to look. That is where the lived-in feel comes from.

## Plan the Whole Layout Before You Build

A restaurant brick MOC succeeds or fails on paper, long before the first brick goes down. Split the ground floor into four functional zones: kitchen, cashier, dining area and takeout counter. Put the kitchen at the very back and separate it from the dining area with a half wall or a low cabinet — this shows off the prep counter without blocking sightlines. Set the cashier right next to the entrance so minifigures can walk in and order without crossing the room. Save the window side for booth seating. Make the takeout counter a small window opening onto the street, so pedestrians outside can interact with the staff inside.

Lay out the rough zones on baseplates first, then start building walls. Changing a layout on a bare baseplate costs nothing; changing it after the walls are up costs a full teardown.

## Bar Counter and Seating Details

The bar is the soul of a restaurant MOC. Use dark tiles for the countertop, then pack two or three plates underneath to create a clear height step. Add a curved slope brick along the front edge as a trim, and the surface immediately reads as a different material from the floor. Inside the bar, set a few tall stools built from cylindrical pieces with a tile on top — and leave enough legroom for a minifigure to actually sit down. A stool that a figure cannot occupy is just a decoration.

Do not fill the dining area with identical tables. Mix round tables with square ones, alternate two-seat and four-seat arrangements, and tuck a potted plant or a small serving cart into a corner. That single irregularity makes the room feel furnished rather than gridded out.

## Signage and Menu Boards

The sign is what makes your restaurant identifiable from across the street. Above the door, use a clip piece to grip a tile, then apply letter stickers to spell out the shop name, and flank it with two small wall lamps. A vertical light box is another option and adds depth to a street scene at night. Keep the sign proportionate to the façade — an oversized sign flattens the whole building.

The menu board is the detail most builders skip. Hang a dark plate behind the cashier, spell out a few lines of dishes with white studs, and place a mini cash register and a printed receipt beside it. Realism jumps immediately. And do not forget a small chalkboard on the pavement outside reading something like "Today's Special" — atmosphere lives in exactly this kind of small object.

## Lighting and Atmosphere

If you want a restaurant brick MOC to feel warm, lighting is the deciding factor. Where budget and wiring allow, fit light bricks into the pendant lamp positions so warm yellow light spills through transparent elements onto the dining area — the effect is immediate. No light kit? Transparent yellow pieces standing in for lamp shades create much the same illusion of warmth, at zero wiring cost.

For the floor, alternate light and dark studs to suggest a tiled pattern. Scatter a few brick-texture pieces across the walls to suggest age, and keep the overall palette inside warm tones. Do that consistently and the cozy, busy feel comes out on its own.

## Telling a Story With Minifigures

Placing the minifigures is the last step and the most enjoyable one. The chef stands in the kitchen working a wok; the server carries a tray through the aisle; a customer flips through a menu by the window; the delivery rider waits at the counter for an order. Give each figure its own action and its own line of sight, and the scene acquires a plot. Add a cat stretched out under a table, or two customers queuing at the door, and passers-by will stop to look twice. A good restaurant MOC is really a collection of small, warm, ordinary moments.

## FAQ

**Q: How should I plan the layout of a restaurant MOC?**
A: Divide the ground floor into four zones: kitchen at the very back, separated from the dining area by a half wall or low cabinet; cashier next to the entrance; booth seating along the window; takeout counter as a street-facing window. Lay out the zones on baseplates before building walls — it prevents a lot of rework.

**Q: How do I add detail to the bar and seating?**
A: Use dark tiles for the countertop, pack two or three plates underneath to create the height step, and run a curved slope brick along the front edge. Put tall stools inside the bar and leave legroom for a seated minifigure, then mix round and square tables rather than repeating one design.

**Q: How do I build the menu board and the sign?**
A: Above the door, use a clip piece to hold a tile, add letter stickers for the shop name, and flank it with small wall lamps or a vertical light box. Behind the cashier, hang a dark plate with white studs spelling out a few dishes, and place a small chalkboard outside advertising the daily special.

**Q: How do minifigures turn the scene into a story?**
A: Give every figure an action and a viewing direction: the chef working a wok in the kitchen, the server crossing the aisle with a tray, a customer reading a menu at the window, the delivery rider waiting at the counter. Add a cat under a table or queuing customers at the door and the scene instantly has a plot.
