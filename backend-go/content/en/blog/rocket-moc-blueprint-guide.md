# Rocket MOC Blueprint Guide: Saturn V to Long March Builds

After years of building, if there is one subject I never get tired of, it is rockets. The tall cylindrical body, the smooth cone, the stacked boosters — set one on your desk and you have a miniature launch pad. Rocket MOCs look great, but once you actually start building, there is a lot of technique involved. This guide covers the subjects, the structure, and where to find blueprints, so you can go from browsing to building.

## Choosing a Classic Rocket MOC Subject

Rocket MOCs generally fall into three camps. The first is the lunar-class heavy lifter, and the flagship example is the Saturn V: a real 111-meter vehicle compressed into a few dozen centimeters of brick, with two stacked stages plus a payload fairing that creates a strong layered look. It is the kind of subject anyone recognizes at a glance. The second camp is the home-grown favorite, the Long March series, from Long March 2 through Long March 5. The orange-and-white color scheme is instantly recognizable and feels approachable, which makes it a great starting point for newcomers. The third camp is the space shuttle, whose distinguishing feature is the three-part combination of orbiter, external tank and solid rocket boosters — and once it is finished you can detach the orbiter and display it on its own, which adds a lot of play value.

For your first rocket MOC blueprint, I suggest starting with something mid-sized like a Long March. The proportions are easier to control and the cost of getting it wrong is low.

## Building the Cone, the Body and the Nozzle

The hard part of any rocket MOC is the curves. Start with the cone: the round fairing is the face of the whole rocket, so do not just stack flat plates. Use wedge bricks, curved bricks and curved plates to step inward ring by ring, shrinking layer by layer from a wide base up to the tip, and the curvature comes out far more natural.

Next, the body. The main tube is easier than the cone; the key is a consistent diameter. Pick one standard diameter and use the same size ring and cylindrical pieces throughout, then join section to section with technic elements or pins for a rigid connection. That way the model stays solid and will not fall apart the moment you pick it up.

Finally, the nozzle. The exhaust is where the model really sells itself: turn a cone element upside down, wrap a ring of gray or black slope bricks around it to suggest the engine bell, then tuck one or two small trans-clear round tiles inside the opening to suggest engine glow. The detail level jumps immediately. Build these three sections separately and assemble them at the end, and the finished model will stand on its own.

## Using Trans-Clear and Printed Parts

The premium feel of a rocket MOC often hides in part selection. Trans-clear elements are the finishing touch: trans-clear round tiles for portholes, a trans-clear curved canopy for the cockpit, and a clear part or two at the hatch and observation windows. Suddenly the model reads as a spacecraft instead of a gray column. Printed parts solve the lettering problem — the flag, the vehicle number, the NASA logo and so on. A printed brick gets you there in one step, and it sits flatter and lasts longer than a sticker. If you do not have many printed parts, you can also build the number yourself from 1x1 or 1x2 tiles, and the result holds up well.

When picking a blueprint, pay attention to how many trans-clear and printed parts appear in the parts list. The more sensibly those two categories are used, the closer the finished model will be to the real thing.

## Where to Find Rocket MOC Blueprints

My personal order of preference for sources: community platforms first, forum shares second, paid blueprint collections last. Community platforms have the advantage of volume and clear categories, and the comment sections often include feedback from people who actually built the model, which helps you avoid the ones that look good but cannot be assembled. Older forum threads also hide plenty of classic rocket MOC blueprints worth digging through. Advanced builders chasing higher accuracy can go for well-printed paid blueprints. Whatever the channel, once you have the file, check your inventory against the parts list first, then build section by section following the steps. Your success rate goes up a lot that way.
