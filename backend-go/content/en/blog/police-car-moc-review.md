# Police Car MOC Instructions Review: Design, Parts and Build

## Police Cars: Law Enforcement in Brick Form

Among all the themes builders return to, **police vehicles** never really go out of style. The patrol sedan working a city beat, the interceptor built for highway pursuit, the armored command truck rolled out for a crisis — each one comes with its own functional layout and unmistakable visual identity. A good police car MOC plan has to do more than copy the hard lines of a real cruiser; it needs clever part choices and a smart internal structure so the finished model is as playable as it is displayable.

This review looks at **police car MOC instructions** currently circulating on the main sharing platforms, judged on four things: how faithfully the vehicle is reproduced, whether the parts are actually obtainable, how the build feels to assemble, and how much play value the finished model delivers. The goal is to help you pick the plan that fits your parts bin and your skill level.

## Design Accuracy: Getting the Look Right

The real test of any police car plan is how well it reads as the actual vehicle. Strong designers tend to work from the same handful of anchor points:

**Proportions and silhouette.** Take the classic Ford Explorer police interceptor. A high-fidelity plan nails the grille shape, the raised hood lines and the angle of the rear spoiler. By combining wedge plates, slope bricks and curved elements, the designer squeezes a believable, flowing profile out of a grid of studs. Some top-tier plans go further and mount a full roof light bar using transparent red and blue elements paired with LED module kits for a convincing effect.

**Livery and markings.** The black-and-white or blue-and-white paint scheme is the hardest thing to solve in brick. Good plans either supply a detailed sticker reference sheet or use the natural colors of the parts themselves to create the two-tone split. The door badge, the "POLICE" lettering along the flanks and the emergency contact number on the rear all feed directly into how real the finished model feels. Some premium plans even include a PDF template for custom stickers so you can print your own.

**Interior and equipment.** The center console layout, steering wheel position, seat arrangement and rear equipment storage area are all fair measures of how detailed a plan really is. The most obsessive police car designs add opening doors, a pop-out trunk and a removable roof light assembly, so the model offers more than a static shelf piece.

## Part Compatibility: Common Bricks First, Specialty Parts Second

For most builders, part availability decides whether a plan is even worth starting. The mainstream police car instructions follow a sensible rule: lean on generic parts, use specialty parts sparingly.

**Structural parts.** More than 95% of the required elements are standard bricks — basic bricks, plates, slopes, wedge plates, technic pins and axles. These are available in quantity from LEGO and from Chinese brands alike, which keeps the barrier to entry low. Depending on scale, a police car MOC typically needs 800 to 2,500 parts, with a parts budget of roughly 200 to 600 RMB. That is a reasonable value proposition.

**Specialty parts.** Light bars, wheels, windshields and curved panels are the pieces that need extra attention. Some designs use specific transparent elements (a 1x2 transparent red brick, a 1x4 transparent blue brick) to simulate the light bar, or unusual wedge angles to shape the nose. Before buying a plan, check the parts list the designer provides — usually in Rebrickable or BrickLink format — and confirm you can actually source the unusual pieces.

## Build Experience: Chassis First, Body Second

A well-structured plan is also well sequenced. Take a typical city patrol cruiser plan and it usually breaks into three phases:

**Phase one: chassis and suspension.** This is the structural foundation. The designer walks you from the frame outward, then through the front and rear suspension, the steering mechanism and the driveshaft. For builders who want movement, some plans support adding Power Functions — an M motor and a servo motor — for remote-controlled driving. Chassis accuracy determines how rigid and how smooth-rolling the finished model is, and it is the most patience-testing stretch of the whole build.

**Phase two: body and interior.** With the chassis done, you wrap the body. The fun here is watching a skeleton turn into something with mass — from the dashboard, steering wheel and seats of the cabin to the side panels, hood and rear deck. Each subassembly produces visible progress. Designers usually build key structure first and fill in detail afterward, which keeps the cognitive load manageable.

**Phase three: exterior detail and police equipment.** This is the rewarding finish. The roof light bar, front and rear bumpers, mirrors, plate holder and departmental markings go on one at a time, and the model shifts from "looks like a car" to "is unmistakably a police car." Some plans throw in extra accessory builds — a removable road barrier, an onboard computer terminal — which add story and play value.

## Side by Side: Which Style of Police Car Plan Suits You

To make the choice easier, the mainstream police car instructions fall into three groups:

**City patrol series.** Compact sedans and SUVs, generally 800 to 1,500 parts, suited to builders with moderate experience. The design focus is clean body lines and complete police livery, and the finished size is easy to display. This is the recommended entry point for a first police car MOC.

**Special operations series.** SWAT armored cars, command vehicles and riot trucks. These usually run above 2,000 parts, lean heavily on technic elements and specialty panels, and are harder to build but visually dramatic. Armored car plans tend to have more complex mechanical work in the chassis and suspension, which makes them a good fit for advanced builders chasing a hardcore build.

**Film and screen replicas.** Police cars recreated from movies such as *Fast & Furious* and *Batman*. These plans allow the most creative freedom and often fold in custom modifications — nitrous setups, armored plating — that give the model instant recognition on a shelf. They demand more from the designer's imagination and from your parts stock.

## Summary and Buying Advice

The appeal of a brick police car is how neatly it joins the functional aesthetics of real law enforcement vehicles to the creativity of the building hobby. Whether you prefer the classic lines of a patrol cruiser or the mechanical heft of a special operations truck, there is a plan worth your time.

When choosing, check three things first: how well the designer's previous builds were finished and reviewed, whether the parts list is built from widely available elements, and whether the difficulty matches your experience. If this is your first attempt, start with a city patrol car plan in the 800 to 1,500 part range. It takes about one to two weeks to assemble, which gives you the full building experience without the frustration of an overreaching project.

And when it is done, share photos and build notes with the BrickPlans community — every brick police car carries a bit of the designer's and the builder's shared enthusiasm. We look forward to seeing yours pull up.
