# How to Read an MOC Parts List: Check Bricks Without Missing Pieces

The most frustrating moment in any MOC build isn't a structural problem you can't solve. It's sitting down with the instructions, spreading the parts list on the table, and discovering you're short. Over the years I've hit enough missing-piece snags to circle the table with them, so here's everything I know about reading an MOC parts list properly.

## What a Good MOC Parts List Should Contain

Let's start with what counts as a "passable" list. A reliable MOC parts list needs at least three things: **part number**, **quantity** and **color**. Lists that give a number but no color are the worst offenders — the same part number looks identical in black, white and gray, and without a color callout you simply can't place an order. More advanced lists also separate "special parts" from "common parts," pulling printed elements and rare pieces into their own section so you can judge up front how hard the model will be to replicate.

## How to Verify Your Stock and Prepare Spares

Don't order the moment the list lands in your hands. My habit is to sort the loose parts I already own first, rough-sorting them by color into storage boxes, then ticking off the list item by item. Two things deserve extra attention during the check. First, **round up**: if the list says "2x2 brick x10" and you only have 8, note it and top up in one batch later. Second, **check repeated parts separately**: a large MOC can run into the thousands of pieces, and the same part may appear in several different steps, so it's better to verify step by step rather than sorting everything at once. For spares, I'd suggest stocking roughly 5% extra of your common parts — especially basics like 1x2 and 1x4 bricks and technic elements. They're cheap, and they save you at exactly the wrong moment.

## Where to Buy Parts

My personal ranking for part sources is: **official brand stores on e-commerce platforms**, then **third-party parts sellers**, then **local builder groups**. Official stores ship fast and handle after-sales reliably, which makes them ideal for urgent gaps. Third-party sellers carry the widest range — rare and printed parts are usually findable — and their pricing is flexible. Local builder groups are the cheapest route of all: plenty of people sell off entire collections when they leave the hobby, and buying loose parts by weight turns up some genuinely good finds. Before you check out, go through every part number and color on the list one more time — don't order on gut feeling.

## Small Habits That Prevent Missing Pieces

A few techniques I use every time. First, **check before you order**: count your stock clearly, buy only what's missing, and don't hoard a pile of parts you'll never use. Second, **overstock the wear-prone parts**: hinges and connection pins take the load and are the first to loosen or snap, so a few extras are always worth it. Third, **leave shipping time on the calendar**: a large MOC often needs two or three restock rounds, so don't schedule your build too tightly. Do these three things and you'll sidestep about 90% of the missing-piece embarrassment.
