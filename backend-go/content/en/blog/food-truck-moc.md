# Food Truck MOC Guide: Fold-Out Counter and Kitchen Details

Bottom line first: lock in the scale before you design any features. At minifigure scale, aim for a body 6 to 8 studs wide and 24 to 32 studs long, build the chassis frame from Technic elements, then use hinges and slide rails to create the fold-out serving counter.

## Why a food truck is a great MOC subject

The food truck is one of the most photogenic subjects in brick building. It is a manageable size, packed with functions, and sits somewhere between a vehicle and a building — which means you can cram in a huge amount of everyday detail. A well-built food truck MOC parked next to a street scene is instantly a focal point. Better still, the fold-out feature gives a designer real room to play: flip open the side panel and the whole kitchen interior is revealed. That one-vehicle-two-states gimmick is exactly what makes brick building fun.

Before you pick up a single brick, decide the scale. At minifigure scale, keep the width to 6–8 studs and the length to 24–32 studs. That gives you genuine room to move inside without making the truck look bloated. Once the scale is fixed, parts planning has something to work from.

## Chassis and wheels: start with Technic

The chassis is the skeleton of any food truck MOC. Build the frame from Technic elements: two long beams (11-hole or 15-hole works well) as the main longitudinal rails, braced in the middle with cross connectors to form a stable H-shaped structure. For the axles, connect the wheels directly with cross-hole Technic axle and bush assemblies. Steering does not need to be complicated — run a single axle straight through the front wheels with a steering knuckle, and focus on making the whole thing roll smoothly.

Wheel size matters. Food trucks usually run small wheels (around 30 mm diameter) with mudguards; for an American retro diner look, swap in white wide tyres. Build the mudguards from curved pieces — 4x4 curved plates or 1x4 curved bricks stacked in layers — to get that rounded wheel-arch line. Keep chassis height to 2–3 bricks. Any lower and the suspension looks wrong; any higher and it starts reading as an off-roader.

## The fold-out counter: hinges and slide rails together

The fold-out counter is the soul of this food truck MOC. The most common approach is a side flap: mount hinge elements (the kind with locking positions, like door hinges) at the bottom of the side wall. The flap sits vertical when stowed and horizontal when deployed, propped at the edge with 1x2 plates with handles or small struts, and you have an instant serving counter.

If you want the unfolding to feel more like a ceremony, try a slide-rail solution: pair 2x2 plates with grooves and 1x2 sliders so the counter slides outward first and then folds down. It is a slightly more complex mechanism, but the deployed counter has more depth and can hold more display props. One thing to watch: leave at least half a brick of clearance around every moving part, otherwise surface friction will make opening and closing feel gritty. Lay the counter top in tiles — it looks cleaner and gives minifigures somewhere stable to put their burgers and hot dogs.

## Kitchen details: SNOT and sideways building

Kitchen detail is where food truck MOCs separate themselves. Interior space is tight, which is exactly when SNOT (Studs Not On Top) earns its keep — mount parts sideways so you can build counters, shelving and hanging rails on vertical walls. A row of 1x1 bricks with side studs holding 1x2 tiles becomes a spice rack; 1x1 bricks with holes plus short axles will hang spatulas and small frying pans.

The stove is the heart of the kitchen: a 2x2 black tile for the hob surface, a 1x1 round tile on top to suggest a burner, and a transparent curved piece as a pot lid — the detail level jumps immediately. You do not need to fully enclose the fridge or sink. Use sideways building to make a half-open cabinet at the rear of the body, faced with 1x2 tiles as doors: fewer parts, better result. An extractor hood can be suggested with an inverted 1x2 slope, with an exhaust pipe (flex hose, or 1x1 cylinder bricks stacked) running up to the roof. It looks great.

## Color advice and common problems

For color, go with a large area of main color plus high-contrast accents. Pick a retro main color — white, red or mint green — use dark gray for the chassis and wheel arches, then accent the signage and awning in yellow or orange. Build the roof awning in a wavy profile from curved pieces; it is the signature element of an American-style food truck.

A few traps to avoid. First, always test-fit moving parts before you fix them down, or you will discover the interference after everything is locked in. Second, tile the interior edges so minifigures do not snag on studs when they step inside. Third, keep an eye on overall weight — do not stack too many layers of Technic beams in the chassis, or the wheels will sag. Studying someone else's structure and then modifying it into your own version is the fastest way to improve.

## FAQ

**Q: What scale should a food truck MOC use?**
A: At minifigure scale, keep the width to 6–8 studs and the length to 24–32 studs. That gives you real interior room to work with without making the truck look bloated.

**Q: How do you build the fold-out counter?**
A: Use hinges for the flip and slide rails for the pull-out: the counter sits flush against the body when closed, swings outward during service and is supported by struts. Leave play in the hinge, and add a stop piece so the counter does not droop when open.

**Q: What techniques work best for the kitchen details?**
A: Use SNOT and sideways building to turn brick sides outward and create flat work surfaces, then stack small parts into a stove, sink and shelving. Once the detail density comes up, the truck immediately feels lived in.

**Q: What colors should a food truck be?**
A: One main color, one supporting color, plus accents. Use a bright main body color (red or blue), contrast the counter and lower body trim in white, then add small touches of yellow or metallic. It will stand out in a street scene.
