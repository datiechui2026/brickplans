# LEGO Gear Transmission Guide: Ratios, Reduction and Reversing

After finishing a stack of static buildings, most builders suddenly want to make something that moves — a steering off-roader, a crane that lifts, a Ferris wheel that turns on its own. What those models share isn't a huge parts count; it's the gear system underneath. LEGO gear transmissions are easier than they look: get a few core concepts right and power flows smoothly from the motor to every part that needs to move. Below we go from gear types to gear ratios, speed reduction and direction changes, so you can get your first drivetrain running.

## Know the common gear types

Before building a LEGO gear transmission, get familiar with the usual suspects.

- **Spur gears** are the most basic. Their teeth are parallel to the axle, so they carry power between two axles on the same plane.
- **Bevel gears** have angled teeth and turn the drive direction by 90 degrees — the go-to part for steering and chassis work.
- **Crown gears** are similar to bevel gears and are often used for lightweight right-angle drives.
- **Worm gears** are special: a worm can drive the gear it meshes with, but that gear cannot drive the worm back. That built-in self-locking is ideal for mechanisms that must hold their position.

Knowing which gear suits which job is step one for any LEGO gear transmission.

## Gear ratio, speed and torque

The core concept of any gear train is the gear ratio. For two meshing gears, the tooth count ratio determines how speed and torque trade off:

- **Small gear driving a large gear** gives speed reduction and torque increase — what you want for parts that turn slowly with force, like a crane winch.
- **Large gear driving a small gear** gives speed increase and torque reduction — what you want for fast-spinning parts like fans and flywheels.

When you chain several gear pairs together, the ratios multiply stage by stage, so you can achieve a very large reduction overall. In practice, a motor driving a large gear, which then drives a small gear, gives you an output that is both smooth and strong after two stages. That multiplying effect is where the fun of LEGO gear transmissions really lives.

## Reversing direction and adding a clutch

The simplest way to reverse a motor's output is to add an idler gear. Drop one idler between two gears and the output direction flips; the idler doesn't change the gear ratio at all, only the rotation direction. That makes it a common trick for doors, robot arms and other mechanisms that need two-way motion.

A differential lets the two wheels on an axle spin at different speeds: in a turn the outer wheel travels farther than the inner one, which is why nearly every vehicle MOC reserves space for one.

Go a step further and you can use a clutch gear as a safety device. When the load gets too heavy it slips automatically, preventing the teeth from stripping and making the whole drivetrain more durable.

## Hands-on: build a simple geared car

With the theory covered, let's build. You need two wheels, one motor, a handful of spur gears and some axles.

1. Decide the transmission layout: fit an 8-tooth gear on the motor shaft and mesh it with a 24-tooth gear. The ratio is roughly 3:1 — speed reduction with a torque increase, so the car pulls away with more force.
2. Add one idler stage to route the power back to the rear axle, giving the motor and rear axle an independent drivetrain.
3. Follow the instructions and watch the gear spacing: the teeth must mesh fully, with no binding or grinding when you turn the axle by hand.
4. Fit the battery box, switch on, and test. Your first gear-driven car is done — and for many builders that's the starting point of the whole LEGO gear transmission journey.

## Designing more complex transmissions

Once the basics are solid, take on harder systems: use universal joints to change the angle of a driveshaft, bevel gears to move power from a longitudinal axis to a transverse one, or combine several gear stages into a gearbox.

Before you build, sketch the power path on paper: which gears the motor drives in order, what each ratio is, and whether the final speed and torque match what you expect. Referencing high-resolution instructions from official LEGO software or from BrickPlans (https://brickplan.cn) will noticeably raise your success rate.
