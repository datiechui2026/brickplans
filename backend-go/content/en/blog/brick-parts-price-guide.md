# How Brick Part Prices Are Set: A MOC Buyer's Guide

Half the fun of building a MOC is the design work — the other half is hunting down the parts. Plenty of newcomers sit down with a parts list for the first time and hit a wall: the same 2x4 brick can cost a few cents from one seller and a few dollars from another. So how are brick part prices actually set? Here is what I have learned from years of overpaying, under-planning and slowly getting it right.

## What Determines the Price of a Brick Part

Three things dominate the cost of any single part: the mould, the colour, and how rare it is. Basic bricks come from moulds that run constantly, so the tooling cost is spread across an enormous output and the per-part price stays low. Specialised connectors, transparent elements and printed parts use expensive moulds with small production runs, which pushes the unit price up. Colour matters just as much — an unusual shade has less demand and fewer production batches, so it usually costs noticeably more than a common one. Size and material play a role too: Technic parts and gears that have to hold structural tolerances are generally worth more than a plain brick.

## Single Parts, Loose Lots, Clone Parts and Retired Parts

The biggest price splits in brick part pricing come from the format you buy in:

- **Single new parts**: sold individually by the official source. Pricing is transparent and basic elements are affordable, but rare parts get expensive fast.
- **Loose lots (parted-out or secondhand)**: builders break up whole sets and sell the contents. The unit cost is often well below buying individually, which makes this the main way MOC builders recover budget — though condition and wear vary a lot.
- **Clone parts (compatible third-party)**: the cheapest tier by far, usually a fraction of the official equivalent. Good for tight budgets, but watch for colour mismatch and inconsistent clutch.
- **Retired parts (out of production)**: the most expensive tier. Once a mould has been idle for years, remaining stock shrinks and prices climb. Some rare retired parts trade for dozens of times the price of an ordinary element.

Once you understand these tiers, it makes sense why the same MOC instructions can cost one builder a couple of hundred dollars and another well over a thousand.

## How to Budget Parts for a MOC

Don't start ordering the moment you open the instructions. Work through it in this order: first, list every part type and quantity from the parts list in the instructions; next, sort that list into three piles — "already in my bins", "must buy new", and "can substitute with compatible parts"; finally, price each category and total it up. Pay closest attention to parts that are both expensive per unit and used in high quantities — on most MOCs, the budget is concentrated in a dozen or twenty specialised elements.

Leaving some headroom is important. Broken parts during assembly and colour-mismatch returns are both common, so a ten percent cushion is a sensible buffer. If the total blows past your limit, swap the decorative parts to compatible versions first and keep official parts for load-bearing and core structure.

## Where You Buy Changes the Price Most

The same part can differ several-fold in price depending on the channel. Broadly there are three: official online stores, which guarantee authenticity but charge high unit prices and suit small top-ups of scarce parts; secondhand parted-out sales and builder groups, which offer the best value and are where many veteran builders hunt loose parts; and third-party loose-part shops on e-commerce platforms, which are the friendliest on price and suit bulk purchases of basic bricks. Compare a part across several sellers before committing — not paying the first price you see is the single most effective way to save.

## Common Pricing Mistakes Beginners Make

A few mistakes I see over and over. First, chasing the lowest unit price and ignoring shipping and minimum-order thresholds, so a small order ends up costing more. Second, buying every single part new and official, which quietly inflates the bill for no benefit. Third, panic-hoarding anything labelled "retired" — in reality many retired parts circulate on the secondhand market continuously and the prices are not as dramatic as they look. Higher price does not mean better; the right part for the job is what matters.

Once your budget and parts list are sorted, the next thing you need is reliable high-resolution instructions. If you would rather skip hunting across the internet, head to https://brickplan.cn, which collects a good number of high-resolution brick MOC instructions — working from the parts list there makes sourcing much smoother. Collecting the parts is part of the MOC hobby in itself. Take it slowly and you will get a feel for it.

## FAQ

**Q: Why do identical brick parts vary so much in price?**

A: Mostly three factors: mould, colour and rarity. Basic bricks come from high-volume moulds with the lowest amortised cost, so they are cheap. Special connectors, transparent elements and printed parts use costly moulds with small runs, so unit prices are high. The more unusual the colour, the more you pay.

**Q: How do single parts, loose lots, clone parts and retired parts differ in price?**

A: Single new parts are sold individually by the official source with transparent pricing, but rare parts are expensive. Loose lots (parted-out or secondhand) often cost much less per unit than buying individually. Clone parts are the cheapest. Retired parts carry the highest scarcity premium — the same instructions can run from a few hundred to over a thousand dollars depending on which tier you buy.

**Q: How should I budget parts for a MOC?**

A: List every part type and quantity from the instructions, then split them into "already have", "must buy new" and "can substitute with compatible parts". Add a ten percent buffer for breakage and returns, and if you go over budget, replace decorative parts with compatible versions first.

**Q: What are the most common pricing mistakes when buying brick parts?**

A: Three: watching only the unit price and ignoring shipping and minimum-order thresholds, so small orders cost more; buying every part new and official and overspending; and impulse-hoarding anything marked "retired" when plenty of those parts still circulate steadily on the secondhand market.
