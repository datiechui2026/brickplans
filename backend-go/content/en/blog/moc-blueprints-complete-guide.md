# MOC Blueprints: How to Read and Use Any Plan

**A MOC blueprint is two documents in one: a build sequence and a parts list. Read the parts list first, price it, then read the sequence — and always build sub-assembly boxes separately before attaching them. Skip any of those three and you will discover the problem at the point where the model is hardest to open up again.**

MOC stands for My Own Creation: a model a builder designed rather than one that shipped in a box. A good plan is effectively a teacher, because it encodes decisions you have not had to make yet — how a heavy roof is supported, where a joint should be hidden, which parts carry the load.

## What a complete blueprint contains

| Component | What it gives you | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Title and finished photos | The target | Photographed model, not only a render |
| Part count and step count | Size and density | Density matters more than size |
| Parts list with quantities | Purchase and verification | Quantities must match the sequence |
| Overview image | Proportions and orientation | Front, back and underside if available |
| Step sequence with callouts | The build itself | Readable at zoom |
| Sub-assembly boxes | Separate clusters to build first | Some plans mark them inconsistently |
| Colour notes or substitutions | Alternatives when a colour is unavailable | Any notes on retired colours |
| Version or revision date | Which file to build | Newest revision wins |

If two or three of those are missing, you are holding a design sketch rather than a plan. That is fine for inspiration and wrong for a 900-part purchase order.

## Formats, and when each one is the right choice

- **PDF** — the default. Prints, zooms, works offline, and is usually the highest-resolution version of the plan. Choose this for anything over 500 parts.
- **Image series** — convenient on a phone, but there is no parts list and no page order to trust. Acceptable as a secondary reference while a PDF does the real work.
- **3D design file (`.io`, `.ldr`)** — essential when you intend to modify the model, because the parts list recalculates automatically after every edit. Open it in a free editor; every change updates your shopping list.

## How to read a blueprint: a six-pass routine

Do these passes in order. They take about fifteen minutes and save hours.

1. **Parts list first, not the steps.** Total the parts, note the rare colours, and check how many are parts you already own. This is also your budget calculation.
2. **Skim the whole sequence once.** You are not reading details; you are finding the hard sections — the ones with many small parts, or a step where the model is turned over.
3. **Identify every sub-assembly box** and build each cluster before continuing. Building a cluster in place is the single most common cause of mid-build dismantling.
4. **Locate mirrored sections.** Where the plan shows one wing, one leg or one tower, assume two and count the callouts. Missing a multiplier typically costs one unsolvable step later.
5. **Study the underside.** Roughly a third of MOC plans have a structural layer that is invisible in the finished photos. Check it before your parts are allocated.
6. **Work out where the load goes.** Find the heaviest element — the roof, the tower, the engine block — and trace the route to the base. If the plan leans on one 2x2 column, plan to reinforce it yourself.

## Estimating cost, time and difficulty

Rough bands that hold up in practice:

| Part count | Build time | Parts cost (common parts) | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 300 | 1–2 hours | 10–25 USD | First build, technique practice |
| 300–800 | 3–6 hours | 25–70 USD | Beginners with one build done |
| 800–2,000 | 8–15 hours | 70–200 USD | Confident builders |
| Over 2,000 | 20 hours and up | 200 USD and up | Display builds, Technic |

Three refinements that matter more than the headline number:

- **Cost is set by the parts you lack**, not by the total. A 1,500-part plan where you own 1,100 parts is a 400-part order.
- **Rare colours dominate the bill.** One discontinued colour can account for a third of the total cost of a model.
- **Step density predicts frustration.** A 900-part plan with 160 steps is far more demanding than a 1,200-part plan with 90 steps.

## Red flags in a plan

1. **No photographed finished model** — nothing to check progress against.
2. **A parts list that is a round number** — "about 1,000 parts" usually means it was never verified.
3. **No underside view** on a plan that stacks weight upward.
4. **Very low file weight** for a very large model, which indicates downscaled step images.
5. **No revision history** on a plan that has clearly been updated, so you cannot tell which parts list goes with which sequence.
6. **Steps that add many parts with one flat diagram** and no exploded view — you will be guessing, and guessing means rebuilding.

## Splitting a large plan into sessions

A 2,000-part plan is not one build, it is five or six. Split it deliberately rather than stopping when you get tired:

- **Break at sub-assembly boundaries.** A finished cluster can be set aside for a week; half a cluster usually cannot.
- **Finish each session by attaching what you built.** Loose clusters on the desk get knocked, mixed and lost.
- **Note the step number on a sticky note** on the model. Coming back after a gap, finding your place in a 200-step plan takes longer than the first ten steps did.
- **Leave the next step's parts out, sorted.** Starting the next session with the parts already picked up removes the cold-start problem entirely.
- **Reprice at each break.** If the next section needs a part you do not own, order it now instead of the evening you want to continue.

Most abandoned builds are abandoned at session three, and the usual reason is a split that destroyed the structure rather than a loss of interest.

## What to do when a step makes no sense

Five fixes, in the order worth trying:

1. **Look at the next step.** Its silhouette shows which parts are already in place, which usually resolves the ambiguity in the current one.
2. **Count the parts you are meant to add.** If a step says three parts and you have two, the missing one is the confusion.
3. **Check for a mirrored twin.** The instruction may be about the other side, drawn once.
4. **Compare against the finished photos**, zoomed into the corresponding area. Photographs resolve orientation questions that flat diagrams leave open.
5. **Set the sub-assembly down flat and look from the side.** A surprising share of ambiguous steps are about depth, not shape, and the side view answers them immediately.

## Five beginner mistakes, and the fix for each

1. **Placing a bulk parts order before reading the list.** Fix: read the list, identify the top 20 part types by quantity, and buy those first.
2. **Skipping the sub-assembly boxes.** Fix: build each cluster separately on a tray, then attach.
3. **Sorting by colour.** Fix: sort by part type — instructions ask for types, and colour is noise once the parts are on the table.
4. **Not counting multiples.** Fix: count every "×4" and "×6" in the callouts before assembly starts.
5. **Working from a phone on a dense build.** Fix: tablet or printout at 100%, with the parts list open beside it.

## Working with plan revisions

Plans change. Stepped corrections are common as builders report problems, and a revised plan may include a stronger joint, a different part, or a corrected quantity. Practical rules: build the newest revision; keep the older file if you have already bought parts against it, since quantities may differ; and if a step in your version contradicts the overall photos, trust the photos and check for an update — the photos were usually shot from a fixed design.

## A first-project plan

Pick a plan between 300 and 600 parts with a photographed model and a clean parts list. Budget about four hours. Before starting, sort the parts by type into shallow trays, build every sub-assembly box separately, and check the underside structure against the plan. Do that once and the second build takes half the time.

## FAQ

**Q:** What is the difference between a MOC blueprint and a set manual?
**A:** A set manual comes from a production design and is fully verified. A MOC blueprint comes from an individual designer, so quality varies — the parts list, the finished photos and the callout readability are your quality test.

**Q:** Can I build a MOC with non-LEGO parts?
**A:** Yes, if the parts follow the standard stud geometry, which most compatible brands do. Watch for colour differences on a visible face and keep each structural sub-assembly to one brand so the clutch feels consistent.

**Q:** Do I need special software to open a MOC file?
**A:** Free editors open every common format. You only need them for 3D files; PDF plans need nothing more than a reader that can zoom.

**Q:** How do I know if a plan is too hard for me?
**A:** Divide the part count by the step count. Under about six parts per step is beginner-friendly; above ten means dense sub-assemblies and a lot of small-part work.

Start with a plan you can finish rather than one that impresses. The [BrickPlan blueprint library](https://brickplan.cn/en/blog) lists plans by theme and part count, and every plan page shows the parts used before you commit.
