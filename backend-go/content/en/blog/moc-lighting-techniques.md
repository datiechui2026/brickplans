# Brick MOC Lighting Techniques: A Practical Guide

Here's the short answer up front: cinematic shots of your MOC come from light, not gear. A three-point setup, a window, and a few cheap LED panels are all it takes.

Plenty of builders assemble a genuinely good MOC and then end up with photos that look flat and ordinary. The problem is rarely the camera, it's the light. This guide walks through brick MOC scene lighting from the ground up, using low-cost equipment throughout, and you can follow it step by step to push your builds up a level.

## Why Lighting Matters More Than Gear

Bricks are plastic: smooth, highly reflective, and quick to blow out into white or crush into black if the light is even slightly off. A phone or an entry-level camera is good enough. What actually decides the final image is the direction, hardness and color temperature of the light. Don't rush to upgrade your equipment — put the desk lamps and curtains you already own to work first and the difference shows up immediately.

## Three-Point Lighting: The Most Reliable Foundation

Three-point lighting comes from film and TV, and it works just as well on miniature scenes. The key light illuminates the subject and describes its structure. The fill light sits on the opposite side to recover detail in the shadows. The rim light comes in from behind to separate the build from its background.

For a small scene you can pull this off with one desk lamp as the key, a sheet of white paper as a reflector for fill, and a flashlight for the rim. When you place them, watch the height of each source — sitting slightly above the camera's viewpoint reads as more natural.

## Window Light: The Best Free Source

Window light on a sunny morning or afternoon is a free softbox. Turn the scene so it faces the window side-on and the light creates a pleasing transition from highlight to shadow. If the sun is too hard, draw a white gauze curtain to soften it. Remember to switch off the other lights in the room, otherwise mixed sources will skew the color temperature and leave a muddy cast.

## LED Panels and Color Temperature: Cheap Gear, Good Results

LED panels costing a few dozen yuan are the value champion here — two of them give you a complete set of light positions. Color temperature is the thing to watch. At 3200K the light runs warm, which suits cozy subjects like street scenes and cafés. At 5500K it's close to daylight, which suits modern architecture and cool-toned scenes. Many panels support bi-color mixing, so you can aim one warm and one cool at each other for a richly layered result. Keep the brightness modest — just short of blowing out the frame — and add a softbox or tracing paper to make the light quality gentler.

## Backlight and Light Position: Build Atmosphere, Avoid Harsh Shadows

Backlight is the atmosphere tool. Put the source behind the scene and it traces a silhouette outline around the architecture; a dusk street or forest shadows suddenly look cinematic. Direct frontal light is the fastest way to produce hard black shadows. Try lowering the lamp, moving it to the side, or bouncing white cardstock into the shadow side — the shadows soften and the build actually reads as more three-dimensional.

## Macro Photography and Post-Processing: Depth of Field, Small Apertures, Simple Grading

Depth of field is extremely shallow in macro shooting. If you want the whole scene sharp, stop the aperture down to f/8 or beyond, or lock focus using your phone's pro mode. Then move into post: correct white balance first, fine-tune exposure and shadows next, and finish with a touch of sharpening and grain to suggest a real-world sense of scale. Post is the icing — the light positions you chose beforehand decide whether the shot works.

There are no standard answers in lighting. Try several positions, shoot a set of comparisons, and you'll gradually build your own instinct for it. I hope these brick MOC lighting techniques help — share your lit shots and what you learned on brickplan.

## FAQ

**Q: I only have a 100-yuan desk lamp — how do I light a brick MOC?**
A: Use it as the key light at roughly 45 degrees from above and the side to illuminate the subject, put a white paper reflector on the other side to lift the shadows, then bring a flashlight in from behind for the rim. The whole setup comes in under 100 yuan.

**Q: Should I choose a 3200K or 5500K LED panel, and which one for street scenes?**
A: 3200K runs warm and suits cozy subjects like street scenes and cafés. 5500K is close to daylight and suits modern architecture and cool-toned scenes. Aiming one warm and one cool panel at each other gives the richest layering.

**Q: Direct frontal light on bricks always leaves harsh black shadows. How do I avoid that?**
A: Lower the light and move it to the side, or bounce white cardstock into the shadow side. The shadows soften and the build looks more solid. Direct frontal light is the worst offender for hard black shadows, so avoid it where you can.

**Q: My macro shots of the whole brick scene come out blurry. What aperture and post settings should I use?**
A: Stop the aperture down past f/8, or lock focus in your phone's pro mode. In post, correct white balance first, then adjust exposure and shadows, and finish with light sharpening. Light position matters more than post-processing.
