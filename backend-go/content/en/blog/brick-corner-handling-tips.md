# Brick Corner Techniques: Stronger, Cleaner MOC Builds

In brick MOC building, corners are the most overlooked part of a model and the one most likely to fail. Whether it is the right-angle turn of an exterior wall or the curved transition on a tower or dome, **brick corner handling** decides both how solid the build feels and how good it looks. Plenty of beginner models fall apart at a touch or end up with walls that will not line up, and the cause is almost always the corner structure. This article covers the practical techniques for handling corners in brick builds.

## Why Corner Structure Matters So Much

The clutch between bricks comes from friction between the studs and tubes, and the corner is where stress concentrates. A straight run of wall interlocks layer after layer, so it is naturally stable. But if a corner is just two walls butted vertically against each other, the interlocking chain breaks at the bend, creating the weakest point in the whole wall. The corner is also a visual focal point — the eye reads a model's lines and silhouette at the corners first. So **brick corner handling** is about structural safety and overall appearance at the same time.

## Right-Angle Corners: From Stacking to Interlocking

The most common right-angle corner is the exterior wall of a boxy house. The classic beginner mistake is letting the two walls each go their own way at the corner, simply touching. The correct approach is to overlap the bricks across the joint: place a 2x2 brick on the first layer as an anchor, then on the second layer use 2x1 bricks that straddle the two walls, forming a bond like masonry. This spreads the pull from both directions across neighbouring bricks and makes a right-angle corner far more rigid.

For a cleaner corner, use a 1x1 corner brick, or a 1x1 brick combined with a column, so the facade shows a neat rounded edge or a crisp arris. One core rule of **brick corner handling** is this: corner bricks should straddle both walls wherever possible, not just sit against one of them.

## Curved Corners and Round Towers

Curved walls and round towers are required coursework for the more advanced MOC builder. The key to a circle is using slope bricks and curved slopes to step the angle gradually layer by layer, expanding outward by half a stud or a full stud each time and filling the gaps with bricks of different lengths, so the stacked layers naturally form a smooth arc.

A tower corner takes lateral pull and vertical load at the same time, so it is worth embedding a technic axle inside, or building a double-layer structure of brick plus plate sandwiches. For smaller-diameter corners, such as a round tower 4-6 studs wide, round bricks work well, paired with a turntable base for a rotating design that is both sturdy and fun. ## Reinforcing Corners: Don't Let One Weak Point Sink the Building

Whatever the corner type, reinforcement is unavoidable. A few field-tested ideas:

- **Stiffener**: add a vertical support column on the inside of the corner, such as a 1x2 brick stack or an axle, bracing the corner from within and clearly improving resistance to twisting.
- **Sandwich method**: every 3-4 layers, insert a layer of plates at the corner to form a ring-beam-like structure that ties the corner to the walls on both sides.
- **Top tie-down**: always cap the corner with a lintel or top plate above it, otherwise bricks above will come loose when the model is moved. On multi-storey buildings, each floor's corner needs a load-bearing point directly below it.

Also, keep the interlocking direction of all corner studs consistent when placing bricks, and avoid half-engaged, floating connections — that is the real reason a model feels loose and wobbles.

## Corner Decoration: Details That Lift the Finish

Beyond structure, **brick corner handling** is also about good looks. Right-angle corners can take columns, eaves or wall lamps to give the silhouette more depth; curved corners suit gradient colour-blocking with slope bricks in different shades to imitate stonework texture. Small parts such as window frames and flagpoles can also be set into the corner to break up a monotonous line.

## Closing Thoughts: Corners Test Your MOC Skill

Getting **brick corner handling** right takes observation, teardowns and practice. Next time you build, start with a small right-angle corner or a round tower and experiment repeatedly with the angle of the interlocking bond and the reinforcement method. On [BrickPlans](https://brickplan.cn) you can find plenty of plans showing excellent corner work, so follow the experts and level up step by step. Remember, there is no single correct answer for **brick corner handling** — if the structure is solid and the proportions are right, it is a good corner. Give it a try.
