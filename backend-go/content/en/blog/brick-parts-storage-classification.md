# Brick Parts Storage and Sorting Guide for Loose-Piece Builders

Anyone who has bought loose parts for a while learns the same lesson: building is fun, sorting is not. Once everything is jumbled together, hunting down a single 1x1 brick can mean digging through an entire bin. This guide covers sorting standards, a review of storage products and workspace layout, so you can build a system that actually survives contact with real life.

## Sort First: What Standard to Use

The sorting standard decides whether the whole system works. The most practical approach is sorting by shape — separate bricks, plates, slopes, wheels, minifigures and specialty parts, because shape gets you to the right container faster than color does. Color sorting looks tidy but performs badly, since the part counts within any single color are rarely balanced. A good default: primarily by shape, secondarily by color, and within one shape group split out the colors you use most.

If you build one theme repeatedly — architecture or vehicles, for example — add a "purpose" layer and keep the parts you reach for constantly in their own group. Don't over-divide, though. Too many levels are harder to maintain than they are to use.

## Storage Product Reviews

I have used all the common categories on the market. Each has trade-offs:

- **Large sorting cases:** Best for high-volume everyday parts such as standard bricks and plates. A clear body lets you spot contents at a glance.
- **Drawer cabinets:** Modular and stackable, with each drawer narrowing down to a sub-category. This is the main workhorse for advanced builders; the downside is the floor space it eats.
- **Pill boxes / compartment boxes:** Made for small parts. Gears, pins and small elements each get a compartment, and the boxes are compact enough to tuck away.
- **Label makers:** Label every storage slot and you never have to open a lid to check. The efficiency gain is huge over the long run — strongly recommended.

## Workspace Layout

The guiding principle for a storage workspace is reach. Put the parts you use most within arm's length of the building surface, and push specialty and rarely used parts further out. Keep a clean patch of desk free for assembly, arrange the boxes in category order, and put things back the moment you finish with them. Light it well, and choose clear containers — paired with labels, they save a lot of eye strain.

## Plans for Different Collection Sizes

- **Small scale (a few hundred to 1,000 parts):** One or two sorting cases plus a pill box is enough. Don't overspend.
- **Medium scale (several thousand parts):** Drawer cabinets as the base, plus a label maker, with main drawers divided by shape.
- **Large scale (tens of thousands of parts):** Zoning plus theme grouping, a full wall of cabinets, and a numbering system so that storing and retrieving both follow rules.

## Build the Habit of Putting Things Back

A perfect system fails without follow-through. The core habit is "put it back as you go" — at the end of each build stage, return the loose parts to their slots instead of saving it all for "later when I have time." Tape a parts diagram to each container so that family members helping you tidy up can identify the contents too. Do a short inventory once a month or so and pull out anything that has drifted into the wrong box.

A well-organized parts library is an asset in itself. When you can find any single element in ten seconds, building a MOC feels completely different. Start giving your loose parts a home today.
