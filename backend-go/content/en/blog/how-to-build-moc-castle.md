# How to Build a MOC Castle: Step-by-Step From Base to Towers

The short version first: build the base before the towers. Sandwich plates and bricks in the chassis so it stays flat and does not curl at the edges, build walls as a double layer with offset joints, raise round towers by stacking curved parts while rotating each course, and check at the end that every spire tops out at the same height.

Castles are the most classic MOC subject and also the easiest to wreck: big silhouette, lots of parts, dense detail, and if the structure is wrong you end up with collapsing walls halfway through. This tutorial splits a castle into four stages — base, walls, towers, roof — and gives the structural and shaping priorities for each one. Beginners can follow it end to end.

## Before You Start: Pick a Scale and a Style

Answer two questions before you touch a brick: **how big, and in what style**. Scale decides your part count and how the core is built; style decides colour and detail direction.

- Micro castle (300–600 pieces): the silhouette and the tower profiles carry the build;
- Mid-size castle (1000–2000 pieces): enough for full curtain walls, a drawbridge and a courtyard;
- Large diorama (3000+ pieces): needs an internal frame and modular sections.

On style, grey brick with a dark roof reads as realistic, off-white with a red roof reads as fairytale. Pick one palette and carry it through the whole build so the colours never look scrambled.

## Step 1: The Base — Flat and Stable First

Everything in the castle sits on the base. Build the footprint from large plates and choose an even-stud dimension (32x32 or 48x48, for example) so symmetric layouts stay easy later. Three things matter:

1. **Thicken the base in layers**: a single layer of plates is too flexible, so add a layer of bricks in the middle as a sandwich to stop the edges curling.
2. **Leave an internal cavity**: if you want an interior scene or lighting later, reserve routing space at the base stage.
3. **Mark your axes**: use hidden bricks in a different colour to mark the centre line and tower positions so symmetry does not drift.

## Step 2: The Walls — Offset Bonding and Slopes for Texture

A curtain wall is not a flat panel; it is a structure with thickness. Build two layers of brick, inner and outer, filling or hollowing the space between, and use **offset bonding** on large surfaces (stagger the joints between courses) to tie the whole thing together.

Three shaping tricks that lift the texture:

- **Slopes at the bottom**: use sloped plates at the wall foot to flare the stone footing outward, and the castle instantly looks rooted in the ground;
- **Relief in the middle**: use plates to create surface variation and merlons instead of one flat face;
- **Crenellations on top**: space 1x2 bricks at consistent intervals to form the battlements.

## Step 3: The Towers — Round and Octagonal Sections

Towers are the visual focus of a castle. You cannot fake a round tower with square bricks; the standard approach is to **ring curved parts into a circular cross-section**, then raise it course by course with a rotational offset to read as a cylinder. Key points:

- Offset each course by one unit before stacking so the vertical joints do not line up and the circle reads better;
- Close the top with curved and conical parts, and check the cone is actually centred;
- Reinforce the joint between tower and main wall with technic pins or connectors so nothing hangs in mid-air.

For four corner towers, build two diagonal towers first and confirm they match in height before doing the other two — far more reliable than raising all four at once.

## Step 4: Roofs, Windows, Doors and Finishing

Build roof pitches from slopes and curved parts, keeping the slope direction consistent. Dress doors and windows with arch and fence parts, and place them on the principle of large below and small above. Then run three final checks: are all spires level, is there any obvious colour mismatch across the walls, and do the drawbridge and gate actually open and close?

## FAQ

**Q: How do I keep a MOC castle from collapsing halfway through?**
A: It comes down to the base and the walls. Sandwich plates and bricks in the chassis to stop edge curling, build walls as a double layer with offset bonding for rigidity, and reinforce tower-to-wall joints with connectors. Then it will not fall apart mid-build.

**Q: How do you actually build a round castle tower?**
A: Ring curved parts into a circular cross-section, then stack courses with a rotational offset so the joints between courses are not vertically aligned — the roundness improves noticeably. Close the top with curved and conical parts, centred.

**Q: What if I do not have enough parts for a castle?**
A: Protect the silhouette, not the detail. Spend your parts on tall towers and battlement profiles, keep wall faces in large areas of one solid colour to cut visual noise, and create presence through height and symmetry. Add detail once your part supply catches up.

**Q: How do I choose castle colours that do not look muddy?**
A: Commit to one dominant colour across the build. Realistic style means grey brick with a dark roof; fairytale style means off-white with a red roof. Avoid mixing light and dark bricks at random — once the wall colour is inconsistent, the whole model instantly looks dirty.
