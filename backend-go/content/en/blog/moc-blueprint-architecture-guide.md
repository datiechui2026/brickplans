# Choosing MOC Architecture Blueprints: Houses, Landmarks, Streets

You want to build a proper building, but the blueprint is the part that decides whether the project finishes. Pick the wrong architecture MOC and the failure modes are predictable: parts run short, the structure turns out floppy, proportions look off, and the build stalls halfway. Having worked through dozens of building designs, here is how the three main families compare on scale, structure, parts lists and budget — all of it usable before you spend anything.

## First, separate the three blueprint families

- **Residential** (villas, apartments, guesthouses): small footprint, a tight part palette, usually within a 32x32 baseplate, a few hundred to 2,000 pieces. Good for a first architecture build and easy to modify.
- **Landmark** (clock towers, churches, city halls, replicas of famous buildings): busy facades, a lot of stacked structure, often 3,000-4,000 pieces. Demanding on proportion and structural logic, so best for experienced builders.
- **Street scene** (bakery, café, theatre that combine into one row): the goal is a street that connects. Mostly 16-stud or 32-stud modular designs, and the blueprints usually leave standard interfaces so the buildings line up.

Whichever family you prefer, decide the display setting before you buy: a single model on a shelf, something that has to fit inside a cabinet, or a full street. The baseplate and scale logic changes completely with the answer.

## Scale and baseplate: the first hurdle

The classic architecture MOC failure is a model that reads as a building from across the room and looks awkward up close. Almost always it is proportion. Check three things in the blueprint:

1. **Baseplate size**: 32x32 studs is the standard street-scene unit, while 16x32 suits a narrow shopfront. Confirm the blueprint's baseplate actually matches your cabinet and display shelf dimensions.
2. **Floor height and door height**: a ground-floor door runs about 6 to 8 bricks tall, and window and balcony proportions have to agree with the floor height. A handsome render does not guarantee a sane finished scale.
3. **Minifig scale**: if minifigures go inside, door openings and floor height must clear them — floor height is generally no less than 10 bricks. Otherwise you have a model to look at, not one to play with.

## Structure complexity and difficulty tiers

The most common beginner mistake is choosing something that looks great and then builds like a punishment. Sort blueprints by difficulty before committing:

- **Beginner (1 star)**: a single-storey small shop or cottage, mostly straight stacking, no interior. The right pick for a first architecture attempt.
- **Intermediate (2-3 stars)**: two or three storeys with interior detail, stairs and balconies. Expect a modest amount of SNOT (studs-not-on-top) and some overhang.
- **Advanced (4-5 stars)**: landmarks lean on internal frames, domes that close in toward the top, and Technic beam reinforcement. Step counts are dense — save these until you have some experience.

The test is simple: look for how much unconventional building the steps contain. The more sideways, upside-down and corner-piece work a blueprint asks for, the more experience it demands. Don't judge from the finished photo alone — flip through a few step pages and check how dense they are.

## Parts list and budget: do the maths before ordering

Part cost on architecture MOCs is routinely underestimated. Whenever you evaluate an architecture blueprint, open the parts list first:

- **Piece count and budget**: loose parts average roughly 0.2-0.4 RMB per piece, so a 2,000-piece building means several hundred RMB in parts alone. Price the list before you order anything.
- **Share of special parts**: the fewer large plates, transparent window parts, corner pieces and slopes a design needs, the more likely you can fill it from your own stock. A special-part-heavy design means separate orders, and both lead time and shipping belong in the budget.
- **Substitutions and colour mismatch**: the same colour from different batches will not match exactly. For large single-colour walls, favour common colour numbers so future top-ups blend in. Well-made blueprints list substitute colour numbers for exactly this reason.

## Lining a street up

Before choosing a street-scene blueprint, confirm it has **standard modular interfaces**: whether neighbouring buildings reserve 1x2 or 2x2 connection points at the base, and whether corners are rounded or chamfered. When you arrange a row, follow the usual rules — vary the heights, alternate the colours, and leave narrow alleys. Offsetting two buildings by half a floor to a full floor, then adding a few trees and lampposts, is what gives a street its lived-in feel.
