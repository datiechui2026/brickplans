# MOC Waterfall Build Review: Clear Bricks, Rocks and Water Flow

In brick landscaping, a waterfall is the element that does the most with the least motion. Every piece is static, yet the eye still reads water tumbling downward. Getting that sense of flow depends on three things working together: how you use clear bricks, how much vertical drop you build in, and how the surrounding rock frames it. Below are my test-build notes from several waterfall MOCs, working from the rock base up through the water layers.

## 1. The Rock Base: The Waterfall's Skeleton

### Stacking the mountain in layers

A waterfall needs a mountain before it can be a waterfall. In testing, the best results came from mixing dark grey and light grey 2x2 and 2x4 bricks, stacking them in layers with a slight step in or out at each level to create a natural rock face. Dropping a few 1x1 bricks into the same layer as "loose rubble" breaks up the colour and instantly gives the rock a rougher read. Don't stack the mountain into a tidy rectangular box — twisting it slightly off-axis makes it look far more real.

### Planning the drop

Leave a flat cliff edge at the top of the mountain as the water's source. The cliff should be at least 4 to 6 studs high. Any shorter and the waterfall reads like a vertical pane of glass rather than falling water.

## 2. Clear-Brick Water: The Key to Flow

### Layering the water for texture

Build the waterfall itself from transparent or translucent bricks in light blue and pale aqua, working downward one layer at a time with each layer projecting one stud forward. That produces the stepped, cascading effect. In testing, 1x2 transparent tiles gave the best water of anything I tried — the smooth surface catches light in a way ordinary studded bricks simply cannot. Aim for at least 3 to 4 steps between the cliff top and the pool below so the water doesn't look stiff.

### Splash detail as the finishing touch

Stacking nothing but clear bricks quickly turns monotonous. Add a few white 1x1 bricks partway down to suggest spray, then lay a ring of small white pieces around the impact zone at the bottom as foam. The sense of movement jumps up a level immediately, and it's the cheapest, highest-impact step in the whole build.

## 3. The Foreground Pool and Scene Integration

A waterfall needs a pool at its feet. Use light blue plates as the base, then lay transparent tiles on the surface to catch reflections, and scatter a few small white elements along the edge as ripples. Rounded plates make far more natural shorelines than square corners. If you want the "waterfall feeding a stream" look, run the pool out to one side as a shallow creek — the overall composition reads as much more complete.

## 4. Structural Bracing and Common Problems

A waterfall's biggest risk is the water collapsing. Clear bricks stacked vertically put the centre of gravity high, so add a horizontal connector every few layers through the middle of the fall, tying the clear bricks back into the rock behind them. In testing this noticeably improved stability.

Another common issue: scratches and fingerprints on clear bricks are painfully obvious under light. Wipe the pieces clean before assembly and handle the finished waterfall as little as possible, and the overall finish will hold up.

## 5. Overall Verdict and Advice

Building a waterfall isn't complicated. The core is three steps: leave a drop in the rock, step the clear bricks down one level at a time, and add white pieces for splash. Compared with other landscaping subjects it has a low parts barrier but delivers immediate results, which makes it an excellent first nature scene to attempt.
