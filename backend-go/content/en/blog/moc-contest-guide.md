# MOC Contest Guide: Rules, Theme Fit, Instructions, Display

## Read the Rules First, Talk Creativity Later

Plenty of builders spend weeks on a build and then lose at the very first gate: **they never actually read the rules**. Before you enter a brick MOC contest, go through the entry terms line by line — maximum footprint (32×32 and 48×48 studs are the common caps), whether parts are restricted to one brand, whether the model must be original and previously unpublished, submission format (renders or photos of the real thing), and the deadline. Just two clauses — "brand-restricted parts only" and "must be an unpublished original" — are enough to knock out more than half of all submissions. A great model that breaks the rules scores zero. There is no workaround for that.

## Matching the Theme: Relevance Beats Showmanship

Almost every contest has a theme: "future city", "mythical creatures", "fairy-tale cottage". Before you start building, answer two questions: **how do I read this theme, and how do I express it differently from everyone else?** Judges weight theme fit heavily — a technically modest build that lands the brief dead centre usually outscores a virtuoso build that wandered off topic. My advice: skip the most literal, obvious reading. If the theme is "ocean", don't just build a big whale. Add a story or a viewpoint. Staying with "ocean", an offshore drilling platform ecosystem, or fishing boats coming home at dusk, is far more memorable than one more whale — and far more likely to stand out in the jury room.

## Instructions and Plans: Your Entry Has to Be Reproducible

Offline contests judge the physical model. What do online contests judge? **Your plans and instructions.** Many online brick MOC competitions require a buildable, reproducible plan, and at that point the clarity of your instructions decides your placing. Do the step-by-step work properly: an unambiguous part count added at each step, one consistent camera angle throughout, clearly labelled colours, and ideally a BOM parts list plus an estimated price. Renders should be high enough resolution (2000 px or above is the usual recommendation) and shown from multiple angles — front, side, top-down, and one detail close-up each. If your plans are genuinely good, the contest doubles as an audience-building exercise: plenty of designers were first discovered through a competition entry and turned those entrants into long-term followers. That is the hidden value of entering.

## Physical Display and Lighting: The Final Push

For an offline contest or a live exhibition judging, the display is your first impression. On lighting, use **two side lights plus one top light** to avoid blown-out highlights and dead black shadows; a white or light grey backdrop is least likely to compete with the model. Raise the piece to just below eye level so judges don't have to stoop to see the details. Bring an A4 spec card too: model name, concept, part count, build time — readable at a glance. When you shoot photos, take plenty of detail close-ups. Judges and visitors are drawn in by the overall silhouette first and won over by the details second; those close-ups are your second showing.

## Contest Mindset: Rankings Matter, But Don't Stare at Them

One practical note to finish. Rankings obviously matter in a brick MOC contest, but the more valuable thing is **the chance for more people to see the work**. Before you submit, post the model on your own community page or gallery as a dry run, collect feedback, sharpen the details, then polish one more version before the deadline. Once the contest is over, whatever the result, turn the build into a complete write-up and leave it on your profile permanently — it works as a portfolio, and it lays groundwork for plan licensing and group exhibitions later. If you want to keep raising the finish level of your builds, or you're looking for reference plans on the same subject and the style of past winners, the BrickPlans (brickplan.cn) blog has plans and build notes collected by theme; a scan before you enter often turns up the breakthrough idea you were missing.

## Summary

The full chain of entering a brick MOC contest is: **understand the rules → express the theme → deliver solid plans → present it properly**. The four steps lock together, and every step shakes out a share of the field. Get each one right and you are already ahead of most of your competition. Build, submit, and treat every contest as an upgrade cycle for the model — the build that gets you noticed will come.
