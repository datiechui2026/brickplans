# Brick Blueprint Tutorial: Read the Legend, Then Build

Most newcomers open a MOC blueprint and immediately think, "I can't read this." What this brick blueprint tutorial fixes is exactly that: what every line and symbol on the page is actually telling you. Once you understand the three pillars — legend, step sequence and part list — even a complex model can be built from the page. Below is the whole process, from zero to a finished build: reading the plan, prepping parts, assembling, and finally modifying the design.

## 1. Four sections to read before you touch a brick

A standard brick blueprint — whether it comes from a brand's instruction booklet or from a builder sharing a MOC plan — is usually made of four parts: a cover (with a photo of the finished model and the part count), a part list, a legend, and the step-by-step build diagrams. Read all four before you start. The cover tells you how big the project is, the part list tells you how much to prep, the legend explains the color and symbol rules, and the step diagrams are the core of the whole thing. Reading first and building second saves you from tearing apart a half-finished section later.

## 2. How to read the legend: colors, arrows and orientation marks

The legend is the most basic lesson in any brick blueprint tutorial. In the vast majority of plans, **color stands for part type**, and the legend tells you which part each color maps to. **Arrows and dashed lines** show assembly direction — the arrow points the way the piece goes in. Some plans also use a "+" sign to indicate overlap, a circle to highlight the part being added in that step, and a shaded background to mark what was already built in previous steps. When you see a label like "1x1" or "2x4," those numbers are the stud dimensions — a 2x4 is a brick measuring two studs by four studs. Get these symbols down and you are already halfway to building from the page.

## 3. Part list and quantity check: count before you build

Always check the part list before you build. Count out every numbered part, circle anything missing in pencil, and you avoid the classic mid-build discovery that something is short. Three things to watch for during the check: first, **separate same-color, similar-shape parts** — dark gray and light gray blur together on a printed page; second, **parts that come in pairs** (left- and right-hand pieces, mirrored pieces) need double the count you first read; third, **special pieces** (hinges, gears, Technic pins) go into their own bag so they are quick to grab. If the part count is large, sort by color and type — the build goes much more smoothly.

## 4. Build pacing: bottom to top, layer by layer

When you actually start, a few habits keep you on track. First, **stack from the base upward**, pressing each completed layer down firmly and confirming the clutch is seated before moving on. Second, follow the **step numbers** in order and do not skip ahead — most plans add only a handful of parts per step, and skipping is the fastest way to lose pieces. Third, on large flat surfaces or long spans, lay a foundation with **SNOT (studs-not-on-top) technique** or reinforcing plates so the finished model does not end up loose. Compare against the photo of the finished model as you go, so a reversed orientation gets caught early rather than at the end.

## 5. From following the plan to changing it: recolors, swaps, bases

Once you can read a plan, the next step is modifying it. Three beginner-level moves: **recolor** — swap the plan's main color for whatever you already have in your bins, saving a dedicated part order; **substitute** — replace retired or hard-to-find pieces with functional equivalents, for example using ordinary bricks plus a plate in place of a dedicated corner piece; **add a base** — put a baseplate under a modular street scene so it stands firm and can be combined with other layouts. The rule for modifying is "structure first": lock in the connection points before you touch the appearance.

This brick blueprint tutorial covers the full loop — reading the plan, prepping parts, building, and modifying. The core of it is simple: read the plan first, prep the parts second, put hands on bricks last.
