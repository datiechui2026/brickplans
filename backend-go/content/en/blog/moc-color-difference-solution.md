# LEGO MOC Color Mismatch: Prevent, Detect, and Fix It

One of the most maddening things about building an MOC is that the same color on the instructions comes out looking patchy and mismatched in real life. A workable color-difference solution isn't something you improvise after the problem appears — it's a full process covering part selection, testing, and repair. Here's the whole thing laid out in order: why color differences happen, how to spot them, how to avoid them, and how to fix them.

## Where color differences actually come from

It isn't magic. There are basically two causes. The first is **different production batches**: the colorant formula, the production run, and even the storage age of the parts can all shift the hue. Take the same white part — a batch from two years ago next to one made this year — and you can see the warm/cool difference with the naked eye. The second is **different brands**: manufacturers don't share a single color-card system, so a part labeled "light gray" can be a completely different gray depending on who made it. On top of that, parts left in strong sunlight or exposed to chemicals will fade and yellow over time — that's color difference acquired after the fact.

## How to test for color differences quickly

Don't rush to put new parts on the model. Run a check first. The most practical method is **grouping and comparing within the same color**: lay parts that look identical on a sheet of white paper, view them side by side in natural light (not warm yellow lamps — they lie to your eyes), then shine a flashlight across them from the side to see whether the surface reflections match. If you're dealing with large quantities, sort them into bins by color code and do a "color difference inventory" before you start building. For high-precision builds, you can also use a color-picker app on a photo and compare the values — if the numbers fall within a reasonable range, treat the parts as usable.

## How to avoid color differences when buying

Prevention is always less work than repair. **Buy parts for one project in a single batch where possible** — especially when you're filling a large single-color wall. Better to over-order once than to place two or three separate orders; this is the single most effective way to avoid color differences. Try to **stick to one brand and one series** for your parts, and request samples for comparison before mixing brands. Also check whether the seller lists a batch number or production date — official channels tend to be more transparent with that information. Finally, inspect the color as soon as new parts arrive and return or exchange them promptly if something's off, rather than discovering it halfway through the build.

## How to salvage parts that already differ in color

If the difference is already there, you have three options depending on severity.

- **Replace**: for parts that aren't structurally critical, are out in the open, and show an obvious difference, simply swap them for same-batch pieces or replace the whole surface. Least hassle.
- **Paint**: when the difference is concentrated in a few small parts, you can unify them with brick-specific spray paint or a marker. Test the color somewhere inconspicuous first, and apply a protective clear coat to stop it rubbing off.
- **Adjust the layout**: move the mismatched parts to the back, the interior, the underside — anywhere less visible. Or use decorative elements (windows, signage, foliage) to visually break up a same-color area so the difference stops standing out.

A word on **domestic parts versus LEGO parts**: LEGO has tighter quality control and stable batches, so over the long run its color differences are smaller. Domestic parts have improved noticeably in recent years, and mainstream brands control color well within a single batch — the risk goes up when you mix batches or brands. So when you combine the two, divide by function: use one brand from one batch for large visible surfaces, and mix freely for internal structural parts. It saves money and nobody can tell.

Most experienced MOC builders agree on the core of any color-difference solution: **buy in concentrated bulk, test before you build, and mix by zone**. Get those three right and the vast majority of color problems are solved at the source.
