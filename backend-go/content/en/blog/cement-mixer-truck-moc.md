# Concrete Mixer Truck MOC: Drum Drive and Hydraulic Lift

Bottom line first: a mixer truck is judged on two mechanisms. The drum turns because a bevel gear pair drives a ring gear mounted inside it, and the discharge works because the chute lifts. When you shop for a plan, start with photos of the finished model.

The concrete mixer is one of the most recognizable machines in the construction lineup — the tilted drum, the heavy hydraulic bracing, the discharge chute hanging off the tail. Drop a finished one into a layout and the job-site feel arrives instantly. But the real selling point was never the silhouette; it is that the drum spins and the chute moves. This guide walks through picking a plan, the drum drivetrain, the lifting gear and the cab details.

## Where to Find Plans and What to Check Before Buying

There are four main routes to a concrete mixer truck MOC plan.

1. Overseas communities such as Rebrickable and Bricklink. Professional plans, but mostly in English.
2. Player posts on Chinese brick forums and Tieba. Chinese callouts are friendly, though image quality varies a lot.
3. Digital plan packs on e-commerce platforms. These usually bundle a large batch of construction-vehicle subjects, good for filling out a collection in one go.
4. Dedicated plan-sharing sites such as brickplan, where you can search directly with keywords like "mixer" and "concrete" for plans and parts lists with Chinese labels.

When buying, favor versions that include real photos of the finished build plus rendered step-by-step images. Check the finished length and scale noted in the plan: the common 1:15 to 1:10 range corresponds to roughly 600–700 up to 1,000-plus pieces. Confirm what is already in your loose-parts bins before you order.

## The Rotating Drum: The Heart of the Build

The drum is the visual core of the truck and the structural hard part. Most reliable plans use a rear cross-shaft running in, meshing with gears inside the drum: a drive shaft is fixed to the rear chassis, a bevel gear set passes power to the ring gear inside the drum, and turning the crank at the tail — or running a motor — rotates the drum on its own axis.

Three things to watch while building:

- Build the drum skeleton as a closed cylinder first, then wrap the curved bricks over it. Skip that order and the surface goes lumpy.
- Leave enough backlash between the internal ring gear and the driving bevel gear. Too tight and it locks up; too loose and it slips under load.
- Lay the helical mixing blade on the outer wall with sloped bricks set at a diagonal. It matches the real machine and makes the drum read as fuller.

Some higher-end plans add a motorized option so the drum genuinely turns while the truck drives.

## Hydraulic Lift and Discharge Chute

The discharge system is the mixer's other soul. On the real machine, hydraulic cylinders raise the front of the drum and the concrete pours down the chute. MOC builders usually reproduce this in one of two ways: a gear-and-rack setup that simulates the lift, which feels direct to operate and is simple to build; or pin-lock support struts arranged as a three-stage lift, which looks more accurate but plays as a mostly static feature.

For the chute itself, split it into a main chute and an extension chute joined with hinge pieces so it folds and stows. One structural rule matters more than any other: anchor the lift mechanism to the chassis rails. Do not let the force bear on the drum housing shell, or the whole assembly will come apart at full height.

## Chassis, Cab and Detail Work

The chassis has to carry weight and keep its shape. Lay the longitudinal rails first, make sure the front steering axle and the rear tandem axles sit on the same level line, and align the drum's bearing mounts with the chassis. Fit a steering linkage to the front wheels so a hand knob actually turns them.

For the cab, use SNOT side building to pull in a rounded profile, then add seats, a steering wheel and mirrors, with a warning light on the roof if the plan calls for one. Finish with the details that sell the subject: dark grey round plates as the hopper at the drum mouth, anti-slip steps at the rear, and job-site warning stickers. That gets you a mixer that reads correctly from any angle.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I get the drum to actually rotate?**
A: The standard approach is a rear cross-shaft running in and meshing with internal gears. A drive shaft is fixed to the rear chassis, a bevel gear set transfers power to the ring gear inside the drum, and a crank or motor spins it. Leave enough backlash — too tight and it will not turn, too loose and it slips.

**Q: The drum surface is uneven. What went wrong?**
A: Almost always the order of assembly. Close the skeleton into a cylinder first, then wrap the curved bricks over it, keeping the curved bricks aligned and the cylinder's wrap direction consistent. For the helical blade on the outer wall, set sloped bricks at a diagonal — it matches the real shape and makes the drum look fuller.

**Q: The lift falls apart at maximum height.**
A: In most cases the support struts were never tied into the chassis rails. Re-reinforce the load-bearing points. You can simulate the lift with a gear-and-rack arrangement or use a three-stage pin-lock strut; either way, split the chute into a main section and an extension joined by hinges so it folds away.

**Q: How should I choose a plan, and what does one cost?**
A: Prioritize versions with real photos of the finished model and rendered step-by-step images, and read the stated length and scale — the usual 1:15 to 1:10 works out to roughly 600–700 to 1,000-plus pieces. Check your spare-parts stock before ordering so you are not left short a handful of pieces.
