# How to Choose a Garbage Truck MOC Blueprint: Structure Guide

The short answer first: a garbage truck build stands or falls on two mechanisms — the tipper lift and the packer blade. If Technic parts make up less than 15% of the parts list, it is almost certainly a static model. A mid-size build costs roughly ¥150–¥400 in loose parts.

Garbage trucks are an underrated subject in the engineering-vehicle category. The boxy body, the lifting hopper and the compactor blade at the rear give you both the mechanical look of a real sanitation truck and a model that actually does something once it is finished. The problem is that blueprint quality varies wildly: some tippers will not lift, and some packer mechanisms are purely decorative. This guide walks through blueprint sources, the core structures to check, and the build order that keeps you out of trouble.

## How to Choose a Blueprint: Sources and What to Look For

There are three main places to find a garbage truck MOC blueprint. First, overseas communities such as Rebrickable and Bricklink — professional, but usually annotated in English. Second, builder shares on Chinese brick forums, Tieba and short-video platforms — Chinese-friendly, but image clarity varies a lot. Third, dedicated blueprint sharing sites such as brickplan, where you can search keywords like "garbage truck" and "sanitation" for blueprints and parts lists with Chinese annotations.

When you are choosing, check three things first: photos of a finished build (confirms the tipper really lifts), step-by-step renders (tells you whether the instructions are clear), and the parts list (count the Technic parts). A mid-size blueprint — 20–30 cm long and 800–1500 pieces — is the best fit for a first attempt.

## Tipper Lift: The Core Moving Mechanism

The tipper lift is the structure most worth getting right. Reliable blueprints usually use one of two approaches. The first is a gear-and-rack lift: a crank at the rear of the truck drives a rack that pushes the hopper up. It is intuitive and has a solid, mechanical feel. The second is a worm-gear reduction: a worm drives the lift arm, giving high torque and self-locking, so the hopper will not slide back down at the top of its travel. It is more faithful to the real machine, but the parts are more complex.

Three build notes. Make the hinge between hopper and frame from Technic parts with holes so rotation stays smooth. The load path of the lift arm has to land on the chassis rails — the body shell alone should never carry the weight. And make the hopper's rear panel openable so it dumps as the hopper rises, which instantly upgrades the play value.

## Packer Mechanism and Body Details

Beyond the lift, the packer is the truck's second soul. Real vehicles use a blade to push waste into the body; in a MOC you can add a rail inside the body with a blade that slides back and forth, driven by a crank on the side or by gears, to simulate the compression cycle. Build the body itself from large plates and panels into a square box, add reinforcing ribs on the roof to stop it sagging, and fit a hinged door at the rear opening.

For details: sanitation decals or printed parts on the sides, a warning light on the roof, a rear crash bar, and wide tires to match the heavy-duty stance. Green-and-white and orange-and-white are both high-visibility color schemes typical of real sanitation fleets.

## Chassis and Cab Build Notes

 The chassis determines whether the whole truck rolls smoothly. Build the longitudinal rails first, keeping the front steering axle and the rear tandem axles on the same level line, and pre-drill the holes for the hopper hinge and lift mechanism in the chassis before you plate over the body — that way nothing ends up misaligned. Give the rear axles a dual-bearing load structure so the truck does not sag when you put weight on it.

For the cab, use SNOT side building to get a square silhouette, and fit opening left and right doors, a steering wheel, seats and mirrors. Leave a gap between cab and body to reproduce the real truck's separate-body look. If you want to go further, a motorized version lets the truck drive and dump — budget roughly ¥100–¥300.

## Parts Check and Pitfalls to Avoid

Before you buy a blueprint, check the Technic share of the parts list. The lift and packer both rely on gears, cross axles and hole-bearing beams, so a blueprint below 15% Technic parts is essentially a static model. On cost: a mid-size garbage truck of 800–1500 pieces runs about ¥150–¥400 in loose parts.

Build in the order chassis → lift mechanism → body → cab. Verify the hopper lifts properly before you add cosmetic detailing, or you will end up taking it apart again.

A structurally sound blueprint plus a disciplined build order, and your brick garbage truck will genuinely lift and dump.
