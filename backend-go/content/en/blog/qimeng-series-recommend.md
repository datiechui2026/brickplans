# Which Qiji Series Fits Your Builder? Age and Theme Guide

Qiji is one of the long-running domestic brick brands, and its reputation rests on two things: solid safety standards and friendly pricing. The problem is the catalogue — there are so many series that the shelf gets confusing fast. Parents keep asking which Qiji series is actually the good one, and the honest answer depends on the builder's age and the theme they care about. Here is a breakdown of the four main lines.

## City Series: The Safe Bet for Ages 3-6

The City series covers everyday scenes — firefighting, construction, traffic and the like. Parts are on the large side and the build difficulty is low, which lines up with where younger children are in their fine-motor development. This is also the line where safety is handled most carefully: rounded corners, no odd smells, nothing that feels risky in small hands. That makes it the natural first brick set for a child.

If your kid is still in kindergarten, the City series is close to a no-thinking-required purchase, and the value for money holds up as well. You are not buying clever techniques here — you are buying a set that will survive being emptied onto the floor and rebuilt badly, over and over.

## Princess Series: Strong Following Among Girls

The Princess series leans on castles, carriages and fairy-tale settings, with a soft pink palette and carefully detailed minifigures. Parts run a little smaller than City and the instruction steps are more numerous, so it suits roughly ages 6-10 — children who can sit with a build and stay patient through a longer manual.

The real strength is how complete the scenes are. Once the build is finished it is immediately playable as a dollhouse-style setup, and plenty of young builders get hooked after the first one. One caveat: this line uses a lot of stickers, so builds go more smoothly when a parent helps with placement.

## Military and Police Series: Boy-Focused and Step-Up Builds

Military and Police take a harder-edged theme, with tanks, police vehicles and base structures. The part variety is wider and the structures are relatively more complex, which puts them in range for independent building at age 8 and up.

For this kind of builder, the question of which Qiji series is better usually comes down to Police. It stays closer to everyday life, and its parts are more interchangeable, which matters if the pieces are going to be pulled apart and reused. Military offers more detail, but the parts are smaller and more scattered — younger children lose pieces easily, so parent supervision is worth planning for.

## How Should Adult MOC Builders Choose?

It is a mistake to assume Qiji only serves the kids' market. Part quality has improved noticeably over the years, and both clutch and the feel of the brick surface hold up. When adults use Qiji parts for MOC work, start with the City series for wall bricks and baseplates — they are the most versatile pieces in the catalogue. Odd-shaped parts from the Military series work well as feature decoration on a model.

Precision is still slightly behind the premium brands, so don't expect the last word in tolerance. For testing out an idea, though, or for filling in a small-town layout, it is more than sufficient.

One practical note for anyone building at scale: buy a single set first and check the clutch of the batch you receive before committing to multiples. Part quality has improved across the board, but consistency between production runs is still something adult builders like to verify for themselves.

## The One-Line Summary

Buying for a child: City for ages 3-6, Princess or Police for ages 6-10, and save Military for hardcore builders 8 and up. Buying for yourself: start with City for basic bricks and plates, and go to Military when you need unusual shapes.
