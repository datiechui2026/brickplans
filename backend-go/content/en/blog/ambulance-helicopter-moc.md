# Ambulance Helicopter MOC: Rotor and Cabin Build Guide

The short answer: the success of an ambulance helicopter build comes down to the rotor drive and the cabin layout. Use two 40-tooth gears with one 12-tooth gear for the reduction, leave about a 0.5-stud gap between the main rotor head and the main shaft, and make the stretcher slide out on rails. Do that, and the model will look right and play well.

A red-and-white ambulance helicopter with spinning rotors, an opening rear door, and a stretcher in the cabin is a milestone project for many builders. This ambulance helicopter MOC uses about 1,200 pieces and balances display value with play value. Here is how the core build works, from the rotor head to the medical interior.

## Main Rotor Structure

The main rotor is the heart of the model. The instructions build the rotor head from Technic elements: two 40-tooth gears and one 12-tooth gear form the reduction set, carrying power from a crank at the bottom of the cabin to the main shaft. The shaft passes through a Technic cross-axle connector, then hinge pieces connect the three blades. Long curved pieces extend each blade, which keeps the proportions believable and reduces wobble when the rotor turns. Leave about a 0.5-stud gap between the rotor head and the main shaft; if the gears mesh too tightly, the whole assembly will bind.

## Tail Rotor and Drive

The tail rotor drive is where beginners most often run into trouble. A Technic cross axle runs from the main reduction box through the tail boom. At the end, two 45-degree bevel gears turn the drive through 90 degrees and spin the tail rotor in sync, reproducing the counter-torque layout of a real helicopter. The tail boom is built from Technic liftarms into a hexagonal cross-section. That makes it light and strong, and it lets you shape the tapered rear fuselage at the same time. Fit an axle bush at each end of the tail rotor shaft to stop axial play, and keep one stud of clearance between the tail rotor and the vertical fin so the parts do not interfere.

## Ambulance Cabin Layout

The fuselage uses curved pieces for a smooth, streamlined outline, with a white body and red stripes for a clean emergency look. The rear hatch is built with hinge pieces so it opens as one complete door, making it easy to load and unload a patient. Inside, the key feature is the stretcher rail: two smooth Technic axles laid parallel so the stretcher slides in and out without snagging. It is a simple mechanism, but it adds a lot of play value. Large transparent windows on both sides of the hatch let light into the cabin and make the interior details easy to see.

## Medical Details

What turns a helicopter into an ambulance helicopter is the small medical equipment. Build the stretcher on a base of flat plates, add railings on both sides, and use a 1x1 slope at the head end as a pillow. Make the IV bottle from a transparent 1x1 round plate stacked on a cross axle. Route the oxygen line along the cabin wall with hose pieces. For the ECG monitor, use a smooth tile plus a printed piece to suggest the screen, and reproduce the red cross with a printed piece or sticker. These additions cost very little, but they stand out in photos and make the model much more fun to handle.

## FAQ

**Q: How many parts does this ambulance helicopter MOC need?**

A: About 1,200 pieces. It is a medium-sized, medium-difficulty build, best suited to builders with some Technic experience. The main rotor, tail rotor drive, and ambulance cabin are the three main building stages.

**Q: What should I check if the main rotor will not turn?**

A: First check whether the gears are meshing too tightly, especially the reduction set with two 40-tooth gears and one 12-tooth gear. Then confirm that there is about a 0.5-stud gap between the main shaft and the rotor head. Too little clearance will make the rotor bind.

**Q: How can I secure transparent pieces that keep coming loose?**

A: Transparent pieces are brittle, so reinforce the connection points with cross axles or Technic pins instead of forcing them into place. On windows and light covers, you can add a 1x2 plate on the inside as backing. That holds the part more firmly without blocking the light.

**Q: Can I replace the hand crank with a motor?**

A: Yes. Replace the crank at the bottom of the cabin with an M motor, but keep the original reduction ratio. Build the hand-crank version first to check that the mechanism runs freely, then add the motor. This avoids binding and overheated parts.
