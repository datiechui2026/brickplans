# How to Build a LEGO City: Layout, Modules and Lighting

Every brick builder carries an ideal city around in their head. It might be a busy downtown block or a quiet, orderly residential district. This guide walks you through the whole process, from a blank baseplate to a miniature metropolis, covering layout planning, infrastructure, minifigure placement, modular street design and lighting detail.

## Step 1: Plan the Layout Before You Build

The most common mistake is grabbing bricks and starting on a building. The result is a cluster of structures with no logic to how they sit together. A well-built city starts with planning.

**Roads come first.** Use the stud direction of your baseplates, or standard road plates, to mark out main avenues, secondary streets and pedestrian zones. A main road should be wide enough for at least four lanes so car models sit naturally. Once the roads are fixed, the skeleton of the whole city is standing.

**Zone by function.** Divide the city into residential, commercial, civic and cultural, and park and leisure areas, linked by roads or planted strips. Treat one block as a unit of "one main building plus two supporting buildings" to avoid jarring height differences, and leave an expansion "interface" at the edge of every district for future growth.

**Watch the circulation.** Leave enough open ground, plazas and walkways that the city actually "walks" — people and vehicles can move through it. The finished display looks far more alive when the routes make sense.

## Step 2: Infrastructure — Housing, Shops and Civic Buildings

Infrastructure carries the life of the city, and it is the most information-dense part of any city build.

**Housing** lives on a sense of everyday life. A two-storey house with a balcony, a chimney on the roof and a small front garden, plus doors that open and windows that let light through, already reads as a warm home. Varying brick colours on the exterior walls adds brickwork texture and layering.

**Shops** need to feel commercial. Display windows are the soul of a storefront: frame a display case in transparent pieces, fill it with goods and props, then add an awning, a signboard and a cash register, and the whole street gains energy. Coffee shops, bakeries and flower shops are the easiest storefronts to make stand out.

**Civic buildings** are the face of the city. Police stations, fire stations, libraries and city halls can be built larger, with more formal facades. White and grey walls against a dark roof give strong contrast. Place them on a plaza or at the end of a main road so they act as the visual focus of the block.

## Step 3: Minifigures and Vehicles Bring It to Life

However many static buildings you stack up, without people it is still an empty city. Minifigures and vehicles are what finish a city build.

Minifigures are the story's lead characters: office workers, students, police officers, bakers, an elderly resident walking a dog. Spread different professions across their matching zones and the narrative appears on its own. A minifigure drinking coffee outside a café is ten times more convincing than a bare storefront.

Vehicles are the city's bloodstream. Distribute sedans, buses and trucks sensibly across roads and parking areas, and keep the logic consistent: a police car parked in front of the police station, a fire truck beside the fire station. Add street lamps, mailboxes and newsstands as small civic props, and the sense of realism climbs sharply.

## Step 4: Modular Street Design for Endless Expansion

If you want to move past "finish it and cap it", learn to think in modules. Fix every building onto a baseplate of one standard size, leave standard interlocking connections between floors, and keep facade orientation consistent across the street.

Each building then becomes an independent unit that can be swapped out, shifted and recombined at any time. Expanding the city with a second or third phase becomes effortless, and moving and displaying it is far easier too.

## Step 5: Lighting and Detail for Maximum Realism

The last step is atmosphere. Add LED light kits to street lamps, building windows and signs, and use warm white light to suggest a street at dusk — the city instantly gains warmth. Put antennas and a water tower on the roofs, grow vines up a wall, scatter fallen leaves on the ground, or use SNOT techniques to create a textured, uneven facade. These seemingly minor touches are exactly what moves a city build from "good looking" to "impressive".

## Final Thoughts

City building is a hobby you can take as deep as you like, and it never really ends — enjoying every moment of the process matters most.
