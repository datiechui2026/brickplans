# Harvester MOC Review: Header Lift + Drum Threshing Linkage

The short version: this harvester MOC earns its reputation on two working mechanisms — a header that pitches through a real range of motion and a threshing drum driven by the wheels. At 1,420 pieces, roughly 7 hours of building and a 46 cm finished length, it is one of the most complete farm-machinery designs currently available, and it is easy to recommend.

### 1. Instruction Structure and Readability

The manual runs 94 steps, split into four chapters that follow the machine itself: header, cab, grain tank and chassis. Every chapter opens with a parts overview image, so you can pull the pieces for that section before you start turning pages instead of hunting through the whole inventory.

The PDF is about 38 MB. At the key linkage points the designer adds magnified inset diagrams, which matters here because the gear and axle routing is where most mistakes happen. Print quality is clean and part callouts are legible at normal zoom. You can find the listing in the BrickPlan library by searching for the harvester MOC, and the first 20 steps are available as a free preview so you can judge the drawing style before paying.

### 2. Parts List and Cost

The parts list totals 1,420 pieces. Green track links and the special drum components account for roughly 15% of that — about 210 pieces — and they are the hardest part of the inventory to substitute from a general parts bin. If you plan to source everything yourself, expect to spend time chasing those specific elements.

Cost breaks down three ways:

- Instructions only: about ¥12.8
- Instructions plus your own parts: about ¥175 in parts
- Instructions plus the matched parts pack: ¥238

Buying the parts pack is the lower-stress option. It removes the risk of a missing element halfway through the header assembly, when a single absent track link can stall the whole build for a week.

### 3. Build Experience and Accuracy

Plan on about 7 hours. The header linkage alone eats roughly 2.5 hours of that, and it deserves the time — the mechanism uses 4 cross axles plus a worm gear to give the header a 0–45 degree pitch adjustment. The threshing drum section is tied to wheel rotation, so the two systems move together when the model rolls.

Stated accuracy against the real machine is around 88%. The finished model measures 46 cm long and 28 cm high. Push it across a table and the header and drum run in sync, which puts the play value well above a static display piece. This is the difference between a shelf model and something you actually pick up.

### 4. Value for Money

You are getting a farm model with two live linkages for under ¥250 all-in. Comparable official sets at the same piece count are priced at ¥600 and above. For builders who like agricultural machinery, that gap is the whole argument: the mechanism count is high, the part cost is low, and the finished model reads clearly as a harvester rather than a generic tracked vehicle.

Difficulty sits at medium-to-advanced. The gear trains are the reason — not the piece count. If you have not built a set of 1,000 pieces or more before, the header assembly will be a rough introduction.
