# Penguin MOC Blueprint: Shape, Colors and Stance

Among animal brick MOCs, penguins are consistently one of the most popular subjects. The round body, the unmistakable black-and-white pattern and that waddling, deadpan stance add up to a silhouette almost anyone recognizes — and you don't need exotic parts to get there. What makes a penguin readable at a glance isn't the number of pieces, it's how well you handle body proportions and color layering. This guide walks through the full process, from shaping the model to setting it in a scene.

## A Rounded Body: Curves Instead of Corners

The most appealing thing about a penguin is that plump, full body. Stack everything from square bricks and plates and the result looks stiff. Instead, use slopes, curved bricks and dome or sphere pieces to soften the outline. Keep the transition from belly to back gentle, so the body reads as an egg shape — narrower at the top, wider at the bottom. Let the belly bulge outward slightly, and don't force a hard boundary between head and body: one or two wedge pieces blended in is enough, and the cute factor comes for free.

The wings don't need to be complicated. Two short flippers that hug the body will do, mounted slightly toward the rear so the side profile matches a real penguin more closely. The feet can be wide and flat, angled slightly outward and pressed against the bottom of the body — accurate to the animal and useful later when you need the model to stand on its own. For the beak, one or two yellow slope or wedge pieces are plenty: small, but it's the detail that sells the whole build.

## Black, White and a Grey Gradient

Penguin coloring looks simple but there's more to it than you'd think. Pure white against pure black is striking, yet it can feel harsh. A more sophisticated approach is to work a grey gradient between the two: the back moves from dark grey into solid black, while the belly gets a slight lift from off-white or light grey, giving the body natural volume under light. The black area on the head should wrap down and around the jaw, leaving white only on the belly and cheeks — that ratio of black to white is what brings the model closer to a real emperor penguin.

If you have transparent blue or light blue parts on hand, scatter a few along the sides of the belly to suggest light bouncing off Antarctic ice. It adds a little life to an otherwise plain color scheme. The rule for color is always less is more: this blueprint encourages you to build one monochrome penguin first as practice, then introduce environmental colors once you're comfortable.

## Stance and Center of Gravity

Penguins are upright birds, so the stance decides the whole character of the model. Keep the center of gravity as low as possible, put the widest part of the body in the lower middle, and make the span of the feet slightly wider than the body — that's what lets it stand firmly. If you want the head tilted for a cuter look, build it as a detachable or slightly rotatable section so you can adjust the angle.

A common trick is to add a transparent support brick behind the penguin. It stays out of sight from the front but keeps any pose stable. After all, most penguin MOCs end up on display, so the build isn't really finished until it can stand securely and survive being turned around.

## Adding an Ice Scene

A single penguin feels a little lonely, and a simple ice base immediately makes the build feel complete. Use large white and light blue baseplates as the ground, then add transparent pieces along the edges to suggest broken ice, and scatter a few small ice chunks at varying heights. The scene doesn't need to be big — enough room for two or three penguins is fine. Leaving plenty of empty space actually strengthens the sense of a vast polar landscape.

For a family feel, build a smaller juvenile penguin with grey-black pieces all over to represent downy fluff and place it beside the adult. The story appears instantly. Whether the finished model goes on a bookshelf or out as a gift, it's a crowd-pleaser.
