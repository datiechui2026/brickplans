# MOC Mechanism Design for Beginners: Gears and Linkages

Start with the short answer: building moving structures into a brick MOC is not hard. Once you understand the four basic mechanism types — gears, linkages, universal joints and joints — you can produce about 80% of the motion effects you will ever need. Beginners should practise on a 1:3 reduction pair made from an 8-tooth and a 24-tooth gear; the parts for that cost under 50 yuan.

## Step 1: Decide what the model has to do

Before you pick up a single piece, work out the motion: rotation, reciprocating (back-and-forth) travel, oscillation, or a multi-joint sequence. The motion decides the transmission. Rotation points to gears; reciprocating travel points to a linkage. Write it down — a design with a stated motion has a direction.

## Step 2: Learn what gears will and will not do

Common brick gears have 8, 12, 20, 24 and 40 teeth. The tooth-count ratio is the speed ratio: a 24-tooth gear driving an 8-tooth gear gives 1:3 reduction — more torque, less speed. Run it the other way and you get 3:1 overdrive. A very large tooth difference invites gear skipping, so keep any two adjacent stages within 3x.

## Step 3: Build your first gear train

Mount a 24-tooth and an 8-tooth gear on two separate axles and mesh them at right angles, then drive the input with a hand crank. Watch the output axle: it turns at one third of the input speed and delivers noticeably more force. This is the key step for understanding gear transmission.

## Step 4: Turn rotation into reciprocation with a linkage

The crank-and-connecting-rod mechanism is the classic technique in MOC mechanism design: the crank rotates, and through the connecting rod it drives a slider in straight-line reciprocating motion. Pistons, windscreen wipers and mechanical arms all work this way. The longer the connecting rod, the longer the stroke — and the crank radius has to grow to match.

## Step 5: Universal joints let a driveline turn a corner

When a drive axle has to change direction, connect two shafts at an angle with a universal joint. Keep the angle within 15 degrees; past 30 degrees you get visible shuddering. When you chain several universal joints together, keep every individual angle as small as possible so efficiency holds up.

## Step 6: Joint design sets the range of motion

Knees and elbows on figure MOCs use hinged pieces with end stops, giving about 90 degrees of travel. Shoulders and hips use ball joints, which offer roughly 120 degrees of multi-directional movement. Leave 0.1 to 0.3 mm of clearance at each joint — too tight and it will not turn, too loose and the model will not stand.

## Step 7: Test, lubricate and finish

Once the model is assembled, dry-run the mechanism. If it binds, check first whether the gears are meshed too tightly and whether an axle is bent. A small amount of silicone grease made for ABS parts is acceptable between rubbing surfaces; never use machine oil, which attacks plastic. When the test passes, reinforce the key nodes.

## FAQ

**What basic parts do I need for MOC mechanism design?**
Four each of 8-, 12-, 20- and 24-tooth gears; 20 axles and 20 axle bushes; 4 universal joints; 6 hinged pieces and 6 ball joints; plus two hand cranks. The full set costs about 40 to 60 yuan.

**The gear train binds and will not turn — what now?**
Check for over-tight gear meshing and bent axles first, then confirm that no stage exceeds a 3x tooth ratio and causes skipping. Loosen the axle bush positions slightly to leave a small gap; that solves most binding.

**How do I make a mechanism run more smoothly?**
Keep axles square to their bushes, keep the gear contact faces clean, and apply a little silicone grease to the friction points. Shorter transmission paths are more efficient — put the motor or hand crank as close to the output end as you can.

**Motor or hand crank first for a beginner?**
Start with the hand crank: low cost, controllable speed, and easy to watch what the parts are doing. Switch to a motor once the mechanism runs cleanly. Going straight to a motor spins too fast and tends to throw weak structures apart.

**My mechanism falls apart after a few runs — how do I strengthen it?**
Double-fix load-bearing nodes with a cross axle plus an axle bush, and add end stops to moving joints so they cannot over-travel. After testing, press down on the model to find loose points and add a friction pin wherever there is play — stability improves noticeably.
