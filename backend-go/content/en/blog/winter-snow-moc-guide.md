# Snow MOC Guide: Building Realistic Winter Scenes in Bricks

## Why Snow Scenes Live or Die on White Layering

Snow is one of the easiest brick themes to get wrong. Pile on too much white and the whole build flattens into an unreadable blur with no depth. Experienced MOC builders know that real snow is never pure white — it's a combination of **white, light gray, beige-gray and cool white**. White carries the broad snowfield, light gray presses in the shadows and lit faces, beige-gray or pale blue-white handles the backlit areas. Interleave those tones and the snow gains volume. So the first step in a snow MOC isn't rushing to stack white bricks — it's assembling your snow palette first.

## Part Selection: Don't Just Hoard White Plates

A lot of beginners start by stockpiling white plates, but the soul of a snow scene is **small pieces and slopes**. Scattered 1x1 plates mimic snow that's been trampled, slope pieces build drifts and gentle banks, and 1x2 slope bricks are ideal for snow on eaves. For the ground, I'd lay white plates as a base, scatter an irregular layer of 1x1 white plates on top, and transition the edges with light gray plates — the surface ends up both flat and natural. Trans pieces are also extremely useful here: trans-blue for ice, trans-clear for fluffy fresh snow. Mixing them gives a completely different texture.

## Layering Snow and Pressing It Onto Roofs

Making a building look snow-covered is the highlight of the whole scene. The classic **roof snow** approach: wrap the outer edge of the roof plates with another layer of white plates that overhangs by half a stud, forming a thick snow cornice; then stack a few 1x1 white plates along the ridge to suggest the curve of accumulated snow. On windows and doors you can lay a long white brick across as a "snow sill," and while you're at it cover drainpipes and steps with white plates — the building instantly looks like it just weathered a heavy snowfall. Ground snow needs **variation in depth**: thicker at doorways and along the road, thinner on the central path, then dot the surface with light gray plates for tire ruts and footprints. The lived-in feel comes through immediately.

## Detail Elements: Snowmen, Pines and Ice

Small details decide how refined the finished model looks. A **snowman** is three white ball pieces stacked in decreasing sizes, two brown bars as arms, small black plates as eyes, and a red scarf piece (or a wrap of red plates) to finish it off. **Pine trees** are standard kit for snow scenes: stack green slope pieces into a conical canopy, pick a few white plates out at the edge of each layer as "hanging snow," and top the tree with a white star or a small yellow plate. For **ice**, lay down a sheet of trans-blue plates, build a snowbank of white plates around the edge, and add a skater minifigure (with a thin layer of light blue under the trans plates) and you're set. If you want steam rising off water, a few trans bars with a small white plate as vapor is a bit of fun unique to winter scenes.

## Lighting the Winter Mood

Snow and lighting are a perfect match. Warm yellow light falling on white snow produces that particular winter-evening feeling of cold and warm at once — many of the standout snow MOCs win on exactly this step. In practice, bury a warm LED inside the building so the windows glow orange, and place a street lamp on the snowy road (a pole with a trans-yellow 1x1 piece as the lamp head). Ring the lit area with light yellow plates as a "halo" to contrast against the cool white snow. No light kit? A mass of trans warm-colored pieces built as a light box works just as well.

## Handling Different Scales

At **micro scale** (smaller than minifigure), the priority is atmosphere: spread white plates across a wide area, add a few trees and a street lamp, and don't chase detail. **Standard minifigure scale** is where detail matters — roof snow, window sills, snowmen and ice all need proper treatment. For **large display bases**, pay special attention to **continuity of the snow** — don't let buildings float on top of the snowfield. Build snow slopes from white and light gray plates around the base of each building so it looks like it's sinking into the snow, and the whole composition holds together.

## Final Thoughts

Four rules cover most of it: alternate white and gray in your palette, scatter plates across the ground, press snow onto roofs with an overhang, and add one warm light. Follow that logic and even a build made from basic white bricks will read with real depth.
