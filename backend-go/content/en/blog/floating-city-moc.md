# Floating City MOC: Anti-Gravity Base and Island Build Guide

Start with the conclusion: a floating city MOC succeeds or fails on one thing — whether the float looks believable. Hide the load path inside an inverted-cone anti-gravity base and a Technic skeleton, keep the island footprint between 20 and 32 studs, and set the main tower height at 1.2 to 1.5 times the base diameter.

## The Core Challenge: Making the Float Believable

The most attractive part of a brick floating city MOC is also the part that scares builders off: how do you make an island float? Brick is a gravity-sensitive material, and any suspended structure ultimately transfers its load through supports. So the first step is not stacking castles — it is designing supports that nobody can see. There are three common approaches: transparent support columns, an inverted-cone rock base, and a hanging bracket. The inverted cone delivers the best result — bury the supports in rock, vines and clouds, and the island appears to float while the structure underneath stays rock solid.

For proportions, keep the island base diameter between 20 and 32 studs and the main tower at 1.2 to 1.5 times that diameter. Too tall and it looks top-heavy; too short and you lose the grandeur of a sky city.

## The Anti-Gravity Base: Inverted Cone and Technic Skeleton

The anti-gravity base is the load-bearing core of the whole build. Use Technic parts for the internal frame: raise a main support shaft from the center of the base, run an axle through multiple layers of beams, then radiate ribs outward with connectors to form an inverted-cone truss. The outer rock shell then only handles appearance — all the force travels through the Technic skeleton, which makes the structure very stable.

For the outer rock, mix dark gray and tan slopes: lay a foundation of large slopes, then fill the gaps with small slopes and curved pieces to create natural rock strata. Taper the bottom of the base to a point so the island looks like it grew out of a sea of clouds. Don't forget vines in the rock crevices — a flexible hose or plant pieces trailing downward breaks up the flatness of the stone and lets the support structure disappear completely.

## The Island Surface: Layering and Cantilevers

The island surface itself needs to feel airborne. Build the terrain in three layers: the bottom layer is stalactites or tree roots hanging down (inverted slopes and 1x1 bricks staggered at different heights), the middle is a dirt cross-section (a rim of brown plates), and only the top layer is the actual building floor. Seen from the side, the island then has thickness and a visible cross-section instead of reading as a flat slab.

For the building layout, lean hard on cantilevers: push towers, platforms and walkways past the island edge with nothing underneath. The visual impact is enormous. Reinforce the cantilever internally with long beams or Technic parts as hidden beams, and keep the overhang to 3 to 5 studs — go further and the front end sags. Pin-based Technic connectors at the joints are far more reliable than stacking bricks alone.

## Clouds and Waterfalls: The Trick That Makes the Base Disappear

The best way to hide an anti-gravity base is to add clouds. Build cloud banks from white curved pieces such as 1x4 curved bricks and 1x1 round plates, and wrap a ring around the middle of the base — it blocks the support structure and adds to the floating feel. For a waterfall, hang trans-light-blue 1x2 plates from the island rim, finish the end with white 1x1 studs to suggest spray, and make sure the waterfall stops in mid-air with a small floating pool beneath it. That single move sells the fantasy.

## FAQ

**Q: How do you make a brick floating city actually float?**
A: Brick is gravity-sensitive, so suspended structures always transfer force through supports. Hide the supports inside an inverted-cone base, then mask the load points with clouds and waterfalls — visually, the island floats.

**Q: How do you set the island-to-tower ratio?**
A: Keep the island base diameter between 20 and 32 studs and the main tower at 1.2 to 1.5 times that diameter. Too tall is top-heavy; too short loses the grandeur of a sky city.

**Q: How do you build the anti-gravity base?**
A: An inverted cone plus a Technic skeleton: a wide cone base keeps the center of gravity low, beams and pins form the load-bearing frame inside, and the island's weight is passed down layer by layer to the table. Wrap the outside in rock plates and clouds.

**Q: What are clouds and waterfalls for?**
A: They make the base vanish. Stack white curved pieces into rolling clouds over the cone edges, then drop trans-light-blue pieces as a waterfall. The eye is interrupted, and the support structure hides inside the picture.

The most common beginner mistake is an under-built base frame: Technic beams joined at a single point come loose over time. Fix every connection with at least two pins, and reinforce critical joints with connector plates such as a 3x3 cross block. The second issue is balance — place the main tower slightly behind the center of the base and leave a plaza in front, otherwise the whole island tips forward.

Advanced builders can add a rotation mechanism: put a gear train on the central axle of the base and drive the entire island slowly with a crank. Combine it with light bricks and you have a sky city that breathes. Reference builds and step-by-step instructions are easy to find in the BrickPlan instruction-sharing community — search "sky city" or "floating island" and study someone else's frame design before you start; it saves a lot of detours.
