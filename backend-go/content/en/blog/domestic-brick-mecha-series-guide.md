# Chinese Brick Mecha Brands: Gobricks, SEMBO and Panlos

Chinese brick mecha design has moved fast over the past two years. Articulation, part precision and overall design quality have all caught up, while prices often sit at half of what international brands charge, sometimes less. This guide rounds up the flagship mecha lines from three of the main players, Gobricks, SEMBO and Panlos, covering what each does best, where each falls short, and which one deserves your money.

## Gobricks: the precision benchmark, and the best base for modding

In this lineup, Gobricks earns its reputation almost entirely on part quality. Tolerance control is tight, clutch is firm, and colour is consistent, so joints on a finished mecha don't go floppy. Most of its mecha output is licensed or original small-scale designs, roughly 20 cm tall, but articulation is excellent, with ball joints or gear joints at the shoulders, elbows and knees. The weakness is that Gobricks is not beginner-friendly: instructions are brief and parts are not sorted into fine sub-bags, so it suits builders with some experience. If you plan to recolour, add parts or build your own MOC later, Gobricks is the best foundation thanks to how well its general-purpose parts integrate.

## SEMBO: the widest range, from starter sets to collector pieces

SEMBO's mecha catalogue is the broadest and most mixed of the three. It runs from small starter mecha costing around 100 RMB up to collector-grade large-scale models well over 1,000 RMB. Its strength is design variety: transformable mecha, light-up elements and opening cockpit structures all appear in the line. For newcomers, a SEMBO mecha is the easiest place to start, because instructions are detailed and split across several booklets, so the build flows smoothly. The downside is that some earlier releases used softer plastic and left rough sprue marks. Before buying, check reviews to see whether anyone complains about loose parts on that specific kit, and simply avoid those.

## Panlos: large scale and solid structure, the display centrepiece

If you want the visual punch of a single centrepiece model, Panlos builds the strongest large-scale mecha currently coming out of China. Its flagship mecha often exceed 40 cm in height, with a complete internal skeleton and exterior armour layered on in nested plates, which looks spectacular once finished. Structural design is where Panlos really shines: reinforced parts at the hip joints and clip-locked backpack armour mean poses hold without parts dropping off. The drawbacks are just as clear. Part counts regularly run to three or four thousand pieces, build time is long, prices sit at the high end for domestic brands, and a mecha this size demands serious shelf space. Measure your display spot before you commit.

## How to choose: match the brand to what you actually need

If your budget is limited and you want to test the water, pick a SEMBO entry-level mecha in the 100 RMB range. If part precision matters and you intend to modify your builds later, go with Gobricks. If you want visual impact and are willing to put in the hours, go straight to a Panlos flagship. One warning here: when reading mecha brick recommendations, look past the glamour shots and check the articulation range and joint-holding figures in reviews. Some mecha look great standing still but cannot raise an arm, and every photo relies on camera angle, so the set ends up gathering dust.

## An overlooked trap: mixing parts

Recolouring, swapping parts and adding equipment is normal in mecha building, which makes part compatibility critical. Gobricks has the most complete general-purpose parts range, SEMBO comes second, and some large Panlos pieces use in-house specifications, so confirm compatibility before mixing them with mainstream parts. Before you start modifying a model, write out a parts list and order only what is missing. That works out far cheaper than buying whole boxes for a few pieces.
