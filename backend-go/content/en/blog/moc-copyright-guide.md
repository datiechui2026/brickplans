# MOC Instructions and Copyright: A Guide for Creators and Fans

As LEGO building culture has spread, MOC (My Own Creation) building has become the way more and more fans express their ideas. A strong MOC carries a lot of the builder's time, care and design thinking, which is why copyright around MOC instructions now gets so much attention. Whether you have been designing for years or just posted your first model, the basics are worth knowing: respect original work, and use other people's files within the rules.

## What copyright means for MOC instructions

MOC instructions are the building guide a creator draws up so that their original model can be rebuilt, usually including a parts list, step-by-step assembly diagrams and any technical notes needed to complete the build. Under copyright law, an original set of instructions counts as a "graphic work" or a "written work" and is protected from the moment it is finished. No registration is required to be legally recognised.

One point deserves special emphasis: copyright protects the specific expression in the instructions, not the abstract design idea or the building technique behind it. If two builders present the same structure in different ways, each of them holds a separate copyright in their own version. This is the single most misunderstood part of MOC instruction copyright, and it cuts both ways: you cannot claim someone infringed simply because their model resembles yours, but you also cannot excuse copying by saying the design was obvious.

## Where copyright problems usually come from

In practice, disputes in the MOC community cluster around three situations.

**Sharing instructions without permission.** Collecting someone else's instructions, paid or freely released, into a bundle and re-uploading it to a cloud drive or an online marketplace is infringement, even if your own name never appears on the file and even if you charge nothing for it.

**Copying and light rewrites.** Taking another builder's instructions, making small changes such as swapping part colours or adjusting the camera angles, and publishing the result as original work is both unethical and an infringement of the original creator's rights. Cosmetic edits do not make a new work.

**Blurry commercial lines.** Building from someone else's instructions at volume and selling the finished models normally requires explicit permission from the author. Without that permission, a dispute is almost inevitable, and the fact that you bought the parts yourself does not change the position.

## How creators can protect their instructions

For MOC creators, a few proactive steps meaningfully lower the risk of being copied.

First, publish with a clear copyright notice that states what use is allowed and what is prohibited, so there is no room for a reader to assume permission. Second, use high-resolution preview images with a watermark and avoid releasing the copyable source files directly. Third, keep a record of the design process, including drafts, render screenshots and timestamps; these can serve as evidence if you ever need to defend your work. Fourth, publish on a platform that has copyright protection mechanisms built in, which reduces theft at the source rather than after the fact.

None of this makes a model unshareable. A clear licence plus good preview images lets people admire and even build your design while keeping control of the file itself in your hands.

## How fans can use MOC instructions legitimately

For builders on the downloading side, respecting copyright is not difficult. Before you download instructions, check the licence terms the author has published. If you want to use the file for anything beyond personal building, such as a teaching session or a commercial activity, contact the author first and get permission. When you pass instructions on to someone else, credit the source and name the original creator. And if you come across infringing uploads, report them to the platform rather than quietly circulating them.
