# Hospital Brick Scene Layout: Exam Rooms, Wards and ER

A hospital is the most dramatic building on any city block: hurrying patients, nurses pushing carts, an ambulance pulling out with lights flashing. Yet plenty of builders finish the facade, park a vehicle outside and call it done — lift the roof and you find an empty shell. This guide breaks the hospital down into zones, circulation and detail passes, so you end up with a scene that photographs well from any angle.

## Plan zones and circulation before you lay a single wall

The classic mistake is cramming rooms together until every corridor is blocked. Before you place bricks, sketch four functional blocks onto a 48x32 or 64x32 baseplate: the reception desk at the front entrance, an exam-room wing along one side, wards set deeper into the building, and an emergency bay with its own separate entrance.

The emergency bay should sit against the side or back of the building, with its door opening straight onto a stretch of roadway so the ambulance can park — a brick-built version of the ER's dedicated access lane. Leave a 6-stud-wide main corridor through the middle; two minifigures can walk side by side without clipping.

## Exam rooms: desk, exam table and diagnostic gear

The exam room appears more often than any other space in a hospital, and a standard one works fine at 8x8 studs. The core trio: a doctor's desk (a 1x4 plate for the top, dark legs below), a 2x6-stud exam table (prop the legs on 1x1 cylinder pieces so it reads as clinical rather than domestic), and one piece of diagnostic equipment — a 1x2 side-mounted bracket holding a printed screen tile gives you an ECG machine or a blood-pressure monitor.

Put a waiting chair by the door and a white sticker with a red cross on the wall, and the room suddenly looks like it has already seen patients today.

## Inpatient wards: bed detail decides how real it feels

Wards sit deeper inside the building — quieter, more private — and are usually built two or three in a row. Each one holds a 2x6-stud hospital bed. Build a nightstand from 1x1 bricks in a light color at the head end and stand a small transparent cup on it as a water glass. Keep the bed itself pure white, and cap the foot with a red 1x2 plate to suggest a folded blanket edge.

Fit an openable window on the outer wall, and place two leaf pieces outside it to read as tree shadows — the whole scene warms up immediately. Put the nurse station at the entrance to the ward corridor, enclosed by a half-height counter. Stack a pile of 1x1 white plates on top as patient files and station a minifigure with a treatment cart beside it.

## Emergency bay: the denser the equipment, the higher the tension

This is the part of the hospital scene that delivers the biggest payoff, and it also drains your parts bin fastest. Three essentials. First, a wheeled gurney — swap the bed feet for wheel elements. Second, a heart monitor: stack 1x2 transparent bricks and top them with a red transparent piece as an indicator light. Third, an oxygen cylinder: a 1x1 cylinder plus a gray 1x1 round plate, with a green printed tile wrapped around the body.

Hang a ventilator shape on the wall from 1x2 white curved slope bricks, and lay a folded stretcher on the floor. Dense equipment in cold white tones means that the moment you lift the roof, the room reads as a resuscitation in progress. Don't forget to mark a yellow no-parking zone at the bay door, flowing into the roadway.

## Reception and pharmacy: the ground-floor face of the building

The ground floor sets the first impression, and reception plus pharmacy carry it. Curve the counter corners with curved pieces, stand a 1x2 dark gray plate upright on the top as a registration kiosk with a screen sticker, and put a white-coat minifigure behind it. Place two or three waiting minifigures in front — some seated, some standing — and the scene comes alive.

Build the pharmacy as a half-open counter: shelves from 1x2 bricks stacked three or four courses high, each course lined with rows of 1x1 transparent bricks as medicine bottles, and a small counter element for the register. Pack the shelves until they look full. Once the density is there, the professional hospital feel arrives on its own.

## Ambulance and finishing touches: put the scene in motion

Park a red-and-white ambulance MOC outside the main door: white plates plus red warning lights for the roof light bar, a black printed cross on the side, and rear doors built as two openable panels. Set a stretcher cart and a paramedic minifigure at the emergency bay door so the action flows continuously from vehicle to building.

Finish the main block in red, white and light gray, top the roof with a sign — a cross or a medical emblem — lay gray sidewalk plates in front, and plant two street trees. That's when the hospital scene is genuinely complete.

From zoning and circulation to exam tables, emergency equipment and the ambulance, every detail is a source of realism.
