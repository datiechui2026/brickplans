# LEGO MOC Design for Beginners: Idea to Finished Build

Many builders first meet MOC culture by staring at someone else's masterpiece — impressed, and completely unsure where to begin. LEGO MOC design is far less mysterious than it looks. It works more like a repeatable pipeline: define a theme, gather parts, sketch, model in software, then test build and iterate. Once you can run that pipeline smoothly, an original model is only a matter of time.

## Step 1: Choose a Theme and Collect References

The first step in LEGO MOC design is always deciding what you are actually making. It could be an old house from your memory, a scene from a movie, or a pure structural experiment. The more specific the theme, the easier it is to execute: "a small Japanese shop" lands far more easily than "a house." A concrete subject quietly answers a dozen later questions — what scale to work at, which parts matter most, which colors belong in the palette.

Once the theme is set, collect reference photos, sort through the imagery you want to work from, and write two or three lines of design notes. Those notes act as the anchor for the entire process and stop you drifting off course halfway through the build.

## Step 2: Collect and Sort Your Parts

With the theme fixed, inventory what you already own. Sort your parts by color and type first — knowing your parts library is the foundation every design rests on, and a sorted bin is also much faster to pull from while building. If some parts are badly short, buy them ahead of time, and clear the parts you have plenty of duplicates of first.

Many veteran MOC builders maintain a dedicated parts pool, stocking up on common bricks, plates and slopes so that their ideas are not limited by whatever happens to be in the bin. Do this step properly and the modeling stage that follows goes much more smoothly.

## Step 3: Sketch by Hand, Then Model in Software

A sketch is the first step in making an idea concrete. It does not need to be neat — just mark the massing, the proportions and the color scheme clearly enough that you could build from it. Rough blocks on paper are enough to expose a shape that will not work before you spend hours on it.

Then move into software modeling. The mainstream MOC design tools are friendly to newcomers: they support drag-and-drop brick placement, count parts automatically, and can export instructions directly. Inside the software you can flip the view at any moment and change colors quickly, which makes it the best place to correct proportions and validate structure before any real parts are involved. Once the model is finished, the parts list is generated for you, ready for ordering.

## Step 4: Test Build and Iterate

Instructions are only a waypoint. Real LEGO MOC design happens in repeated test builds. Build the main body with the parts you already have to check whether the structure is stable and the proportions read well, then swap in the target parts step by step. Problems during a test build are completely normal — a roof that is too heavy and crushes the walls below, corner gaps that will not line up. Write each problem down and fix them one at a time; this round after round of iteration is where a model's quality actually comes from.

## Common Mistakes and Practical Tips

The trap beginners fall into most often is going too big — trying to recreate a grand scene on the very first attempt, running out of parts, and leaving the project unfinished. Keep your first few builds to a small size of 200 to 300 parts and get the whole process working end to end before you scale up.

On color, start with one main color plus two accent colors and keep the scheme from getting busy. Structurally, rely on interlocking (overlapping brick seams) and avoid long unsupported plates. After each finished model, review where you wasted parts and which areas worked best — your LEGO MOC design level will rise noticeably on the next build.
