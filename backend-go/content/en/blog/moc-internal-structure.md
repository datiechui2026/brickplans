# MOC Internal Structure: Frames, Layering and Load Paths

Start with the conclusion: the internals decide how long a MOC survives. The rule of thumb is beams for long spans, bricks for vertical supports, SNOT for direction changes, and Technic parts wherever load is carried. Frame first, exterior last.

Most builders open a MOC instruction file and look straight at the outside. But what actually decides whether a model works is the part you never see: the internal structure. Building a proper interior is not a matter of stuffing bricks inside until the shell feels full. It involves frame selection, layering logic, load paths and space planning. Here is how that works in practice.

## Why the internals matter more than the exterior

If you get an exterior detail wrong, you can pull it apart and rebuild it. If the internals are loose, the model will come apart when you carry it, handle it, or put it on display. The first goal of any internal build is to make the model stand up, pick up cleanly and sit steady. Second, the internals control how flat the exterior turns out: wavy walls and visible gaps are usually a frame that is out of alignment, not bad modelling. Third, good internals save parts. Once the frame carries the load, the outer skin does not need stacks of bricks added as reinforcement.

## Choosing a frame: beams, bricks and SNOT

Three workhorses do most of the job: Technic beams, standard bricks and SNOT (Studs Not On Top).

- **Technic beams:** the right choice for roof beams, cantilevers and any long horizontal span. Beams with pin holes joined by Technic pins resist bending far better than stacked bricks, and for a vehicle chassis or a mech, a beam spine uses the fewest parts.
- **Standard bricks:** the right choice for columns and walls. Stagger 2x2 and 2x4 bricks the way you would lay a real wall, then add 1x2 bricks as an inner liner so the wall cannot split.
- **SNOT:** when you need to turn the stud direction 90 degrees or attach a flat panel, a SNOT brick does it. Remember that SNOT connections are limited in strength, so keep load-bearing points on Technic parts.

When you plan the internals, one line covers most decisions: long spans use beams, vertical supports use bricks, direction changes use SNOT, and load goes back to Technic.

## Layering logic: building from the inside out

The most commonly skipped part of internal construction is the order of assembly. Build the frame first, then the liner, then the exterior, working module by module:

1. **Base frame:** surround a grid-shaped chassis with Technic beams and cross-brace all four corners. This is the load-bearing foundation for the whole model.
2. **Vertical supports:** raise Technic beams or brick columns at the four corners and at every load point, cut to the target floor height.
3. **Horizontal tie-in:** lay plates as floor slabs and add diagonal bracing between beams so the assembly cannot twist.
4. **Exterior skin:** tile and shape the model onto the frame. The structure is already carrying the load, so you can build freely.

After each layer, press down and rock the model to check for slack, then add bricks or pins immediately. Fixing it layer by layer is far more efficient than stripping the whole thing down later.

## Reinforcing corners and load paths

Corners are the weakest point in any interior. Two walls meet at 90 degrees with very little contact and tend to split. Three techniques handle it. First, **corner locking bricks**: alternate 1x2 bricks across both faces of the corner so the two walls grow into one. Second, a **corner column**: stand a Technic beam inside the corner and pin each wall to it. Third, a **top tie beam**: run a horizontal beam above the corner to hold the stress down.

On load paths, anywhere heavy sits above (a roof, a minifigure floor, a moving mechanism) needs a support column directly underneath. Never leave a load hanging over open space. A working rule is at least one support column per 4x4 stud area, with diagonal bracing to share the load where spans get long.

## Using the interior space and routing cables

Internals are not only structure; they are usable volume. In a building MOC, plan lift shafts, stairwells and cable runs in advance. Leave a 1x1 hole at a floor corner as a lighting cable channel so wires travel inside the structure and no cable is visible from outside. Mount gears and motors close to the frame, and run drive shafts along the pin holes in the beams for a stable, compact result. Leave SNOT studs on the side of the frame so interior furnishings can be hung in later. Good internals give every space a purpose instead of simply filling it.

Internal structure is the hidden engineering of a MOC, and it decides both the lifespan of the model and how it feels in the hand. Solid internal building is what makes a model both stable and good-looking. ## FAQ

**Q: Why do MOC internals matter more than the exterior?**
A: An exterior mistake can be pulled apart and rebuilt at any time, but loose internals mean the model falls apart when carried, handled or displayed. The internals also determine how flat the walls are and how wide the gaps run, and a good internal build saves a large number of reinforcement bricks.

**Q: Which parts work best for an internal frame?**
A: One line covers it: long spans use beams, vertical supports use bricks, direction changes use SNOT, load goes back to Technic. Roof beams and cantilevers use Technic beams with pins, which resist bending far better than stacked bricks, while columns and walls use staggered 2x2 and 2x4 bricks.

**Q: What is the right layering order?**
A: Frame first, then liner, then exterior. Surround a grid-shaped chassis with Technic beams and brace the four corners, raise supports at the corners and load points, lay floor plates with diagonal bracing, then tile and shape the outside. Press and rock the model after each layer to check for slack.

**Q: How do you reinforce corners and load-bearing points?**
A: Three methods: corner locking bricks (alternating 1x2 bricks across both faces), a corner column (a Technic beam inside the corner with pins to both walls), and a top tie beam to hold the stress down. A working rule is at least one support column per 4x4 stud area, and never leave a load unsupported.
