# Weather Station MOC Tutorial: Field, Screen, Anemometer

A weather station is one of the few MOC subjects that feels scientific and everyday at the same time. A white louvered screen, a tall wind vane, spinning cups — drop them into a brick scene and you immediately get that "observing nature" mood. This tutorial takes a miniature observation site from plan to finished model.

## 1. Overall Planning: Stake Out the Site First

Build the weather station at **1:48 scale** on a 32x32 green baseplate, standing in for a standard observation field. Follow the front-row/back-row logic of a real station: louvered screen and rain gauge up front, wind vane and anemometer mast behind, with a small path running between them. It reads correctly and gives the finished model depth.

**Color scheme**: keep the main body white and light gray (the classic weather-instrument palette), with a little blue for sky and green for grass. One rule — the plainer the equipment, the more real the scene looks.

## 2. Core Structure: The Louvered Screen Is the Heart

The louvered screen is the star of the model and the most detail-critical part, so build it first.

1. **Base**: stack two 4x4 plates to roughly 8 studs of height and add 1x1 bricks at the four corners as legs, so the box floats off the ground — exactly how a real screen is vented from below.
2. **Housing**: run rows of 1x4 white plates in a staggered, offset pattern to create the louver effect. One trick: slip a 1x1 plate between each louver layer to hold the vent gap open, so light can pass through and the look stays convincing.
3. **Roof**: butt two 2x4 wedge slopes together into a gable roof, in dark gray so it contrasts with the body.
4. **Interior**: place a white thermometer (a 1x1 transparent round plate plus a thin cylinder works) and two minifigures, as if the duty officer is taking a reading.

The key to a good screen is **even gaps**. Look at it from the side when you finish: the louver lines should be dead straight. If they wobble, your base plates aren't aligned — pull it apart and re-lay them early rather than later.

## 3. Equipment Details: Wind Vane and Rain Gauge

**Wind vane**: use a 1x16 technic axle as the mast, with a cross pin at the top holding an arrow-shaped piece (build it from 1x2 slope bricks) and a tail made from two 1x1 tiles. Anchor the mast foot with a 2x2 base brick so it won't fall over when the "wind" blows — meaning when you move the model around.

**Rain gauge**: stack two transparent blue 1x1 round bricks, add a white funnel underneath (a 2x2 round plate with a 1x1 step-in transition), and set it on a small dark gray stand. For a "just rained" effect, scatter a few transparent blue 1x1 pieces around the rim to suggest droplets.

**Anemometer mast**: at the top, three 1x1 red ball joint pieces connect three hemispheres into spinning cups. The rotating shaft is a technic cross pin, so the cups genuinely turn — kids will play with that for a long time.

## 4. Scene Details and Pitfall Checklist

Add a low white fence around the edge of the field (1x2 bricks with holes plus thin rods threaded through), one small tree, and two gray stone slabs for the path — the station instantly comes alive. Don't forget a 2x4 white tile in one corner of the baseplate as a record board, with a sticker for the temperature.

**Pitfalls to avoid**:
- Always pin mast-type parts down with base bricks, or they work loose with repeated handling.
- Keep transparent parts away from colored parts where possible — they scratch easily, so bag them separately when storing.
- The floating screen structure can only carry so much, so don't pile weight on the roof; keeping it light is what makes it look right.

## 5. Final Notes

This weather station sits at medium difficulty and uses common parts, which makes it an ideal step-up practice build.
