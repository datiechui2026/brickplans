# Carousel MOC Build Guide: Gear Reduction and Lift Linkage

Bottom line first: aim for a total reduction ratio of about 1:15 so the carousel turns at a believable pace, keep every cam at the same phase so the animals rise together, and if this is your first attempt, build from plans rather than improvising.

Among amusement-park brick MOCs, the carousel is both the most photogenic and the hardest on drivetrain design. The canopy on top has to spin, the animals below have to orbit while bobbing up and down, and those two motions have to stay in sync. This guide breaks the build into four parts: the center shaft, the gear drive, the canopy, and the lift linkage.

## Center Shaft Rotation: Where All Motion Starts

The heart of a carousel is a single **center shaft**. Use a thick cross axle running vertically through the base, with the top end attached to the canopy frame and the middle section driving the animal platform. Add **axle bushes as limit stops** at both ends so the shaft cannot slide up and down while turning. Leave a bearing seat inside the base — a friction piece with an axle hole or an axle connector can stand in for a real bearing — so rotation stays smooth and free of wobble.

Power enters from the bottom. Mount a motor or a hand crank on one side of the base and use **bevel gears** to turn horizontal motion into vertical drive on the center shaft. Bevel gears are the standard choice here; get the meshing angle right and the rotation will run smoothly.

## Gear Drive: Reduction Is the Whole Secret

A carousel needs a **large reduction ratio**. Spin it too fast and it stops looking like a fairground ride and starts looking like an electric fan. Plan for at least two stages: a 1:3 gear pair at the motor output, then a 1:5 pair at the canopy end, for a total ratio around 1:15. That gives a moderate speed with a calm, elegant feel.

Three things go wrong with gear drives. First, **gear module has to be consistent** — mixing gears from different systems produces unreliable meshing. Second, **axle centers must line up**; use beams plus axle seats to lock the transmission path into a single straight line so the gears cannot bite at an angle. Third, gather the gear train into one removable **gearbox** so maintenance and speed changes stay easy later on.

## Canopy: The Showpiece and the Structural Load

The simplest way to build the canopy is a cone of **slope bricks**, edged with curved pieces on the outer rim and finished with light pieces or colored accents. A canopy is heavy, so do not skimp on the load-bearing structure. Support the frame with a large cross-axle hub at the center, then radiate 6 to 8 beams outward as ribs. The canopy stays rigid that way without crushing the drivetrain underneath.

You can also hang a ring of **decorations** under the canopy — small flags or colored balls. They travel with the canopy as it rotates and add a lot of visual value for very little cost.

## Animal and Seat Lift Linkage: The Soul of the Carousel

The bobbing motion comes from a **cam lift linkage**. Install a ring of cams (or eccentric axles) under the animal platform, with each seat post pressing down on its matching cam through a connecting rod. As the center shaft turns, the cams turn with it, the posts ride the cam height difference, and the animals get that classic turn-and-rise rhythm.

To make every animal rise in sync, **all cams must share the same phase** — mount them on one ring axle or one drive ring, with their eccentric directions aligned. A display MOC only needs 6 to 8 animal positions, and each animal base should have a cross-axle socket so you can swap in different figures.

## Common Pitfalls and Where to Get Plans

- **Cam seizure**: leave 1 to 2 plate thicknesses of lift travel between the cam and the post instead of pressing them tight together.
- **Canopy sag**: soft rib beams collapse under load — switch to Technic beams or thicker plates.
- **Noise and stalling**: usually dust in the gears or a bent axle. Check that every axle spins freely as you assemble.

The carousel is a chain of interlocking structures, so building from plans is strongly recommended the first time. ## FAQ

**Q: How are the rotation and the lift synchronized on a carousel?**
A: Through the cam lift linkage. A ring of cams or eccentric axles sits under the animal platform, and each seat post presses onto its matching cam through a connecting rod. As the center shaft turns, the cams turn with it and the animals rise while they orbit.

**Q: How much gear reduction does a carousel need to look right?**
A: At least two stages: a 1:3 pair at the motor output, then a 1:5 pair at the canopy end, for roughly 1:15 overall. That gives a moderate speed with an elegant feel; spin it faster and it looks like an electric fan.

**Q: Where does a carousel most often jam?**
A: Between the cam and the post, where you need 1 to 2 plate thicknesses of lift travel instead of a tight fit. Canopy sag usually comes from rib beams that are too soft — use Technic beams or thicker plates. Noise and stalling usually trace back to dusty gears or a bent axle.

**Q: Which key parts should I have ready for a first carousel build?**
A: One thick cross axle running vertically through the base as the center shaft, with axle bushes as limit stops at both ends; bevel gears to convert horizontal drive into vertical drive; beams plus axle seats to hold the transmission in a straight line; and a removable gearbox to house the gear train.
