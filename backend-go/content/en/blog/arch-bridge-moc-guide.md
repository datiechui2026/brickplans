# How to Build a LEGO Arch Bridge MOC That Holds Its Shape

## Why the Arch Is the Smartest Structure You Can Build in Bricks

An arch bridge may be the most rewarding structure in all of brick building. On a real bridge the arch carries deck load along its curve and pushes it out into the abutments on both banks, which is why it can clear a wide span. A brick arch works the same way: you do not need a thick, heavy deck. Get the arch ring right and a thin deck will cross the river without complaint. Building an arch bridge MOC is really about trading structure for material, and that is exactly why it saves bricks and looks good at the same time. Once you understand the forces, your bridge will not collapse the moment you finish it, and it will survive being picked up and played with.

## How Arch Forces Work: Understand Before You Build

The secret of an arch is that **compression travels along the ring**. Weight on the deck comes down and splits along the curve into thrust that pushes toward the piers on each side. So the thing an arch bridge fears is not vertical weight but the two sides being forced outward. Two hard requirements follow from this. First, the arch ring must be **complete and continuous**, with no gap anywhere in the curve. Second, the piers at both ends must be **heavy and stable enough** to hold that outward thrust in place. Understand these two points and most arch bridge failures are prevented before you place a single brick.

## Laying the Arch Ring: The Curve Decides Everything

The arch ring is the heart of the bridge. The recommended method is to build the curve layer by layer from **wedge plates or slope bricks**, offsetting each course by half a stud so the line stays smooth. Never force flat bricks into a curve; it looks wrong and comes apart easily. A common layout is a double-layer ring: an inner layer of 1x2 slopes forms the main arc, an outer layer of wedge plates adds thickness, and the two layers are staggered so they interlock. Strength doubles immediately. Where the deck meets the ring, use small bricks to bite the two together, so that load on the deck travels firmly down into the arch ring instead of the two parts acting separately.

## Reinforcing Piers and Deck: Practical Methods

Piers are the foundation of the bridge. Build them in two parts, a base and a shaft. Make the base 2 studs wider than the shaft on every side and pack it with dark grey plates and bricks, so it is heavy and resists tipping. Build the shaft from interlocking plates up to the height of the ring's support point. Deck reinforcement comes mostly from **beams**: run two holed beams lengthwise under the deck and lock them into the piers at both ends, and the deck will not sag in the middle. If minifigures or small cars will cross, add another layer of plates as a road surface, and put low railings on both sides (posts plus bars) for safety and detail.

## Stone, Wood and Modern Arch Styles

Different materials call for completely different brick choices. For a **stone arch bridge**, the classic of classics, build the ring from dark grey and light grey slopes and wedge plates, pave the deck with old grey plates, and make stone pillar railings. A few dark studs hung under the arch read as shadow inside the opening and instantly give it age. A **wooden arch bridge** such as a covered bridge goes light brown and dark brown: lay brown plates crosswise to suggest planks, use brown tones for the ring, and add a roofed gallery on top as the finishing touch for a historical scene. A **modern arch bridge** is about line and material contrast: a slender white or light grey ring, transparent glass railings, a thinner deck, and a clean overall look. Pick whichever suits your scene, but the mechanics stay identical.

## Riverside Scene and Full Build Order

A good arch bridge needs a river and banks around it. Lay transparent blue plates for the water, build the banks from white or light grey plates, and place a tree and a few minifigures at each end of the bridge to complete the crossing. For the full build, work in this order. Step one: build the pier bases on both banks and fix the riverbed plates down. Step two: build the arch ring symmetrically outward from the bottom of the arch, holding pieces temporarily with small bricks as you go. Step three: add the deck beams and road plates. Step four: fit the railings and the decoration at each bridgehead. Step five: check that the arch ring has no gaps and that the piers are solid. Follow that sequence and you end up with a bridge you can display, play with, and even drive a small car across.

The fun of an arch bridge MOC is that a little knowledge of mechanics buys you a bridge that is both stable and good looking. Remember the three rules: the ring must be continuous, the piers must be heavy enough to hold the thrust, and the deck needs beams underneath. Then build in the order of piers, ring, deck, railings, and even a beginner can put a display-worthy arch bridge on the table.
