# Brick European Building MOC Guide: Arched Windows & Towers

Plenty of builders put together a **European building MOC** with a solid frame, then lose the look the moment the facade goes on: flat windows, bare roofs, colors fighting each other. The character of European architecture lives entirely in the facade details. Below, four styles — **German half-timbered, French Haussmann, Italian hill town, English countryside** — broken down into arched windows, bell towers, roofs and color schemes, all of it ready to build.

## Start by reading the four European styles

**German half-timbered (Fachwerk)** lives on exposed beams: white wall underneath, dark brown timber bracing in crosses and diamonds. Lay smooth white brick for the wall face, then apply 1x1 corner pieces or thin bar bricks to fake the beams, with angled hinge pieces or dedicated slope bricks for the diagonal bracing.

**Parisian Haussmann** is about order and grandeur: cream limestone walls, symmetrical full-height windows, a small wrought-iron balcony on every floor, and a mansard roof at the top. It rewards repetition and symmetry above everything — copy the same bay three to five times and the whole-street impression appears.

**Italian hill town** wins with warm color and layering: ochre, earth yellow and terracotta walls with dark green shutters, red clay tile roofs, and a ground floor built from 1x2 half-round plates laid in a row to suggest an arcade.

**English countryside** is quiet and soft: off-white and pale cream walls with dark timber doors and windows, steep dark grey slate roofs, tall chimneys. It is the most demanding on color — keep saturation low.

## Arched windows and surrounds: where depth comes from

Arched windows are the easiest place to shine in a **European building MOC** and the easiest place to fake it. Don't just stick a flat pane of glass on the wall:

- **Round-arch windows**: turn 1x1 round plates upside down to build the half-circle arch, set trans-clear pieces inside as glass, then ring the outside with 1x1 bricks as a surround. The depth reads instantly.
- **Window surrounds and moldings**: project a plate layer above the frame as a lintel, and use 1x1 corner bricks on both sides as capitals. For something richer, stack two plate layers into a stepped molding.
- **Sills and shutters**: run a 1x2 plate out below the window as a sill, and use light-colored horizontal bar bricks inside the frame to suggest timber louvers — especially right for Italian and German builds.

The only rule: **every window needs something that sticks out.** A window flush with the wall is basically a failure in European architecture.

## Bell towers and turrets: proportion before detail

The bell tower is the visual center of a church or town hall, and **proportion matters far more than detail**. The rule of thumb: tower body height is roughly 4 to 6 times its width, the belfry stage takes about one third of the tower body, and the spire is about half the height of the belfry section. Block out the shape in a single color first, step back and check the proportions read well, then refine. Stagger 1x1 corner bricks around the tower corners, and run a plate band as a belt course at each level so the tower rises in visible stages.

## Roofs and chimneys

Pick roof pitch by style: **German steep roofs** at about 45 degrees or more, using medium-to-tall roof tiles (33°/45° slopes); **Haussmann mansard roofs** are shallower, built from low-angle plates with tile edging; **Italian red clay** is a gentle pitch with an overhanging tile eave. Let the eave overhang by one stud before capping it — far more character than a flush edge.

**Chimneys** are a bonus detail on English and German buildings: roughly one third the width of the tower body, stacked from dark bricks (grey, brick red, dark brown), capped with a slope piece as a hood, and rising two to three bricks above the ridge. Stand two or three along the ridge and the rural feel arrives immediately.

## Exterior colors, balcony ironwork and carved details

For color, remember **one main color, one secondary, one accent**: Haussmann in cream plus white window surrounds plus black ironwork; Italian in terracotta plus earth yellow plus dark green shutters; half-timbered in white plus dark brown beams plus red tile. Use smooth bricks for wall faces and save textured bricks for old stone walls.

**Balcony ironwork** is the Haussmann signature: 1x1 bricks with holes plus banister pieces build a fine railing, curved pieces soften the corners, and a thin plate on top serves as the handrail. **Carved details** belong on door lintels, pediments and keystones — two or three spots per building is enough; pile on more and it looks cheap.

## Street layout: give the buildings rhythm

Layout runs on **varied heights, alternating color, and breathing room**: never let two neighbors match in height, offset them by half a floor to a full floor; alternate warm and cool facades; slip a narrow alley between two buildings using a divider or 1x2 bricks, then add a few trees, street lamps and minifigures and the life appears. Make corner buildings curved or chamfered and the street feels like it continues. When you're done, shoot it from the front, from 45 degrees and from a low angle — check that the eaves line up and the tower anchors the frame, then pull apart and rebuild whatever looks wrong.
