# Panlos Dinosaur Series Review: Models, Articulation, Value

After more than a decade of building, large creature MOCs have always been my favourite corner of the hobby, and the Panlos dinosaur line is the most convincing take on the subject to come out of the domestic Chinese scene in recent years. These are not static display pieces in the way a building kit is — a network of joints lets you pose them, and a finished model genuinely anchors a shelf. Here is a rundown of the popular models and what the building experience is actually like.

## Panlos Dinosaur Series: Theme and Build Quality

The line focuses on large-scale, realistic dinosaurs, with colour schemes drawn from real fossils and palaeoart reconstructions — flesh tones, grey-browns and tooth ivory sit together naturally. Structurally, the skeleton is built from a large number of ball joints and turntable pieces, then clad in scale-textured plates, so the result reads as mechanical without losing the animal's silhouette. Against domestic competitors at the same price, it sits in the top tier for detail work and mould precision: the teeth, dorsal spines and claws are all handled carefully, and it is clear the designers did their research.

## Popular Models: From T-Rex to Spinosaurus

The T-Rex is the undisputed flagship — the scale is striking, and it offers both a jaw that opens and closes and swinging arms, which makes it the most photogenic kit in the range. The Spinosaurus is the standout for its back sail, an instantly recognisable model that most dinosaur fans will end up owning. The Triceratops has a rounder body type, with the head shield and horns positioned accurately, and it photographs well. There are also mid-size options such as the pterosaur and the stegosaurus, with moderate part counts that suit anyone trying the series for the first time. Piece counts run from a few hundred to over a thousand depending on the model, and prices span a wide range, so there is an entry point at most budgets.

## How Articulated Is It? Joints and Posing

Articulation is the headline feature. The limbs connect via ball joints, so you can set a walking stride, a lowered head or a swung tail, and the head and neck usually carry several independent joints that allow a hunting posture — great for photography. One caveat: the more joints a section has, the less weight it carries, and the claws on the slender-armed models tend to sag under gravity. When posing, hunt for an angle where the centre of gravity is stable. My advice is to work every joint through its range straight after the build, get a feel for which are tight and which are loose, and only then decide how to pose the model and how long to leave it that way.

## Building Experience and Pitfalls to Avoid

The instructions are logically sequenced and the bags are sorted by colour and by body section. On the larger kits, it helps to build the head, torso and limbs separately and then combine them, which makes both moving and photographing the model easier. Three things to watch for. First, with so many ball joints, do not force anything — line the piece up with the slot before applying pressure, or you will get stress marks. Second, the scale plates are close in colour, so pre-sorting by shade saves a lot of time. Third, the finished models are bulky, so plan the display spot and a load-bearing base in advance, otherwise you are looking at dust build-up or a tip-over that takes out neighbouring sets.

## Verdict: Value for Money and Where to Find More Instructions

To sum up, the Panlos dinosaur series wins on faithful subject matter and articulation, and its value proposition holds up well against domestic rivals. It suits builders who like animals and biological subjects, and it works nicely as a parent-and-child project to spark a child's interest.
