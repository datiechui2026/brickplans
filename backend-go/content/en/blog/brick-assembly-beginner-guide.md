# Beginner Brick Building: How to Pick and Build Your First Set

Opening your first brick set is usually equal parts excitement and nerves: a table covered in tiny coloured parts, a thick instruction booklet, and no obvious place to start. It really is not as hard as it looks. A handful of basic habits will get you from sealed box to finished model without a mid-build crisis. This guide is written for anyone about to cut the tape on their very first set.

## How to Choose Your First Set

The core principle is simple: match the set to your patience. Start with the piece count. Somewhere between 300 and 800 pieces suits most beginners — too few and the build is over before you have settled in, too many and you risk losing momentum halfway through. Next, pick a theme you genuinely like: vehicles, architecture, animals, city street scenes, whatever draws your eye. You will only finish a build if the subject keeps you interested. Finally, consider the brand. The major international manufacturers offer high part precision and a satisfying feel, but they cost more; many Chinese brick brands have improved noticeably in recent years and deliver strong value for the money, so they are well worth a look too.

One more thing to check when shopping: whether the set bags its parts in stages. When each bag covers one phase of the build, finding parts becomes far easier — a big advantage for newcomers.

## Reading the Instructions Is the Core Skill

Before you tear open any bag, spend a few minutes reading the instructions from cover to cover to understand the overall structure and build order. The part legend works like the key on a map: it shows what each part looks like and its number. When you reach a step, check which parts that step uses and how many of each you need, then go hunting in the bags.

Search by shape first, colour second. Shape is the strongest clue; colour is secondary. The same shape often appears in several colours, so never grab a part on colour alone — plenty of beginners lose parts simply because a colour caught their eye first.

## Sorting Parts Saves Serious Time

Before you tip anything out, set out a few small boxes or trays. Empty a bag and roughly sort by shape: plates in one pile, bricks in another, then slopes, technic parts and small pieces. The sorting does not need to be fine-grained — broad categories are enough — and this single step will save you a lot of rummaging.

As the build progresses, return the leftover parts from each finished stage to their categories and keep the table clear. A tidy surface is not just pleasant to work on; it also stops small pieces from rolling off and disappearing.

## Pressing Home and Staying Aligned

By far the most common mistake is not pressing pieces down properly. Every part should go on with a clean click, and you should check that the two pieces sit completely flush with no gap between them. Many sets include a pressing tool, which is genuinely useful for large plates and tightly packed structures.

Alignment matters just as much. Look carefully at the anchor points and stud direction shown for every layer; get the orientation wrong and you will usually have to pull the section apart and start again. Check each step as you finish it rather than pressing on — confirming alignment as you go is far less painful than discovering a mistake thirty steps later.

## Taking Pieces Apart and Storing Them

Mistakes happen, especially at the start. Use a dedicated part separator when you need to undo something: ease it into the seam and lever gently. Never force pieces apart by hand — you can hurt your fingers and damage the parts. If a connection is especially tight, wiggle it slightly left and right before pulling.

If you do not plan to display a finished model long term, you can break it down and store the parts in sorting boxes by colour or type. Keep the instructions, the spare parts and any extras together so a rebuild or a modification is easy whenever the mood strikes.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

A few traps catch almost every beginner: jumping straight into a set with over a thousand pieces and running out of steam; ignoring the instructions and building by feel, which leaves the structure loose and forces rework; chasing speed instead of pressing each part fully home, so the finished model feels wobbly; and tipping every bag onto the table at once with no sorting, then hunting for parts until you give up. Dodge these and the whole experience gets much smoother.

Once your first model is finished, you will probably find the hobby more addictive than expected.
