# LEGO Mars Rover MOC: Six-Wheel Rocker-Bogie Suspension Guide

Start with the conclusion: the heart of any brick-built Mars rover is its six-wheel rocker-bogie suspension. Run one 9L or 11L Technic beam down each side as the main rocker so all six wheels can travel independently and stay planted on uneven ground, then add folding solar panels on top. Everything else — arm, mast, instruments — is decoration around those two systems.

## Six-Wheel Suspension: Building the Rocker-Bogie

The suspension is the part of a Mars rover MOC most worth studying. Real rovers such as Curiosity use a rocker-bogie arrangement that keeps all six wheels touching the ground over rough terrain. In brick it is not hard to reproduce: use one 9L or 11L Technic beam per side as the main rocker, pivot it at its midpoint to the chassis with a cross axle, then hinge a shorter rocker to each end — one carrying the front wheel, the other carrying the middle and rear wheel pair.

The critical detail is at the pivots. Use friction axle bushes or friction pins at every hinge point so the suspension has some damping and does not sag under the model's own weight. For wheels, standardise on large off-road tyres — 56x26 or 49x36 — with dark grey hubs. That single choice instantly reads as "Mars rover" before you have added a single instrument.

## Suspension Travel: Letting Each Wheel Move on Its Own

A rocker alone is not enough. The suspension needs real vertical travel. Add a vertical shock absorber spring (the Technic spring shock unit) between the main rocker and the chassis, or build a simple elastic setup from a 2x2 brick with holes plus rubber bands.

Watch the travel limits: keep the rocker swing within about 20 degrees, otherwise the inner wheel will scrape the underside of the chassis in turns. To test, prop the model on a stack of books to simulate broken ground and press down on each wheel in turn. Every wheel should move up and down independently and spring back smoothly. Get this right and the sight of six wheels rippling over a "rock field" is more convincing than any static display.

## Solar Panels: Foldable Wings

The solar array is the most recognisable part of the rover. Use 8x16 or 6x12 dark blue smooth tiles as the panel surface and finish the edges with 1x1 slopes to suggest the segment lines of a real solar cell array.

Build the deployment mechanism from hinge parts: mount a row of 1x2 hinge plates with holes on the inner face of each panel and connect them to a 2x4 plate on the roof, so the panel folds forward 90 degrees for stowage and swings back out flat. If you want the panel to hold at several angles, add a friction cross axle bush to the hinge axle — the panel will then stay wherever you put it instead of flopping down. For colour, keep the cells dark blue and the support struts silver grey so they contrast against the body.

## Chassis, Robot Arm and Instrument Details

Build the chassis as a ladder frame from Technic parts: two long beams as the rails, three short beams as cross members, with space left in the middle for a battery box and circuit board.

On top, add a two-stage robot arm made from 3L links and a universal joint, with a 2x2 turntable at the tip as the sample drill — it rotates, so you can act out the sampling sequence. For details: a mast built from a 1x1 round bar with two transparent elements as camera eyes, a 2x4 smooth tile at the rear as a radiator panel, and curved pieces wrapping the wheel arches. None of these parts are rare, but together they are instantly readable.

The classic rover palette is a white body with orange-gold accents. Use 1x2 orange bricks as trim strips along both flanks and over the wheel arches; the orange echoes the thermal blanket colour of the real probes.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I build the six-wheel suspension for a brick Mars rover?**
A: Use a rocker-bogie layout: one 9L or 11L Technic beam per side as the main rocker, secondary rockers and axle mounts at both ends, and a hinged connection to the chassis in the middle. All six wheels then move up and down independently.

**Q: How do I make each wheel move independently?**
A: Give every axle its own swing arm travel. Hinge the secondary rocker to the main rocker, add a shock absorber to soak up impacts, and leave enough swing range for the wheels to follow uneven ground.

**Q: How do I make the solar panels fold?**
A: Build a rectangular panel from plates and tiles and connect its base to the chassis with hinge parts so it can deploy and stow. Add stepped stops or friction hinges to lock the angle, which also lets you pose the panel in different sun-tracking positions for photos.

**Q: How should I test the suspension once it is built?**
A: Run three checks: push the rover over a book step and see whether all six wheels stay in contact; lift one wheel and confirm the others still touch; then check the rocker pivots for play and add bushes if needed.

New builders hit three problems most often: suspension so soft the chassis bottoms out, wheels rubbing the body in turns, and solar panels that wobble. For bottoming out, fit a stiffer spring or shorten the rocker; for rubbing, shave half a stud off the inside of the wheel arch or limit steering to 30 degrees; for loose panels, add a friction bush on the hinge axle. When the build is finished, run three tests: roll it in a straight line on flat ground, drive it over a two-brick-high "rock", and open and close the panels 20 times. Pass all three and your rover is ready to "land" in the display cabinet.
