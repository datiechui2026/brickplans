# Huizhou Architecture MOC: Horse-Head Walls and Grey Tiles

Bottom line first: the soul of a Huizhou MOC is the horse-head wall. Hang three or four stepped tiers off the facade with SNOT, mix white and off-white for the plaster, and alternate light and dark grey on the roof tiles for the best result.

Huizhou architecture is the most recognisable subject in the Chinese brick-building genre, and it is also the one that tests your fundamentals hardest. White walls, dark tiles, horse-head walls — nail those few elements and the viewer instantly reads a southern Anhui village. This tutorial walks through the planning approach, the part techniques, the colour scheme and the usual pitfalls, so you can skip the trial and error.

## Set the scale and massing first

A Huizhou house is typically a single courtyard deep, one hall per bay. Before you build, decide the facade width and depth. On a 32x32 baseplate, 16 studs wide by about 20 studs deep gives the building room to breathe. Keep each storey to 12 bricks tall including the floor slab, or the whole thing will look squat. Sketch a rough plan on paper and mark the horse-head wall positions before you open a single bag — it is far more efficient than adjusting as you go.

## The horse-head wall is the whole build

The horse-head wall is a stepped fire gable, and the usual approach is SNOT side-building: attach 1x2 bricks with studs on the side to the outer face of the wall to hang the steps, raising each tier by 2 layers and pushing it out 1 stud so the profile drops away in stages. Cap the top of the wall with 1x2 dark grey slopes turned upside down to suggest the tile eaves. Three or four tiers is enough; more than that and the wall swallows the rest of the model. Make sure the drop direction matches at both ends, otherwise the facade reads as crooked.

## White plaster and dark tiles: keep it plain, not flat

The palette has to stay restrained without going lifeless. Mix white and off-white bricks in the walls so you never get a dead flat white face. Lay the roof with alternating dark grey and black 1x2 slopes to imitate the tonal shift of traditional grey tiles. A 45-degree slope is the easiest way to get the roof pitch; if you want more refinement, use 33-degree long slopes over a SNOT roof frame and let the eaves overhang slightly for extra depth.

## The gatehouse is your front page

Above the doorway, run SNOT and lay 1x1 printed bricks or patterned tiles sideways to build up the brick-carved relief. Use wedge bricks on both sides of the door hood for the splayed corner walls, then make the door leaves from brown plates with two 1x1 gold round plates as knockers — the detail instantly comes alive. Do not skip the central courtyard, the "four waters return to the hall" layout; it is the light source of a Huizhou house. Frame a 4x4 opening in the roof, pave the ground with light grey plates, and the sense of space opens up immediately.

Also don't overlook the small parts that punch above their weight, like the ridge and the eave tiles. Cap the main ridge with 1x2 inverted slopes and lift the two ends slightly with a hinge raised by 1 layer. Hang a 1x1 round plate every few studs along the eaves as a tile end, and the shadows in sunlight fall in a satisfying stagger, lifting the level of finish a notch. Inside, place an eight-immortals table — a brown plate with four 1x1 round plates for legs — and a pair of round-back chairs. Viewed through the doorway, the model suddenly feels lived in.

## Common pitfalls

First, the joint between the horse-head wall and the roof tends to leave gaps: always build the roof first, then attach the wall. Second, white bricks vary noticeably between production batches, so buy them all in one go if you can. Third, don't pack the roof tiles too tightly — leave some thickness at the eaves, or the roof ends up looking like a biscuit. Keep the palette to white, light grey, dark grey and black, with brown reserved for the gatehouse; the result stays understated and readable. If you want existing references, search "Huizhou architecture" on brickplan for MOC instructions and study the step proportions of the horse-head wall and the gatehouse details — much faster than guessing on your own.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I build the horse-head wall on a Huizhou MOC?**

A: Use SNOT side-building: 1x2 bricks with studs on the side hang the steps off the outer wall face, each tier raised 2 layers and pushed out 1 stud to create the stepped drop, then cap the top with 1x2 dark grey slopes turned upside down. Three or four tiers is plenty.

**Q: How do I keep the white-wall, dark-tile palette from looking stiff?**

A: Mix white and off-white bricks in the walls so no face is dead white. Lay the roof with alternating dark grey and black 1x2 slopes to imitate the tonal shift of the tiles. A 45-degree slope is the easiest roof pitch; a slight eave overhang gives more depth.

**Q: What are the most common mistakes on a Huizhou MOC?**

A: Three of them. The joint between the horse-head wall and the roof leaks gaps, so build the roof before the wall. White bricks vary between batches, so buy them in one go. And don't pack the tiles too densely — leave thickness at the eaves or the roof looks like a biscuit.

**Q: Is the central courtyard worth building?**

A: Never skip it. It is the light source of a Huizhou house: frame a 4x4 opening in the roof, pave the ground with light grey plates, and the sense of space opens up at once. Add an eight-immortals table and round-back chairs and the view through the doorway feels lived in.
