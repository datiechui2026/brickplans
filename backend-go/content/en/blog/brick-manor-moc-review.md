# Brick Manor MOC Plans Reviewed: Facade, Courtyard, Parts

## The main manor styles

Most manor MOCs fall into one of four styles, and each asks something different of the builder.

**European manor.** This is the style that lives or dies on symmetry and grandeur. Plans typically include a three-storey main building, a central pediment and a Roman colonnade. The brick palette leans heavily on white arched windows, round column elements and toothed bricks for the cornice moulding. Part counts usually run past 1500 pieces, so it sits firmly in intermediate-to-advanced territory.

**English country manor.** Warmer and more rural: red brick exterior walls under a pitched gable roof, built with slope bricks and a chimney breast for the fireplace. It is a size smaller than the European version, which puts it within reach of newcomers.

**Chinese garden estate.** The courtyard builder's favourite. White walls and dark roof tiles, a moon gate, flying eaves — the roof is made from curved slope pieces and 1x1 round plates standing in for tiles. The part count is more fragmented than the other styles, and small pieces dominate.

**Farmstead.** The simplest option: a single-storey farmhouse plus a barn. Around 600 pieces is enough to get the look, which makes it the natural first manor build.

## Judging the main facade and courtyard

A plan's quality shows up in two places — how complete the main facade is, and how complete the courtyard is.

A good facade plan builds in three clear tiers: a ground floor base of large bricks, a middle floor carrying a run of large windows, and a top floor of small attic windows. That stepping is what gives the building its scale. At the colonnade, well-made plans use arrows to mark which way the capitals face, so the round column elements actually mate with the beam above instead of fighting it.

On the courtyard side, a mature plan hands you a garden laid out symmetrically on a central axis, with the fountain, flower beds and paths all lined up along that axis. A perimeter wall and gate then close the whole composition. The result is a full scene — not one building sitting alone on a baseplate with nothing around it.

## Parts list, difficulty and instructions

The parts list is where manor plans fail most often. A dependable brick manor MOC plan splits the list by colour and brick type, states the total piece count, and offers a substitution for every rare element. A weak plan gives you renderings and nothing else: miss a single roof tile or column element and you are reordering the entire batch.

Difficulty labelling matters too. Good plans state a suggested age and a build time, and a European manor is usually tagged something like "12+ / 8-10 hours".

For instructions, three things get my attention first:

- **Roof tile layering** shown step by step. If the flying-eave pitch drifts, the whole roof face collapses.
- **Colonnade built as a pre-assembled module** that is then attached, rather than raised one column at a time.
- **Repeated wall sections** flagged as "repeat N times", so you are not re-reading the same four steps a dozen times.

A plan that does all three is a noticeably better build.

## Build notes from my test build

I built the European main building together with its courtyard. Four areas took most of the thinking.

- **Cornice moulding on the facade.** Lay the ring of trim first, then add the arched windows. Do it the other way round and you rebuild.
- **Colonnade.** Assemble the entire row lying flat, then stand it up as a single unit. That is far more stable than stacking columns vertically one by one.
- **Garden fountain.** Trans-blue elements read as water jets, and the base needs a spot left free for a light.
- **Gate and perimeter wall.** Check hinge orientation before you close the walls, or the gate swings the wrong way.
- **Landscaping trees.** Brown cylinders for trunks, green plates for crowns, placed either side of the path as accents.

The full build ran about 8 hours. The hardest part was the roof tile pitch, which cost me one rebuild; everything else went smoothly.

## Who it suits, price, and MOC vs retail set

Manor MOCs suit builders who like architecture, street scenes and staging a scene rather than displaying a single model.

If you are new, start with the farmstead or the English country manor and leave the European main building until you have some street-scene experience behind you.

Plan prices mostly land in the 30-80 yuan range, and some sellers offer a free basic version. Loose parts to actually build it run roughly 300-600 yuan on top.

Compared with retail sets: a boxed manor builds straight from the box with every part present, but it usually gives you only the main building, no complete courtyard, and a fixed colour scheme. A MOC plan wins on flexibility — the courtyard can be extended, colours and floor heights can be changed, and the ceiling on accuracy is higher. The trade-off is that you source the bricks yourself.
