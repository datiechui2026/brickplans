# Zelda MOC Blueprint Review: Hyrule Castle, Master Sword, Link

As a longtime Zelda player, seeing the games rebuilt in brick never gets old. Over the past six months I have worked through several Zelda MOC blueprints, covering both landmark architecture and fan-favourite characters. Here is what each build is actually like from a builder's point of view — including the parts that will test your patience.

## Hyrule Castle: A Visual Showstopper With a Serious Parts Count

The Hyrule Castle blueprint is one of the most impressive Zelda MOCs I have come across. The layering is handled beautifully: the spires, the curtain walls and the drawbridge all read clearly from every angle, and the finished model has real presence in a display cabinet.

The trade-off is scale. This build runs past 2,500 pieces and close to twenty hours of work. Towers are built largely from slope bricks, so every step has to be lined up precisely — a small offset early on and the whole tower leans. If you take this one on, give yourself room to breathe and split it across three sessions rather than trying to force it in a weekend. Set the tower down on a flat surface and check it head-on every few rows; correcting a lean after ten layers is far more painful than catching it at three.

## Master Sword and Shield: Small Builds, Real Technique

Weapon blueprints are far more forgiving. The Master Sword I built comes in at roughly 200 pieces, and the blade is the interesting part: it is built sideways using SNOT technique so the sword reads as a narrow, flat blade rather than a chunky one. The crossguard uses a turntable piece to recreate the hexagonal crest, and the finished model has an almost metallic look to it.

The real surprise is that the blueprint also includes variant designs for the shield and the sheath. One blueprint, three display pieces — that is excellent value, and the whole thing is very approachable if you are new to MOCs and want to learn SNOT building without committing to a 2,500-piece project.

## Link Figure: The Face Does the Work

The difficulty in the Link figure sits entirely in the head. In the games, Link's expression is the character, and the blueprint handles it with 1x1 printed parts in different colours to build up the eyes and the layers of hair. The likeness is genuinely good for the scale.

The compromise is proportion: the head comes out slightly large for the body, which is noticeable if you care about that kind of detail. My suggestion is to adjust the colour of the hair pieces yourself once the figure is assembled — a small swap makes the head feel more balanced without touching the structure of the build.

## Two Tips for Beginners

1. Prioritise blueprints that come with a complete parts list. Nothing kills momentum like discovering a missing element halfway through a tower.
2. Start small. Work through a weapon or the Link figure first to get comfortable with the techniques, then move up to a castle-scale build once you know how you like to work.
