# LEGO Harbor Build Guide: Dock, Cranes and Container Ships

A harbor is one of my favorite large-scale builds after years of brick building. A complete harbor setup — dock platform, container yard, ship-to-shore crane, cargo ships coming and going, plus a stretch of water — looks spectacular and gives you endless loading and unloading scenes to play out. The trouble is that a scene this big has a lot of pieces and a messy layout, and plenty of builders stall halfway through. Here is my full process, from planning to the final details, so you can put your harbor together without the panic.

## Planning the Harbor Layout: Decide Before You Build

The first step in a harbor build isn't snapping bricks, it's planning. Measure the baseplates you actually have, then sketch a simple layout on paper: which area the dock platform occupies, where the container yard goes, where the crane stands, and how much water to leave. Keep one rule in mind — leave open "working lanes" so ships can dock, the crane can load and unload, and trucks can drive in and out without crossing each other. That's what makes a scene read as a real harbor.

A modular approach matters just as much. Split the harbor into a few independent zones, build each one on its own, and connect them later. If you want to add a warehouse or a lighthouse down the road, you just build one extra module instead of tearing the whole thing apart.

## Building the Dock Platform and Pier

The dock platform is the foundation of the whole harbor. Start by covering the ground with large baseplates, then run a platform out along the water side, sitting about one brick higher than the water surface — that height difference is what makes it read as a real quay. Along the platform edge, add a line of railings or mooring bollards; short posts and crossbars are all you need.

The pier connects the dock to the cargo ship. Build the deck from plates, add railings on both sides, and support it underneath with small columns. Don't forget a set of steps or a ramp so minifigures can get up and down. Once those details appear, the whole dock comes alive.

## The Container Yard: Tips for Batch Production

Containers are the visual stars of a harbor. Line up a yard of colorful boxes and the scene instantly smells like a working port. A standard container is simple to make: build a block from bricks, then apply a printed piece or sticker to the front face. Decide on a few fixed sizes, make a batch of each, and your output goes way up.

Mix the colors. Red, blue, green and white stacked together is what a real terminal looks like. When you stack them, build the yard two or three containers high, use slope bricks to suggest loading ramps, and park a container truck alongside — suddenly the yard has a story. Don't worry about making too many containers; they are the easiest element in the harbor to copy and paste.

## Crane Structure: The Centerpiece of the Scene

The crane is the most technical and most rewarding part of a harbor build. For a ship-to-shore gantry crane, I recommend a portal structure with two legs and a crossbeam. Mount a slide rail on the crossbeam, add a gear rack and a crank so the trolley travels left and right, and hang a container from the hoist cable. You can then act out a real loading cycle, and the play value jumps immediately.

If this is your first crane, practice on a proven set of crane plans first, understand how the structure works, then modify it yourself. Once the crane gets tall, watch the center of gravity — weight the base so it isn't top-heavy and doesn't tip over when someone bumps it.

## Cargo Ships and Water: Making the Harbor Move

The cargo ship is the bonus element that sells the scene. Build it in three sections: hull, deck and bridge. Start the hull with hull pieces, place containers on the deck, and keep the bridge small so the proportions look right. Add a small tugboat crossing the harbor basin and the picture immediately feels alive.

The water can be a base of transparent light-blue bricks with white wave studs on top to create a sense of swell. Offset the height between the quay and the water surface so photos have real depth. Dot a lighthouse and a few breakwater chunks around the edge, and the harbor's layers are complete.

## Finishing Up and Pitfalls to Avoid

A few pitfalls worth flagging. Harbor parts are a mixed bag, so sort everything into bins by color and function before you start. Large baseplates like to warp, so flatten them before building on top. Tall elements like the crane need counterweight at the base.

Follow the modular workflow and a complete harbor scene comes together in two to three weeks, and you can stop at any point along the way to take photos and post them for fellow builders.
