# Qiji Ship Series Review: Sailing Warship and Steamship Builds

Ship models are a narrow niche among Chinese brick brands, and the Qiji ship series is one of the few lines that has turned ships into a signature. Over the past six months I have built a Qiji sailing warship and one modern steamship set. This review goes through the bricks, the instructions and the finished models, and finishes with a straight answer on who should buy in.

## Bricks and Clutch: Steady, With a Few Bright Spots

The colour palette in the Qiji ship series fits the subject. The hulls are built in dark blue, red-brown and cream, and the shades come across as accurate rather than cheap.

Clutch is moderate across the board — comfortably looser than some domestic bricks that grip so hard you start doubting your own hands — and small pieces seat and pull apart smoothly. The weak point is the large hull plates. They fit on the loose side, and joints can show slight gaps.

That leads to the most useful thing I learned while testing: reinforce the deck with plates, working layer by layer. I did not do it on my first ship, and after six months in the display cabinet the bow had visibly sagged out of shape. If you plan to keep the model on show rather than take it apart, that extra layer of plates is worth the handful of parts.

Sail cloth and rope parts are where Qiji earns extra credit. The material is soft and easy to shape, and the sails hold their tension nicely.

## Instructions and Build Sequence

Qiji follows the big-booklet-plus-numbered-bags approach. The step breakdown is sensible and the bags line up with the build order, but the diagrams are on the small side, and dark parts are hard to tell apart on the page. In a dim room, picking the wrong piece is easy.

The biggest surprise on the Qiji sailing warship is the mast structure. The three main masts are pinned through with long axles, so the sails hang steady once they are attached — unlike some ship models where one light touch brings the whole rig down.

One trap to avoid: wrap the ropes in exactly the order the instructions give. Skipping ahead to save a few minutes throws every sail angle out of alignment, and the rework is painful.

On the steamship side, the stern propeller and rudder are working parts and turn smoothly, which younger builders will enjoy. The engine room interior, though, is fairly simple. This is a set where the outside beats the inside, and builders who want mechanical detail may feel short-changed.

## Finished Model: Genuinely Good in a Display Case

The strongest argument for the Qiji ship series is how well the finished models display. The sailing warship stands with clean lines, and on a base with an acrylic cover it makes a convincing desk piece. The steamship's portholes, funnels and life rings are reproduced well at this scale.

The drawbacks are real too. First, the finished models are large and need dedicated display space — measure the shelf before you commit. Second, small parts such as ropes and flagpoles come loose fairly often; put the spares from the bag aside after the build so you can replace losses later instead of hunting for a matching piece.

Weighed as a package, the parts quality, the clutch and the bag logic all justify the price, and value lands in the upper-middle range for ship model bricks.

## Who Should Buy It

If you like display-oriented builds and care most about how the finished model looks on a shelf, the Qiji sets are worth a try, the sailing warship line in particular. If what you want is complex interior structure or the very best brick feel, this line probably will not satisfy you.

My advice is to start with a small single-mast sailboat to test the water, then move up to the large warship if the style suits you. That way you avoid getting halfway through a big set before realising it is not to your taste.
