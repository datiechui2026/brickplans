# Crocodile MOC Guide: Build a Fierce (and Cute) Brick Reptile

Short version first: go for a medium 20–30 cm crocodile, use hinge bricks for the moving lower jaw, and stick to three greens with a dark back and a pale belly.

A crocodile is one of the most recognizable subjects in reptile MOC building, and it comes with built-in charm: the long snout, the teeth, the armored hide. You can push it toward a man-eating monster or a wide-eyed cartoon croc. Compared with four-legged mammals, the real challenge of a **crocodile MOC** is the long, low body — how a shape that sits close to the ground stays standing, how the mouth opens and closes, and how the scales get any depth at all. This guide walks through plan selection, structure and color, step by step.

## Choosing a Plan: Size First, Style Second

Before you pick up a brick, decide whether your crocodile MOC is realistic or cute. The two demand very different plans. A realistic build suits technic parts for a slim, elongated snout, exaggerated proportions and proper scale texture. A cute build wants a round head, a short snout and big eye stickers — fewer parts, faster to finish.

When you shop for a plan, check three things: does it include a parts list with part numbers, does it include step-by-step diagrams, and does it state the finished dimensions. Beginners should start at the 20–30 cm medium size — anything smaller and the details disappear, anything bigger and the part count doubles.

## Snout and Lower Jaw: Where the Fierceness Comes From

A crocodile's face is almost entirely mouth, and the snout takes up nearly half the body length. Build it with 1x2 slope bricks and curved bricks stacked slightly out of alignment, so the snout reads wide on top and narrow underneath. Leave a channel inside the mouth so the moving lower jaw has room to sit.

For a fierce look, insert white 1x1 tooth elements along the upper jaw edge, offset from each other, and tilt the teeth outward. For a cute look, enlarge the eyes, raise the forehead, use black round tiles as round eyes, and shorten the snout a little. Connect the lower jaw with 1x2 hinge bricks so it swings open naturally when it bites — this is the most appealing interactive feature of the whole build.

## Scales and Body: Layering the Skin

Crocodile skin is defined by a raised back and a flat belly. Run a row of 1x1 round studs or small slope bricks along the spine as scutes; on both sides, step 2x4 plates outward layer by layer and then draw them back in to create the arched back. Build the belly flat with tiles and leave a pale seam strip to suggest the belly color.

To make the skin convincing, interleave dark green, army green and yellow-green following the dark-back, pale-belly rule. Never use more than three greens on the same plane, or the model starts to look muddy. Narrow the rear of the body with cone parts so it transitions into the tail.

## Stance: Keeping a Low Body Stable

A body that hugs the ground is the main reason crocodile MOCs collapse into a pancake. The legs don't need to be tall — 2x2 bricks stacked 3–5 layers high is enough. Make the front legs slightly shorter and the rear legs slightly longer so the hips sit a little higher, and swap the feet for 2x3 plates to widen the contact area.

Center of gravity is the critical part. The heavier the snout, the further forward the front legs have to brace; if necessary, hide a counterweight brick inside the belly. Opening the jaw shifts weight forward, so add weight at the tip of the tail to keep front-to-back balance. Before you close up the shell, test every joint through two full rotations to confirm nothing is loose.

## FAQ

**Q: What size and style should a beginner pick for a crocodile MOC?**
A: Start at a medium 20–30 cm size — too small and the details can't be built, too big and the part count doubles. Decide realistic or cute first, because the two styles make very different demands on a plan.

**Q: How do you get the lower jaw to open and bite naturally?**
A: Connect the moving lower jaw with 1x2 hinge bricks and leave a channel inside the snout first. Then insert white 1x1 tooth elements along the upper jaw edge in an offset pattern, tilted outward, and the fierce look appears immediately.

**Q: How should you color the body and scales so it doesn't look muddy?**
A: Follow the dark-back, pale-belly rule and interleave similar greens — dark green, army green, yellow-green. Don't use more than three greens on one plane, and build the belly flat with tiles while leaving a pale seam strip.

**Q: The low body keeps collapsing and won't stand. What do I do?**
A: Raise the legs with 2x2 bricks stacked 3–5 layers high, make the front legs slightly shorter and rear legs slightly longer so the hips lift, and swap the feet for wider 2x3 plates. If the snout is heavy, brace the front legs further forward and add weight at the tail to balance.
