# Amusement Park MOCs Reviewed: Ferris Wheel, Coaster, Carousel

After more than a decade of building, amusement park MOCs remain my favourite subject. A Ferris wheel that actually turns, a coaster car that really rolls down the drop — the satisfaction of having that on your shelf is something a plain street scene just cannot deliver. This review breaks down the classic amusement park rides on three fronts: the moving mechanism, structural stability, and build difficulty.

## Ride-by-ride review

**Ferris wheel MOC.** This is the flagship of any amusement park layout. The core challenge is concentricity between the spokes and the main axle — if the axle sits even slightly off-centre, the wheel will speed up and slow down unevenly as it turns. My recommendation is to build the main shaft from a Technic cross axle plus a flange plate, then pair it with a worm gear drive to reduce the speed. That gives you a stable rotation with a bit of ceremony to it. On stability, the two A-frame supports must rise symmetrically, and the base should be thickened with plates to act as a counterweight. Overall difficulty is medium-high.

**Roller coaster MOC.** Widely regarded as the ride that scares builders off. The difficulty lies entirely in the continuity of the track curvature and the gradient of the drop. Where a straight track section meets a curved one, any sudden change in curvature will either jam the car or throw it off the rails. My habit is to lay out the track route on paper first, then build it in segments using track pieces of the same colour and test-run each segment as I go. As long as you have enough fixing points and even spacing between the support columns, the finished ride will run smoothly. Highest difficulty of the group — save it until you have some experience.

**Carousel MOC.** It looks gentle, but the mechanism is not simple. The cantilever supports under the umbrella canopy plus the rotating structure around the central column mean every rod has to sit on the same plane once it starts moving. For stability, the base needs to be two to three studs wider than the upper level, otherwise it tips easily. Difficulty is moderate, which makes it a great first amusement park MOC.

**Bumper car MOC.** This one is friendlier. Steering is handled mainly with gears and linkages, and it works well either as a single display car or as a whole arena scene. There is no precision transmission to worry about — it is more a test of colour scheme and shaping, which makes it a good practice project for newcomers.

## Planning the whole park

Building one ride is easy; building a full park is where the fun is. I suggest laying a base out of plates first and arranging everything along a circulation route: put the Ferris wheel on the outer edge as the visual centre, run the roller coaster track along the perimeter of the layout, and place the carousel and bumper cars in the middle as the busy, popular zone. Then dress it with fences, street lamps, a ticket booth and palm trees. Finish with an outer wall around the whole thing and the sense of scale changes immediately.

## Parts and budget tips

With large MOCs like these, the thing that drains you is not the design — it is the parts. Large plates in dark grey and white, transparent pieces, cross axles and gears are all worth stockpiling early, and mixing Technic parts with standard bricks can save a fair amount of money. Also, do not buy every part up front. Buy the core transmission parts first to prove the mechanism turns and runs, then fill in the cosmetic pieces afterwards — that avoids a lot of waste. Overall, amusement park MOCs follow a very rewarding path: start with bumper cars, move up to the carousel, and finish with the Ferris wheel and roller coaster. I hope this review saves you a few wrong turns on the way.
