# MOC Color Schemes: The 60/30/10 Rule and How to Pick Colors

Here is the short version: plan your MOC colors in five steps — pick a main color, reserve a secondary, add an accent, check part availability, then build texture. The 60/30/10 rule does most of the work.

Plenty of builders end up with a model that is packed with parts but still looks messy and cheap, while a much sparser build reads as refined at first glance. The difference is rarely the shaping — it is the color scheme. This guide walks you from random color picking to a repeatable system.

## 1. Start With the 60/30/10 Rule

The 60/30/10 rule is a classic of interior design, and it transfers straight to brick building: **60% main color** sets the overall tone, **30% secondary color** handles the layering and transitions, and **10% accent color** grabs the eye. Build a city street car, for example, with a white body at 60%, dark gray for the windows and undercarriage at 30%, and 10% red for the taillights and a stripe. Clean, crisp and easy on the eyes. Whatever you do, avoid splitting the model into three equal thirds — that just kills the visual center of gravity.

## 2. Use the Color Wheel — Complementary Pairs Photograph Best

Two colors sitting opposite each other on the color wheel are complementary: red and green, blue and orange, yellow and purple. Placed side by side they create strong visual contrast, and on a MOC that translates into great photos. For a fire service theme, a red body with a small amount of yellow or white accent has far more tension than an all-red build. For a sci-fi spaceship, a dark blue hull with orange engine glow is a textbook complementary pairing. If you are worried about getting it wrong, mock it up in the Bricklink Studio palette and iterate until you are happy before ordering parts.

## 3. Check Part Colors Before You Commit to a Scheme

A scheme that only exists on paper often cannot be built — plenty of colors are only produced in a narrow set of parts. Flesh, for instance, barely exists in technic pieces, and transparent parts come in only a handful of colors. So the habit worth building is this: search colors by part on Bricklink or Rebrickable first, confirm which color codes are actually available for the parts you rely on, and only then lock in the scheme. The first principle of MOC color planning is that a scheme has to sit on top of real part inventory — otherwise even the prettiest palette is just talk.

## 4. Neutral Base Plus One Main Color Never Fails

If you do not want to take risks, remember this universal formula: black, white, gray and tan as the base, plus one highly saturated main color. Military vehicles work with dark gray and olive green as the base, accented with a little sand or black — grounded and forgiving of dirt. Mecha builds work with a black skeleton and bright yellow panels and joints, and the mechanical feel jumps immediately. Neutrals hold the model down; the saturated color lifts it. Put the two together and it is very hard to go wrong.

## 5. Build Texture With Color Variation, Not Stickers

The best MOCs tend to read as a single flat color from a distance and reveal detail everywhere up close — that is the trick of color-difference texture. On a brick wall, alternate dark brown, light brown and sand bricks and it will have far more character than a flat single-color surface. In a forest, mix dark green, light green and yellow-green and the canopy instantly gains volume. Botanical themes benefit from a green gradient with white flowers for a fresh, lively look. None of this needs special parts — ordinary bricks will do it, which makes it the best value advanced technique around.

## 6. Pitfalls: Do Not Let a Build Look "Dirty"

Finally, the ways color schemes go wrong. First, mixing too many similar-but-different grays — light gray, medium gray and dark gray all on the same wall reads as faded from a distance. Second, slamming large areas of saturated color against each other, half red against half blue, or half yellow against half green. Third, forgetting breathing room, trying to put color into every single zone. Restraint is where the premium feel comes from. For more reference, search the BrickPlan community for military, sci-fi and botanical instructions and see how other builders handle their palettes.

The core of this whole approach fits in one line: set the main color, reserve the secondary, use an accent, check your parts, build texture. Think the scheme through before your next build starts and the perceived quality of your model will jump a level immediately.

## FAQ

**Q: My builds always look messy — is there a simple ratio I can follow?**
A: Use 60/30/10: 60% main color to set the tone, 30% secondary for layering, 10% accent to catch the eye. Never let three colors take a third each.

**Q: How do I choose MOC colors so the build does not fall flat?**
A: Check part colors before finalizing the scheme — Flesh barely exists in technic parts, and transparent parts come in few colors. A neutral base plus one saturated main color almost never fails.

**Q: Which themes suit complementary colors?**
A: Complementary pairs photograph best: a red fire theme with small yellow accents, or a sci-fi ship with a dark blue hull and orange engine glow. Maximum impact, very photogenic.

**Q: How do I make a single-color MOC look more detailed?**
A: Use color difference for texture: alternate dark brown, light brown and sand on brick walls, mix dark, light and yellow-green in forests. Regular bricks are enough — a high-value advanced technique.
