# Hidden Screw Techniques for Cleaner LEGO MOC Surfaces

One clarification before we start: bricks hold together through studs and tubes, not real screws. What builders call a "hidden screw" is really a hidden fixing point — a connection buried inside the structure so no joint shows on the outside. Instead of leaving reinforcement visible, you swap in pins, axles and sideways connectors, and the surface stays clean. This guide covers the trade-off between strength and appearance in hidden fixing, and which alternative method suits which job.

## 1. Why Hide the Fixing Points at All

Every MOC hits the same conflict in its later stages: a solid structure needs connectors, but the more connectors you add, the more pin holes and studs end up on the surface, and the model suddenly looks like a bare construction site. That matters most on vehicle bodies, mech armour and building facades — nobody wants a row of fixing points visible from the side. So hiding is not about chasing maximum strength. It is about moving the reinforcement into layers you cannot see: internal pins, axles underneath, plates in the sandwich layer, while the outer skin remains a smooth, unbroken surface.

## 2. Pins and Axles Instead of Exposed Fasteners

The workhorse parts for hidden fixing are Technic elements. A 1x2 brick with a pin hole (part 32064) can sit inside a wall, with a pin pushed through horizontally so two bricks lock together from within. A 1x1 piece with an axle hole (part 2450) paired with a Technic axle lets you thread a structure together from one direction. The axle diameter is fixed, so it will not wobble once inserted — ideal for a chassis rail on a vehicle body. The trick is orientation: point every pin and axle inward, downward, or toward whichever face is never displayed, and the outside shows no trace of the fixing at all.

## 3. Side Connectors: Pulling Tight From Inside

When you want to pull two faces tight without damaging the outer surface, side connectors are the first choice. Common options include 1x2 connectors with a side pin hole and 2x3 plates with side holes; they move the fixing point to the side so the front stays completely flat. On mech lower legs or arms, using a side connector to bite the two shell plates together from inside the joint is far stronger than drilling a pin hole in the front face — and the exterior stays clean. Choose parts with a tight fit, or the whole thing will pop apart the first time you flex it and the reinforcement was wasted.

## 4. SNOT and Brick Interlocking: Let the Structure Hide the Fixing

Beyond pins and axles, interlocking lets you conceal fixing points. SNOT (studs not on top) building turns the brick's mating face inward, then you close the outside with tiles so the whole thing reads as one solid block. Clip plates are handy too, such as the 1x2 plate with clip (part 60470): two plates clip onto a plate sandwiched between them, and no load point is visible from outside. On a building facade, put the clip plates inside the wall cavity, then close it with 1x2 tiles (3069) so everything sits flush — the seams line up and no joint shows anywhere.

## 5. Removable Sections vs. Paneled Shells

Hidden fixing does not mean sealing everything shut. For parts that need to open (engine covers, hatches, swappable weapons), build them removable: leave a pin socket at the base and pull the whole module off, which is far easier than prizing off tiles. For display-only areas (roofs, walls, turret armour), panel them in and wrap every fixing point inside. The test is a single question: will this spot be handled often? If yes, use a pin-based quick release; if not, seal it. That way you hide the fixing points without sacrificing playability.

## 6. From Instructions to Practice

Hidden fixing comes down to three steps: decide which face must stay clean, pick pins, axles or side connectors to bury inside, then close the surface with tiles. Disassemble and rebuild a few times, try a few different parts, and the feel comes quickly.
