# Brick Thatched Hut MOC: 480-Piece Cottage Build Guide

The short version: this countryside thatched hut MOC uses about 480 pieces, stands 11 cm tall and takes roughly 1.5 hours to build, which puts it at medium-easy difficulty. The standout features are the sloped thatch texture and the exposed timber frame. The instructions are published on brickplan.cn, so you can follow along and reproduce that warm rural cottage feel.

**Step 1: Baseplate and foundation**

Start with a 16x16 baseplate. Run a ring of dark grey bricks around the perimeter as the foundation, about two brick layers high. Don't forget to leave a removable floor section in one corner so you can drop in minifigures and furniture later. This step uses roughly 60 pieces.

**Step 2: Walls and window/door frames**

The walls go up in cream (off-white) bricks laid in a staggered running bond. Leave a door opening on the front face, 4 studs wide and 5 bricks high, plus two symmetrical window openings. Span a beam above the door and windows using a 2x8 brown plate across the openings — it carries the load and sets up the timber theme early. Close off the walls at layer 8, ready to receive the roof.

**Step 3: The timber frame**

This is the skeleton of the whole hut. Use long brown elements on top of the walls to build a gable (inverted-V) main beam, then add three diagonal braces on each side to form a stable triangular structure. Connect the main beams with short cross bars spaced about every 3 studs, so the roof has something to attach the thatch to. Dry-fit the timber first before fixing anything down, and check repeatedly to keep it symmetrical left to right.

**Step 4: Sloped thatch texture**

Start at the lowest point of the eaves and lay small elements in dark brown and dark tan (1x1 slope bricks work well), alternating them and building upward one overlapping row at a time, the way roof tiles are laid, to create that layered look. Press each row down firmly with your fingers as you finish it so the texture stays neat. The roof takes 12 rows in total, and the very top is capped with a curved element — the silhouette of the thatched hut appears immediately.

**Step 5: Details and finishing touches**

Add two steps at the doorway, a flower pot under the window, a string of peppers or corn hanging from the eaves, and a short wooden fence behind the hut. Finally, check that every brick is pressed down properly, put the minifigure inside, and the countryside thatched hut is finished.

The whole model uses only standard basic parts — no rare or special pieces — so it is easy to take apart and rebuild. If you want a more realistic look, swap the dark tan elements for a mix of dark brown and the texture layers will read even richer.

## FAQ

**Q: How do you build the roof of a thatched hut MOC?**

A: Lay dark brown and dark tan 1x1 slope bricks row by row from the eaves up to the ridge, overlapping each row to create the layered texture. About 12 rows are enough, and the top is finished with a curved element. No special parts are needed at any point.

**Q: Is this thatched hut MOC hard to build?**

A: The difficulty is medium-easy: about 480 pieces and 1.5 hours to complete. The only real challenge is keeping the roof texture even. Build the timber frame before laying the thatch, and follow the brickplan.cn instructions in order and you won't get lost.

**Q: How many pieces does it need?**

A: About 480 standard basic pieces, mostly cream, dark brown and dark tan, with no rare parts. Most builders will already have enough in their collection, and if you're short, brickplan.cn can generate a missing-parts list in one click.

**Q: What does the timber frame do?**

A: The gable main beam plus diagonal braces form a stable triangular structure that acts as the load-bearing skeleton of the roof. It keeps the roof from sagging while you lay the thatch, and it gives the interior that raw rural-architecture character.

**Q: How big is the finished model?**

A: The finished hut is about 11 cm tall with a 16x16-stud footprint, taking up roughly 13x13 cm. It sits nicely on a desk or display shelf, and it can be combined with other countryside buildings in the same series to make a village scene.
