# Brick Library MOC Guide: Blueprint to Finished Rooftop

In a modular street, the cafe stands for leisure and the theatre for noise and crowds — the library stands for something quieter and more formal. A good **brick library MOC blueprint** calms the whole street down: arched windows, wall-to-wall bookcases, a double-height reading hall. You can picture it before you place a single brick. This guide walks you from choosing the blueprint to closing the roof, so every classic library element reads the way it should.

## Why the library anchors a modular street

The library plays the role of cultural landmark, and it carries real visual weight. Its mass is usually square and solid, its facade formal, and it holds a street corner or the middle of a block equally well. Compared with flashy shops, a library demands more restraint in colour: off-white, brick red and dark brown dominate, and the result ages better. Difficulty sits at medium-to-high — you practise basic wall building and interior detail work at the same time, which makes it an excellent subject for builders moving past the beginner stage.

## Choosing the right brick library MOC blueprint

The blueprint decides the outcome. Check three things first: whether the scale matches your minifigures, whether the footprint fits your baseplate, and whether the parts list is easy to fill. Common sources include overseas instruction sites, domestic communities, and Chinese-language sharing communities such as BrickPlan (brickplan.cn), which is especially friendly to local builders — blueprints are sorted by difficulty and size, and often come with step notes in Chinese. Before you order parts, highlight the special pieces the blueprint calls for (arched windows, column capitals, dome elements) and confirm availability first.

## The reading hall: the heart of the library

The reading hall is usually the most striking space in the whole MOC, and the key is height. Open up two or three floors with an atrium, run galleries along both sides, and put skylights in the ceiling so light falls from above — the sense of scale arrives instantly. In the centre, place a long reading table with small lamps and open-book elements; against the wall, add a lending counter with a potted plant in front of it and the scene comes alive. Leave standing room for minifigures so visitors can actually walk into the hall.

## Bookshelves and collection details

Bookshelves are where a library needs the most piled-up detail. Two approaches work well. First, build full-wall shelving from standard bricks turned on their side, then scatter book spines in different colours using 1x1 plates — the more mixed the colours, the more it reads as a real collection. Second, build a few freestanding shelf units that can be repositioned. Cap the shelving with a dark beam along the top and add a baseboard at the bottom, and the layers separate immediately. For extra life, tuck a ladder or a book cart into one shelf level — visitors tend to smile when they spot it.

## Facade columns and exterior walls

A library facade is about symmetry and order. The classic solution is a three-part arrangement: a ground floor of continuous arched windows, a middle level of tall floor-to-ceiling windows in a row, and a top level closed off with short windows or solid wall. Use fluted column pieces paired on either side of the entrance, topped with a simplified capital, and you get the classical library look. Keep the palette light overall with dark edging, and emphasise window frames in dark brown or deep green for a composed, dignified feel.

## Roof and daylight design

The roof is easy to overlook and adds a lot. A flat roof is simplest: run a ring of sawtooth pieces inside the parapet to fake a tile texture. More advanced builders can try a gabled roof, stacking slope bricks into a pitch and setting transparent bricks along the ridge as skylights. Daylight matters just as much — add plenty of windows on the upper-floor walls so light crosses the shelves and creates contrast indoors, which photographs beautifully. Don't forget one or two chimneys and a small skylight turret on the roof; the silhouette improves a lot.

## Common problems and next steps

Beginners hit three typical traps. First, shelves built too deep eat the hall's floor space — keep shelf depth to two or three bricks. Second, facades that stay flat with no projection or setback; break the monotony with protruding sills and overhanging canopies. Third, focusing on the outside and ignoring the interior — decide the internal functional zones before you design the outer walls. To go further, add an underground book store or a back-courtyard reading corner.
