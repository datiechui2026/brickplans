# Brick Kangaroo MOC Review: Legs, Pouch and Part List

Verdict first: a brick kangaroo lives or dies on three things — whether the front-to-back leg volume ratio lands near 1:2:2:1.5, whether the hind leg joints can actually be posed, and whether the pouch is built with SNOT so the opening reads as a genuine recessed pocket rather than a flat patch.

Kangaroos are one of the most instantly readable animal MOC subjects: heavy hind legs, a thick tail, a pouch across the belly. Almost anyone recognizes the silhouette at a glance. Building one accurately is another matter. A kangaroo's proportions have nothing in common with the four-legged animals most builders practice on — the mass sits at the rear, and standing depends entirely on the hind legs and tail. This review breaks the plan down from structure to details to parts.

## Accuracy: Get the Proportions Right and the Character Follows

The first test of any kangaroo MOC plan is proportion. A real kangaroo has short, small forelimbs and long, thick hind legs; standing, the body tips forward and the tail touches the ground to form a tripod. A good plan pulls off that mass difference deliberately, through the brick counts in the part list: the hind leg gets 2x4 bricks plus slope pieces piled into thigh muscle, while the front leg is made from 1x2 bricks and a joint piece and stops there. When you review a plan, put the finished photos next to a real silhouette — if the head, torso, hind leg and tail lengths sit close to 1:2:2:1.5, the accuracy is there.

## Strong Hind Legs: A Mechanics Lesson in Technic and Axles

A kangaroo MOC's soul is in the hind legs. Most high-scoring plans bring in Technic: beams with holes and axles form the thigh frame, axle bushes and connectors lock the knee angle, and curved and slope pieces wrap the outside into a muscle outline. The payoff is adjustable joints — the legs can be set into a standing pose, a pre-jump crouch, or a landing absorb. For the foot, widen it with a 2x3 plate and lift the heel slightly so only the toes and front pad touch the ground; that is the key to the crouched kangaroo stance. During the build, assemble one hind leg first and keep adjusting the angle until the center of gravity sits over the middle of the foot, then mirror it to the other side. Skip that step and the finished model leans.

## The Pouch: SNOT Details Show the Craft

The pouch is the easiest part of a kangaroo MOC to blur into a shapeless lump, and it is the real test of how fine a plan is. The usual approach is SNOT (Studs Not On Top): mount 2x4 plates sideways to enclose a recessed pocket opening, then trace the upper rim curve with curved pieces. Thorough plans go further and leave a 1x2 recess inside the pouch for a small joey built from smooth plates and tiles — instant parent-and-child appeal. Keep the pouch opening low on the belly. Set it too high and it reads, from the side, like the kangaroo is wearing a backpack.

## Part List and Build Difficulty

The part list here is clearly labeled, and odd pieces such as the head contour element come with workable substitutes, so spare-part pressure is low. Overall difficulty is medium-to-high: basic assembly accounts for only about 60 percent, while tuning the Technic angles in the hind legs and building the SNOT pouch both take experience. It suits builders with 3-5 sets' worth of practice behind them. Beginners who want the challenge shouldn't be put off — the brickplan kangaroo MOC community shares build notes and color-swap schemes, and working straight from the list is enough to finish.

## Bottom Line

Taken as a whole, this kangaroo plan is solid on proportional accuracy, hind-leg structure and pouch detail, and its use of Technic and SNOT is well judged. The finished model works both as a static display piece and as something to pose and handle. If you want a project that is a real challenge and still delivers a strong result, this one is worth building.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose a brick kangaroo MOC plan?**
A: Check proportions first. Compare the finished photos against a real silhouette — if the head, torso, hind leg and tail lengths come close to 1:2:2:1.5, it passes. The hind legs should build mass from 2x4 bricks and slope pieces, while the front legs use 1x2 bricks and stop there.

**Q: Why do kangaroo hind legs need Technic pieces?**
A: For adjustable joints. Beams with holes plus axles form the thigh frame, and axle bushes plus connectors lock the knee angle, which allows three poses: standing, pre-jump crouch and landing absorb. Pure brick stacking cannot do this.

**Q: How do I keep the pouch from looking mushy?**
A: Mount 2x4 plates sideways (SNOT) to enclose a recessed opening, then use curved pieces to trace the upper rim. Keep the opening low on the belly — set too high, it looks like a backpack from the side.

**Q: What are the difficulty and part count like?**
A: Difficulty is medium-to-high. Basic assembly is roughly 60 percent; the Technic hind-leg angles and the pouch structure need experience. It is aimed at builders with 3-5 sets' worth of practice, and the parts are mostly basic bricks and Technic elements.
