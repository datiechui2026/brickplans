# LEGO Airport Build Guide: Terminal, Runway and Apron

An airport is one of the most impressive subjects you can take on in a city brick scene: a tall terminal, jet bridges reaching out to parked aircraft, a straight runway, an apron full of airliners and a control tower to top it off. Put those pieces together and you have a complete aviation diorama. This tutorial walks through an airport build step by step, from baseplate layout to the final details, so you can put together a small airport that makes people stop and look.

## Scene Planning and Baseplate Layout

The worst mistake with an airport is building wherever the mood takes you. Fix the baseplate size first. A small regional airport works fine on a 48x32 baseplate; if you want a full-length runway, join two baseplates together. For layout, run the runway along one long edge, place the terminal slightly forward of centre, keep the apron tight against the terminal, and set the tower beside the middle of the runway. That gives you a clear focal point and a scene that photographs well. Lay down light grey plates for the runway and taxiway first, then use green or tan elements for the grass and landside area, so each zone reads at a glance. Planning up front decides how the whole thing looks, so don't rush into stacking buildings.

## Terminal Building Main Structure

Build the terminal over two levels: a glass departure hall on the ground floor and a narrower waiting area above. Leave large areas of transparent windows in the facade, and add a small roof overhang or a wave-shaped profile to give it that modern terminal feel. The interior doesn't need to be complicated; a few check-in counters and seats are enough to bring the scene to life. Reinforce the inside of the building with horizontal connecting elements. Terminal buildings span a long distance, and having one come apart halfway through the build is the most common way these projects go wrong.

## Jet Bridges and Boarding Corridors

The jet bridge is the key part linking terminal to aircraft. Build it as a covered walkway with Technic parts as support legs, with one end meeting the side of the terminal's upper floor. For extra life, use hinge pieces to create a retractable folding bellows section. Work out the jet bridge height in advance so it lines up with the cabin door of the airliner on the apron. Too high or too low and the whole aircraft looks wrong.

## Runway and Taxiway

Build the runway as a wide, long strip of light grey bricks, with runway end markings at both ends and a centre line down the middle. Use dark grey for the taxiway, branching off towards the apron and one stud narrower than the runway. Pick out the marking bands with small yellow or white bricks, or use 1x1 tiles as surface lights. It costs very little and noticeably improves the realism. Add a dark border strip along the runway edge so the grey doesn't sit hard against the green grass.

## Apron and Control Tower

The apron only needs to hold one or two small airliners. Surface it with medium grey bricks, then add a few yellow guide lines and stand numbers and park an airliner, baggage carts and a fuel truck on it, and the scene instantly looks busy. Put the tower to the side of the runway, built as a slim, tall cylinder with large transparent pieces forming the glazed observation windows at the top. That window band is the tower's most recognisable feature, so it has to be transparent. Keep the tower slightly taller than the terminal and you get that sense of it looking down over the whole scene.

## Vehicles and Detail Dressing

Park a baggage conveyor vehicle and a fuel truck on the apron, bring a shuttle bus in on the landside, and the small scene immediately feels operational. Add a row of greenery and street lights in front of the terminal, a windsock at the end of the runway, and a captain, cabin crew and a queue of boarding passengers, and a day in the life of an airport is complete. These small additions take up little space and cost little, yet they are the part visitors stop and look at longest.

## Common Pitfalls

First, not planning the baseplate properly: you build the terminal and only then discover the runway won't fit, and fixing it is expensive. Second, the jet bridge doesn't match the height of the airliner's cabin door. Third, the apron is too small and the aircraft's wingspan overhangs the edge. Fourth, the tower is too short and loses that overlooking presence. Fifth, forgetting the guide lines, which drains much of the airport feel. Avoid these and your airport build will comfortably clear the bar.
