# High-Speed Rail MOC Instructions: Where to Find Good Ones

Short answer first: with high-speed rail MOCs, picking the right instructions matters more than stockpiling parts. For beginners, a Hexie (CRH) design at 10-wide is the safest starting point — and before you buy anything, verify the image resolution and the parts list.

## Why the Instructions Matter More Than the Parts

Most builders' first instinct when they get into brick high-speed trains is to hoard parts. The more important step is choosing the right high-speed rail MOC instructions. Take the same Fuxing train: instructions from different designers vary enormously in structure, scale and fidelity. A good set of instructions makes the build flow; a bad one leaves you short of parts or with a floppy frame halfway through, and the only fix is a full teardown. Spending time studying the instructions before you start is far better value than blindly buying bricks.

## The Three Main Train Types: Fuxing, Hexie and Maglev

Most high-speed rail MOC instructions on the market fall into three vehicle families:

- **Fuxing**: the streamlined nose is complex and demands heavy use of curved slopes and wedge bricks. Strong Fuxing designs put real effort into sub-dividing the nose so the streamlined silhouette comes from the bricks themselves rather than from stickers — a good fit for builders chasing accuracy.
- **Hexie**: the classic model, with the largest number of instructions and the highest maturity. Its structural solutions have been validated by a large number of builders, which makes it the best entry point for beginners. Hexie instructions also have better parts commonality — many parts can be substituted with third-party equivalents, keeping cost under control.
- **Maglev**: fewer instructions but instantly recognizable, with a low, flat body that usually sits on a "floating" display base. Maglev designs lean heavily on special parts, so the parts cost runs higher; they suit advanced builders who want a display piece.

Decide your goal before you pick: play, shelf display, or a community competition. That choice determines both the vehicle and the instructions.

## Comparing Where to Get Instructions

The main channels for high-speed rail MOC instructions are:

1. **Rebrickable**: the largest MOC instruction platform worldwide, with filtering by part count and difficulty. Quality varies widely, so check the designer's track record with previous builds, and note that some of the better instructions are paid.
2. **Chinese brick communities**: the Gobricks forum, Tieba and Bilibili host a large pool of Chinese designers whose style is closer to the Chinese parts ecosystem. Many sets of instructions come with build videos, which is very beginner-friendly.
3. **brickplan**: a site focused on collecting high-resolution brick instructions, with high-speed rail and EMU themes organized and categorized plus parts lists attached, saving you the trouble of hunting around.

Cross-check the same vehicle across several channels and look at photos of finished real builds before deciding — never judge from renders alone.

## Checking Resolution and the Parts List

Once you have the instructions in hand, do two things before you build:

- **Check the resolution**: skip low-resolution instructions with blurry step images, especially around the nose curves, where fuzzy diagrams lead straight to mistakes. Favour PDF files with step images you can zoom into.
- **Check the parts list**: go through colour and quantity item by item, paying particular attention to rare and special parts. High-speed rail MOCs use large numbers of 1xN plates, curved slopes and windscreen parts, so confirm first that you own them or can source them — otherwise the build stalls halfway.

## 8-Wide, 10-Wide or 12-Wide: Choosing a Scale

High-speed rail MOC instructions commonly come in three body widths:

- **8-wide**: harmonizes with city street buildings, takes up little space, works with standard track layouts, and has the lowest parts cost.
- **10-wide**: the balance point between fidelity and size, with noticeably better body detail. This is the mainstream choice for most builders today.
- **12-wide**: for maximum accuracy — cab, doors and bogies can all be fully rendered — but part counts are large, cost is high, and it needs display space.

Beginners should start at 10-wide and move up to premium 12-wide instructions once they are comfortable.

## Final Notes

Choosing the right high-speed rail MOC instructions is half the battle. Whether it is the streamlined nose of a Fuxing, the proven structure of a Hexie, or the floating base of a maglev, work in this order: compare vehicle types, pick a channel, verify the parts list, then settle the scale. That sequence saves a lot of dead ends.
