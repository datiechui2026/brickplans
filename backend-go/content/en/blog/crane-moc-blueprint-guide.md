# Brick Crane MOC Blueprints: Structure, Parts and Build Order

Among construction-vehicle subjects, the crane is the one that looks easy and builds hardest. Plenty of builders assume a long beam chassis plus an upright boom is the whole job — then they turn the winch, and the machine goes straight over. A blueprint worth building from is really a working recreation of real lifting machinery. What follows covers the design pain points first, then how to filter blueprints and prepare parts so you end up with a crane that lifts, slews and stays upright.

## The Four Hard Parts of a Crane MOC

**Boom extension** is the first hurdle. Real cranes telescope their boom sections with hydraulic cylinders; in bricks you either use Technic pneumatic pistons or a sliding rail-and-block arrangement. A good blueprint includes locking pins — without them the boom sags the moment it extends and the mechanical feel disappears.

**Winch and rope** is where the details show. How the pulley block is threaded, how the rope winds onto the drum, and what cord you use (fishing line, cotton string or a dedicated rope) all change how the lift feels. The classic beginner mistake is winding rope directly onto an axle: it slips the moment a load goes on.

**Counterweight balance** decides whether the model works at all. With the boom out front, the number and position of the counterweight blocks behind it have to be worked out, or the model nods at best and tips over at worst. Good blueprints make the counterweight a removable module so you can add or remove bricks while tuning.

**Chassis type** needs deciding up front. A tracked chassis has strong off-road ability and a stable stance, which suits a rough crawler crane; a wheeled chassis with steering is more flexible to play with but needs outriggers planted before a lift. Both have their fans — just don't make the call after the model is finished.

## Five Things to Check When Choosing a Blueprint

With so many crane blueprints around, filter them on these five points:

1. Look at photos of the finished model and confirm the boom genuinely telescopes and the winch genuinely turns — not a static display piece.
2. Check that the parts list gives part numbers and quantities, so you can compare against your stock and order what's missing.
3. Favour blueprints with step-by-step colour renders or 3D views. Black-and-white line drawings are very easy to misread.
4. Confirm the scale. A desk-sized crane and a large-scale engineering vehicle differ enormously in part count.
5. See whether the author explains gear orientation and the rope routing order. These two spots are where builds fail most often.

## Parts Prep and Build Order

Once you've picked a blueprint, gather the parts. Technic elements do the real work: gears, differentials, a turntable bearing (slewing ring), pneumatic parts and universal joints are all essential, alongside plenty of beams, pins and axles. Sorting everything into boxes by colour before you start is strongly recommended, and check the parts list so you neither over-order nor run short — discovering you're a few critical gears short halfway through is miserable.

For build order, follow this route closely: chassis, slewing mechanism, upper structure, boom and winch, counterweight, detailing. Build the chassis first and test that it drives, then fit the slewing ring — this is the easiest place to go crooked, so make sure the teeth mesh and line up. Next come the cab and tower body, and last the boom and rope routing. Test each module by hand as soon as it's finished and rework any binding immediately, rather than saving all the debugging for the end, when tracking down a problem costs far more. When the model is complete, check where the centre of gravity sits and adjust counterweight blocks if needed, so the crane stands steady even with nothing on the hook.

A crane you built yourself can lift small blocks in the day and sit on the desk at night — an satisfaction that a retail set rarely delivers.
