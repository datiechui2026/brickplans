# How to Photograph LEGO MOCs: Gear, Lighting and Composition

Every builder knows the feeling: the model is sharp, detailed and full of clever part usage, but the photos come out grey, crooked and nowhere near the effort you put in. After years of building, I have shot MOCs with a phone straight out of the camera, with a mirrorless setup on a tabletop, and with nothing but a desk lamp pressed into service. This guide covers gear, lighting, backgrounds and props, composition, and post-processing, so your build looks as good on screen as it does on the shelf.

## Gear: Camera or Phone, Don't Get Hung Up on Specs

Short answer first: a current flagship phone is genuinely enough, and there is no need to buy a camera just for shooting MOCs. A phone is light, always with you, and its macro mode is excellent for close-ups of small parts and tight details. That said, if you care about image quality and often shoot large dioramas, a mirrorless body with a flip-out screen is far more comfortable to work with. Manual focus, RAW files and the dynamic range of a larger sensor are things a phone still cannot match.

For lenses, an equivalent 35mm to 50mm prime is the sweet spot: minimal distortion and natural perspective. When shooting a full scene, step back rather than pushing a wide-angle lens right up against the model, or the edges of the parts will stretch and warp. A tripod is non-negotiable. Don't skip it. Whether you shoot on a phone or a camera, a stable position beats cranking up the ISO, and it also makes multi-frame stacking much easier later.

## Lighting: Softboxes and Window Light

Bricks are plastic, and plastic hates harsh specular highlights and hard-edged shadows. My rule of thumb: a softbox with a reflective umbrella, one light on each side, angled in at about 45 degrees, and you will rarely go wrong. No proper lights? Overcast daylight or diffused light from a window is the most natural softbox you can get. Move the table next to the window and let the light fall evenly across the model from the side. The results are often better than a studio setup.

The key principle is one key light and one fill light. Use a reflector board to lift the shadows, and never blast light straight down from above. Top lighting blows out the top faces of the parts and destroys all the detail. If you can, place a sheet of white card stock opposite the shadow side, and the shadows immediately soften.

## Backgrounds, Props and Composition

Keep the background as simple as possible. A sheet of solid-colour paper, an acrylic panel, or a LEGO baseplate laid out as a ground plane all work, and all let the model be the undisputed subject. Props are for telling a story: minifigures, vehicles and plant parts can play supporting roles, but they should never steal the scene.

For composition, follow two rules: shoot the subject at eye level, and leave some breathing room in the frame. Move the camera around and shoot several positions: straight-on, a 45-degree high angle, and a set of detail close-ups, then pick whichever feels best. You can also use a low angle looking up to give the build a sense of scale, or a shallow depth of field with a tilt-shift feel to isolate the key parts.

## Post-Processing and Common Mistakes to Avoid

Don't over-edit. Correct the white balance first, then lift the exposure slightly and pull the highlights down a little. Keep colours as close to the real model as possible and avoid over-saturating. The most common failures look like this: focus locked on the background instead of the model, motion blur from hand-holding, shadows that are black and hard, desktop clutter sneaking into the frame, and fingerprints or dust on the parts that nobody wiped off. When you edit, use cropping to recompose the shot; it rescues plenty of otherwise unusable frames.

In the end, there is no single correct answer to photographing a LEGO MOC. Shoot more, experiment, and you will find your own style. When you are done, don't forget to upload your best shots to BrickPlans (https://brickplan.cn) and share your build and your notes with other builders. The community never lacks an audience; what it wants is every good photo taken with care.
