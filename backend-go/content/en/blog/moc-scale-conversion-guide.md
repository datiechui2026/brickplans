# LEGO MOC Scale Conversion: Minifig to Building Ratio Guide

![MOC scale conversion model example](https://brickplan2-1317217681.cos.ap-hongkong.myqcloud.com/blog/moc-scale-conversion-guide/moc-scale-conversion-guide-1.jpg)

Plenty of builders download a set of MOC instructions and stall at step one: the scale. **LEGO MOC scale conversion** is less mysterious than it looks, though. Once you have the minifigure as an anchor point, you can estimate the real-world size of almost any building or vehicle on the page. This guide walks through the numbers so you can read a plan with confidence instead of guessing.

## Start with the minifigure as your ruler

![MOC building steps](https://brickplan2-1317217681.cos.ap-hongkong.myqcloud.com/blog/moc-scale-conversion-guide/moc-scale-conversion-guide-2.jpg)

A standard LEGO minifigure stands about 4 cm tall. Mapped onto a real person of roughly 1.8 m, that puts minifigure scale at about **1:48**. The useful consequence: if a minifigure standing next to a building in the plan reaches a ceiling that is 3 bricks up, the real floor-to-ceiling height works out to about 2.8 to 3 m, which is exactly the range you would expect in an ordinary home. That single check tells you whether a plan is meant to be a usable building or a compressed diorama.

The fastest way to identify the scale of any plan is to look for a minifigure used as a reference object. If one is standing in the render or the step photos, measure it against the doors, the floor heights and the vehicle beside it. A door that comes up to a minifigure's shoulder suggests a real-world door of about 2 m, so the plan is sitting close to 1:48. No minifigure anywhere? Then look at the stud width of the walls and work backwards from the vehicle or figure sizes below.

Keep in mind that the 1:48 figure is a working approximation, not a precise measurement. Minifigures are stylised, so their proportions do not scale perfectly to a human body. What matters is that you apply the same approximation consistently across a whole build.

## Vehicles and buildings use different scales

Vehicle MOCs commonly come in two scales: **1:32** (roughly 6 studs wide) and **1:64** (roughly 3 studs wide). A 1:32 build is the choice for a detailed sports car, because there is room for fine shaping, interior detail and working doors. A 1:64 car is the choice for set dressing — a filler vehicle parked in a street scene where the architecture is the star.

Building plans, by contrast, mostly key off the minifigure standard of 1:48. This is why a 1:32 car and a 1:48 building rarely sit well together on the same baseplate: the car looks oversized next to the architecture, even if both models are excellent on their own.

For quick cross-checks in either direction, use this conversion: real length (m) = brick count × 0.8 cm × scale denominator ÷ 100. The 0.8 cm is the width of a single stud, so the formula simply walks you from a stud count to a real-world measurement at your chosen scale. Run the number against a known reference — a minifigure, a door, a car — and you will catch a mismatched scale in seconds.

## Converting brick counts across scales

![Brick element detail](https://brickplan2-1317217681.cos.ap-hongkong.myqcloud.com/blog/moc-scale-conversion-guide/moc-scale-conversion-guide-3.jpg)

When you re-scale a 1:32 vehicle plan to 1:48, you cannot simply multiply the part count by 1.5. Volume is three-dimensional, so the **brick count** grows to roughly (48/32)³ ≈ 3.4 times the original figure. That is a serious jump in your parts budget, and it is the single most common reason builders abandon a conversion halfway through.

A practical approach:

- Fix the new length, width and height first, in studs, before you touch the parts list.
- Estimate the brick count from surface area rather than from the original part count.
- Budget about 10% extra of the common, generic parts — 2x4 bricks, plates, tiles — so you are not stuck waiting on an order mid-build.

For building modifications, the cheapest change is keeping the same scale and only altering the number of floors. You reuse every wall module you already designed and you never have to re-engineer the detail work.

## Resizing: three rules of thumb

1. **Going up is easier than going down.** When you shrink a model, the decorative detail often simply will not fit. Shave a stud off a facade and the windows, sills and trim have nowhere to go.
2. **Change flat plate layers first.** When you do resize, adjust the plain plate courses rather than parts carrying prints or stickers. Printed elements are fixed in size, so they dictate the dimensions around them.
3. **Check connection points after every change.** Go back to the plan and confirm that the Technic pins, clips and stud connections still line up once the surrounding geometry has moved.

If you are building from scratch rather than converting, stay at the plan's native scale. Avoiding the second design pass saves a lot of time, and it keeps the proportions the original designer worked out.

Before you start, export a parts list from a platform such as Rebrickable. Seeing the real inventory up front — and comparing it against what is already in your bins — dramatically reduces the amount of rework later.

## Summary

Once you are comfortable with **LEGO MOC scale conversion**, reading a plan gets much faster: identify the scale from the minifigure, calculate the brick count to set your budget, then decide whether resizing is worth it. Scale is the foundation of an MOC — get it solid and the creative part has something reliable to stand on.
