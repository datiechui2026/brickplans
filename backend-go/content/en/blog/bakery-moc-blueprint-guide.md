# Bakery MOC Build Guide: Counter, Shelves, and Signage

A bakery is one of the friendliest subjects for a first street-scene MOC. The footprint is manageable, the palette is warm, and most of the parts are common. You do not need a full storefront to get a convincing result; even half a shop can look great on display. This guide works through the three core areas — the baking counter, the cabinets and shelves, and the sign — with a parts list and structural notes so you can build a small shop with depth and a bit of story. Use it whether you are filling a gap in a street-scene row or making a standalone window display.

## 1. Plan First: Set Size and Modules

Before you pick up a brick, decide where the bakery MOC will live. A common approach is a standard street-scene module about 16 studs deep, with the height aligned to the buildings on either side. For a desktop display, you can shrink the width to 12 studs. Modular design is worth the extra planning: make the baking area, counter area, shelving area, and storefront sign as separate baseplate units that can be lifted apart. Later layout changes and transport become much easier.

A practical order is baseplate and walls first, then door and window openings, then interior fittings. Many beginners do the opposite and pile up interior detail first. When the door goes in and the walls close, the counter sticks out through the wall and the work has to be redone. Treat the outside outline as the first priority.

## 2. Bakery Counter: The Dessert Display Centerpiece

The counter is the visual center of the bakery MOC, and it has to work as both a work surface and a display. A two-level structure works well: use plates to form a storage cabinet below, lay tiles across the top for a flat counter, and leave a small ledge around the front edge for trays and bread.

The bread itself is the heart of the theme. A croissant can be built from a few brown and tan elements plus curved pieces to make the hooked shape. For toast, stack 2x2 tiles and sandwich a dark layer in the middle to suggest the cut face. A baguette can lie across the counter as a long bar made from several light brown plates. Keep one rule in mind: the bread does not need to be realistic. A unified color scheme and medium density matter more, and a counter that looks full reads better than a sparse one.

If you have transparent light elements or a lighting kit, add a small light under the counter to make the bakery MOC feel warmer at night. Without lighting parts, a large window in the wall brings in a natural light position and gives the same sense of openness.

## 3. Cabinets and Shelves: Build Depth With Height Differences

Cabinets and shelves fill the depth of the shop and stop the interior from looking like an empty box. Put a set of shelves on each side wall, fix the shelves horizontally with 1xN plates, and leave one brick of height between them so goods are easy to reach. Place a low cabinet near the door and a tall cabinet deep against the wall. The front-low, back-high arrangement gives the interior layers and makes photos feel deeper.

Reuse common bakery parts for the goods: a small cake is a 1x1 cylinder with a half-sphere on top, a cupcake is an inverted cone, and a cookie is a 2x2 round plate. To keep the shelves from looking repetitive, change the goods color every other shelf — one dark brown, one light yellow, one red with a fruit accent. The rhythm appears at once.

## 4. Signage and Storefront: Instant Brand Recognition

The sign decides how recognizable the bakery MOC is. The safest solution is a store name plus a marker: a large bread or bread ring built in three dimensions above the door. For the sign board, use a transparent element as the base, spell the name with white letter tiles, and close the back with plates. It looks like a lightbox and stays sturdy.

Do not forget the details at the sides of the sign: an awning under the eaves, a small flower bed at the door, and an open sign hanging by the window. These few-stud designs cost very little and greatly improve how complete the shop feels. Many strong builds stay interesting because of these quiet corners.

## 5. Parts List and Color Palette

Here is a general parts list to use as a reference; adjust the quantities to your shop size:

- Baseplates and wall panels: 1 to 2 large baseplates, assorted 1x4 and 1x6 plates
- Countertops and shelving: 2x4 and 2x6 tiles, assorted 1x8 plates
- Bread models: a batch of brown, tan, and beige small elements; curved pieces, cylinders, and half-spheres
- Doors and windows: transparent glass elements, door frames, window frames
- Signage: letter tiles, transparent baseplates, post elements
- Decoration: flowers, leaf elements, small animal elements (a cat on the sign is a nice touch)

For color, a bakery suits warm tones: cream and off-white for the main body, brown as a wood-grain accent, and red or orange as an accent to make the food feel appetizing. Avoid large areas of cool color, which weaken the warm bakery atmosphere.

## 6. Three Practical Tips for Beginners

First, finish one complete small unit, such as a single baking counter, before spreading out. It is easier to stay with the project to the end. Second, build the bread models on a separate plate first. When you are happy with them, move them into the counter as a group and avoid repeated rebuilding. Third, study photos of real bakeries and look at the relative positions of the counter, shelves, and cash desk. Getting the spatial logic right matters more than adding more detail.

Start your own bakery MOC with one baseplate and build the warmth into the model piece by piece. If you have completed a street-scene row, combine it with the other shop modules; the whole street will gain a stronger sense of story at once.
