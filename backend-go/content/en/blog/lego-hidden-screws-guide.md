# How to Hide Pins and Connectors in LEGO MOCs

Every builder eventually hits the same frustration: the model is solid, the shaping works, and then you look at the shell and see a row of pin holes, a stud poking out of a smooth flank, or an axle end jutting from a wheel arch. Those details break the line of the model. Hiding them is not a single trick but a set of habits — covering, filling and concealing — that separate a functional build from a finished one. Here is how to apply them.

## Why Exposed Pins and Connectors Matter

In brick building, the "screws" we talk about are not metal fasteners. They are the **Technic pins, cross-axle ends, hinge joints and protruding connection points** that hold a structure together. They are what makes a model strong, and at the same time they are visual noise. If you want a car, mech or building facade that reads as a real object rather than a toy skeleton, those functional elements have to disappear beneath the outer skin. That is the whole value of hiding them: an invisible structure supporting a visible shape.

## Skinning the Frame with Panels and Tiles

The most direct approach is to dress the skeleton in an outer layer. Build the load-bearing core from Technic bricks and beams with holes, then wrap the outside completely with **panels and tiles** so no pin head shows. Panels have their own clips and snap tightly onto the frame, which makes pin positions vanish entirely. Tiles give you a flat, stud-free top surface — ideal for hoods, roofs and wall planes.

The rule to remember: your outer skin should always be made of parts with **no holes and no studs**. That is what produces a genuinely clean surface.

## Use SNOT and Upside-Down Building

SNOT — Studs Not On Top — is the advanced builder's favorite concealment method. By mounting bricks sideways or inverted, you can point exposed studs inward or onto a vertical face, so the front of the model shows no studs and no pins at all. Combined with **tiles** and upside-down construction, you can create seamless joints on walls, floors and even curved surfaces. This is the most technically demanding part of hiding connections, and the one with the biggest payoff.

## Inner Liners and Double Shells

When the outer decorative parts are thin and interior space is tight, use a two-layer shell: the inner layer carries the load and hides the pins, the outer layer is purely cosmetic. Space the two apart with 1x1 elements or thin plates — enough to create a gap, while the outside shows nothing of the structure inside. For awkward connectors such as ball joints and turntables, you can add a shroud or a decorative element at the liner so the joint reads as a design feature: a cooling vent, an intake grille, a mechanical detail. Two problems solved with one part.

## Visual Tricks: Color and Gaps

Beyond physical covering, **color handling** does a lot of the work. Dark parts and same-color parts reduce how noticeable a hole appears, and deliberately filling a gap with a thin plate in a matching color removes the sense of a seam. For pins that must stay visible, swap them for versions in the same color as the surrounding shell so they blend in rather than jumping out at the eye. Get the details right and the model simply looks cleaner.

## Putting It Into Practice

Hiding connectors is not a slogan — it is a method you build up through repetition. Start with the basics: panel covering on a simple vehicle hood, learning how panels and skin parts work together. Then move to SNOT flipping and inverted mounting on a small sub-assembly where mistakes are cheap to fix. Double shells and color camouflage come last, because they depend on getting the layering right first.

Experienced builders can push toward full-SNOT mech torsos or building facades, where every stud faces inward and the exterior is nothing but tiles and slopes. The reward is the moment a model stops looking like a collection of parts and starts looking like an object — no pin holes, no stray studs, just clean lines. Pick up a handful of panels and tiles and try it on your next build.
