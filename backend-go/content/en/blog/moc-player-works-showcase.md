# MOC Showcase Guide: Photos, Lighting, and Where to Post

After years of building, the lesson that has stuck with me hardest is this: a finished MOC that sits in a cabinet collecting dust is a waste. Showing your work is the most underrated part of the whole hobby. The same model, shot by someone who knows what they're doing, can pull hundreds or thousands of likes; shot badly, it just sits there. Below is everything I've learned about presenting builds, from the boring prep to the part where people actually reply to your post.

## Step 1: Clean the model before you think about the camera

Do the basic work before anything else. Studs and tiles collect dust, and fingers leave oil marks on the surface — on dark parts it shows up almost immediately. My habit is to let a finished model sit for a day or two first, so the parts settle and grip properly, then go over it gently with a soft brush and a slightly damp cotton cloth.

Never reach for alcohol or solvents. Light-coloured parts will yellow, and printed parts will lose their colour. Trading a clean-looking photo for a permanently discoloured model is not a good deal.

## Lighting: the soul of MOC presentation

Light sets the ceiling on what your photos can be. Plenty of newcomers just switch on the room's ceiling light and end up with images that are dim and completely flat, because a single overhead source kills every shadow the model needs to read as three-dimensional. What I suggest:

- Put the key light 45 degrees to the front-left of the model, which gives natural depth
- Bounce light back in with a sheet of white card on the right to lift shadows that get too deep
- Avoid hard, direct light; a softbox or a layer of white gauze is a good helper
- For a night scene, turn off the main lights and use a warm small desk lamp to stand in for street lights

## A simple lighting setup to copy

If you don't want to study complicated lighting, start with this combination I use often:

| Scene | Light source | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daytime | Natural window light | Full-body overall shot |
| Night | Warm desk lamp at 45 degrees | Warm, cosy atmosphere |
| Close-up | Phone fill light | Picking out local detail |

This setup needs no expensive gear — one phone plus one desk lamp is enough. Get these three light sources working properly first, then slowly try more possibilities; your presentation level will climb a step on its own.

## Background and angle: clean beats fancy

The simpler the background, the more it flatters the model itself. Plain coloured card, a matte tabletop, or a piece of light grey cloth are all classics for good reason — none of them compete with the build for attention. On angles, I like to shoot one full-body frame at eye level first, then a top-down pass for details, and finally a low angle for close-ups so the viewer gets the illusion of stepping into the scene. Street dioramas live and die on camera position, so try several viewpoints and don't worry about wasting shutter clicks.

## Posting channels and community interaction

Once the photos are ready, where they go matters. Brick communities each have their own personality — some lean toward the building process, others toward finished detail — so the same set of images can be re-cropped for different platforms, with the most striking shot placed first. When you post, write a real title and a short note on the thinking behind the build; even two or three sentences gives people a reason to stop and look instead of scrolling past.

Replying to comments counts just as much. When creators are willing to engage, a community gets livelier, and that back-and-forth is the warmest part of showing MOC work.

## Treat the showcase as part of the build

In the end, presenting a model isn't showing off — it's a conversation. Working through composition, lighting and a short write-up forces you to see your build's strengths and weak points more clearly, and the next model comes out better for it.
