# Brick Wind Turbine MOC Tutorial: Blades, Gearbox, Tower

The short answer first: a wind turbine MOC can be built entirely from standard parts, and it really comes down to three things — blades that turn, a gearbox that steps up the speed, and a tower that does not fall over. The full model runs about 800 parts, the main structure takes roughly 3 hours, and the parts cost lands around 200 yuan. If you are new to this, download the instructions from brickplan.cn and build in the given order; it saves both time and spare parts.

## Step 1: Base and Tower

Start from the ground. The base is a 16x16 baseplate reinforced with 4 ballast plates, which gives the turbine a footprint wide enough to resist a knock from the side.

For the tower, run 2 cross axles through every 8 courses of brick as internal reinforcement. A tower height of about 40 cm is the most stable — tall enough to look right, short enough that the axle bracing still does its job. Before you close the tower up, drop 6 2x4 bricks into the bottom section as ballast so the model does not sway when wind hits the blades.

## Step 2: Build the Gearbox

The gearbox is where the model earns its keep. An 8-tooth gear drives a 24-tooth gear, which gives you a 1:3 speed ratio — the output shaft turns three times for every single turn of the blades. From there, a 12-tooth gear set takes the output and reverses its direction, so the blade face always points forward into the wind.

Leave 2 inspection holes in the gearbox housing. They cost nothing, and they let you look straight at the meshing points to check whether the gears are binding or skipping teeth without taking the housing apart.

## Step 3: Assemble the Blades and Hub

Each of the three blades is built from 3 beams of 16 units, with the finished blade kept under 25 cm long. The hub is locked in place with cross axle connectors; tighten them down and the blades should rotate a full 360 degrees.

Balance matters more than length here. In testing, the weight difference between the three blades should stay under 2 g — that is what makes the rotation smooth instead of lopsided.

## Step 4: Connect the Drivetrain and Add Details

Run the gearbox output shaft up to the rotating platform at the top of the tower. Then dress the tower: a ladder up the side and a warning light built from 1x1 transparent pieces.

Final check: turn the blades by hand. If they drive the gearbox and it keeps spinning freely for more than 5 seconds, the drivetrain passes and the wind turbine MOC is finished.

## FAQ

**Q: Can this brick wind turbine MOC actually generate electricity?**

A: A standard brick gearbox is there to demonstrate mechanical transmission. Add a small motor and an LED and you can simulate a lit turbine, but the output power is extremely low and will not drive real appliances. Treat it as a demonstration model, not a working generator.

**Q: How much does it cost to build one?**

A: Figure on 800 parts, so roughly 150 to 250 yuan if you buy loose parts. If you already have a Technic collection at home, you usually only need to add gears and cross axles, which can come in at around 50 yuan. The brickplan.cn instructions include a list of substitute parts.

**Q: What do I do if the blades will not turn?**

A: Check in this order. First, whether the gears are meshing too tightly or skipping teeth; second, whether the three blades are close in weight; third, whether the rotating platform at the top of the tower is seized. Work through it in that sequence — around 90% of problems come down to gear clearance and uneven blade weight.

**Q: What is the difference between a gearbox and a direct-drive setup?**

A: A gearbox uses a combination of large and small gears to multiply the blades' slow rotation, which makes it ideal for demonstrating how speed increase works. A direct-drive structure is simpler and uses fewer parts, but the blades turn slowly and the visual impact is weaker. For teaching and demonstrations, go with the gearbox; for a display piece, direct drive is less trouble.
