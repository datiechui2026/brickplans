# Cow MOC Blueprint Review: Proportions, Patches, Stance

Verdict first: what makes or breaks a brick cow is not the part count. It is three things — a rounded body, black-and-white patches that sit at natural, irregular intervals, and four legs that carry the weight vertically so the model actually stands.

Cows are one of those animal MOC subjects that look easy and turn out not to be. A barrel body, the trademark black-and-white pattern, four steady legs — get any of it slightly wrong and you end up with a spotted dog. This review breaks those three problems down one at a time.

## 1. Body Proportions: Head, Torso and Four Hooves

The correct cow proportion is small head, big body, short thick limbs. In a cow MOC blueprint the torso is usually stacked from large bricks plus curved slopes to produce a rounded barrel shape, with the centre of gravity kept low — that is where the stocky, good-natured look comes from. The head has to be noticeably smaller than the torso, with slope pieces pulling the muzzle slightly forward, plus two small horns. That is enough to sell the expression.

The four hooves are the foundation of the stance. Short, thick cylindrical pieces make better lower legs than long thin ones; the hoof tips can be picked out in dark grey or black small parts so the feet read as hooves. Spread the four contact points outward as far as you can and arrange them in a trapezoid, so the centre of gravity falls inside the area the legs enclose. When a beginner's cow will not stand up, the cause is almost always legs that are too thin and contact points that are too close together.

## 2. Black-and-White Patch Schemes

The pattern is the soul of the model and the part that tests your patience. There are two common approaches. The first is white base with black patches: build the body in white, then apply black plates to the flanks and back at the positions the blueprint specifies. The second is black base with white patches, which suits cows with a heavier pattern. In most cow MOC blueprints the patch positions are fixed, so follow the drawings rather than substituting black and white pieces on instinct — swap them around and the cow quickly stops reading as a cow.

On colour detail, the muzzle, the inside of the ears and the belly are usually accented in light pink, with a little grey used to soften the edges of the black patches. Buy black parts in a single batch to avoid colour mismatch between production runs. For the patches themselves, 2x2 and 2x4 plates are the easiest pieces to work with.

## 3. Building a Stance That Holds

Stability is one of the most important things to judge in a cow blueprint. One practical trick is to treat the four legs as a single unit: instead of building each leg separately, tie the tops of all four together with a baseplate or a beam, then add the torso on top. The load spreads through the frame and the model stands without argument. Inside the torso it also pays to leave a solid core, so a hollow shell does not shift the centre of gravity.

If the blueprint includes a posable head and neck or a tail, check that the joints hold tightly enough — otherwise the model will droop its head and sag in the back whenever you pose it. To test the stance, press lightly on the back and watch for wobble. If the four feet sit on the same plane and splay slightly outward, it is not going to fall over.

## 4. Blueprint Quality and Difficulty

Cow MOC blueprints differ mainly in three places: whether the parts list is complete, whether the step diagrams separate colours clearly, and whether multi-angle renders are provided. A good blueprint marks the position of every patch, sometimes highlighting them step by step. A rough one gives you a single overall render and leaves the pattern to guesswork, which is exactly where beginners come unstuck.

On difficulty, cow MOCs sit firmly in the beginner-friendly range. Entry-level designs of roughly 200-350 parts are mostly static display models with tidy structure and can be finished in an afternoon. Advanced designs of around 350-600 parts add a posable head and more complex patch transitions, and suit builders who have already finished a few models. Beginners should look for two things when choosing a blueprint: a colour-separated parts list and step-by-step diagrams. Those two features save a lot of dead ends.

## Final Thoughts

Keep the three rules in view while you build: small head and big body with short thick limbs, white base with black patches plus pink accents, and four legs tied together with contact points spread wide.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose a cow MOC blueprint?**
A: Check three things first: whether the body proportions are rounded and stocky, whether the black-and-white patches fall naturally rather than in mechanical symmetry, and whether the four legs carry the load vertically. Pass those three and the finished model will not turn into a spotted dog.

**Q: How should the black-and-white patches be arranged?**
A: Use irregular blocks of colour. Start from a white base, distribute black patches in mixed sizes at uneven intervals, and soften the edges with slope pieces. Avoid perfect left-right symmetry — the pattern only looks like a real cow when it is a little random.

**Q: How do I get the cow to stand steadily?**
A: The key is vertical load-bearing in all four hooves. Thicken the legs with brick columns plus plates, and widen the feet with 2x2 or 2x4 plates. Set the spacing between the legs slightly wider than the body, and keep the centre of gravity low so it will not tip sideways.

**Q: What is the difficulty and part count of this blueprint?**
A: Difficulty is low to medium and the part count is small to medium. It is built mainly from basic bricks and plates with no complex mechanisms, which makes it good practice for beginners and a solid entry point into animal MOCs.

The core of building a brick cow MOC: proportions of small head, big body and short thick legs; colour of white base, black patches and pink accents; structure of four legs joined as one with contact points spread outward. Follow those three lines and pick a blueprint with a complete parts list and clear steps, and even a first-time builder can produce a cow that stands firm and looks thoroughly contented.
