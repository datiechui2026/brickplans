# Build a Working Cable Car MOC: Towers, Cabins, Cable Drive

The short version first: roughly 1,500 pieces and about seven hours of building gets you a cableway MOC with a working loop drive — four cabins, three support towers and an 80 cm cable run. Turn the hand crank and the cabins travel up and down the mountain scene.

Cable cars are the signature structure of any mountain resort, which is why they make such good brick subjects: mechanically interesting and highly displayable. What lifts this design above a static model is the manual cable drive — the cabins circulate continuously rather than sitting fixed on a slope — and the mountain base, which makes the whole thing photograph well. Build it in the order below and each stage is testable before the next one goes on top.

**Step 1: Gather the parts and plan the route.** Lay out an 80 cm line with a station at each end and three support towers in between. Core parts: 8 pulleys, 2 rubber-band drive sets, 4 cabin hangers, two 32x32 green baseplates, and a supply of brown bricks for the mountain body. Budget around 400 RMB. Do the layout on the floor before you commit: the distance between the towers decides how much the cable sags, and the two stations need to sit at matching heights or the loop will run unevenly from the first turn of the crank.

**Step 2: Build the support towers.** Stack 2x2 column bricks to a height of about 20 bricks, then mount the pulley assembly on top. Reinforce each tower base with a 6x6 baseplate plus counterweight bricks so nothing sways while the cable is running — a tower that flexes by a millimetre at the base will throw the cable off the pulley at the top. Vary the three towers — tall in the middle, lower at both ends — to echo the rise and fall of real terrain.

**Step 3: Run the cable and the drive.** Loop the rubber band between the pulleys at the two stations and the pulleys at the tower tops to form a closed circuit, and fit a hand crank at one station. Turn the crank and the cable carries the hangers along, so the cabins circulate. Because the loop is closed, every hanger moves at the same speed and the cabins stay evenly spaced around the circuit. The drive is geared 1:3, which keeps cranking easy while staying smooth. Test the loop with two empty hangers before you build a single cabin — it is much easier to chase a misaligned pulley without cabins swinging off the line.

**Step 4: Build the cabins.** Give each cabin transparent window pieces on all four sides, a hanger hook on the roof, and two 1x2 weight bricks underneath to keep it level. The low centre of gravity matters: a cabin that hangs tail-heavy will tilt at every tower and clip the structure. Build four cabins and colour them red, yellow, blue and green so they read clearly while moving and you can follow an individual cabin all the way round the loop.

**Step 5: Build the mountain base.** Cover the ground with the green baseplates, pile brown and dark green bricks into slopes, and dress the scene with tree pieces, minifigures and a canopy over the station. Build the mountain hollow so the drive parts hide inside — much cleaner from the outside, and it gives you room to reach in when something needs adjusting. The finished layout covers about 32x32 studs, which is a landscape in itself on a shelf at home.

## FAQ

**How many parts and how much budget does the cableway MOC need?**

About 1,500 pieces and around 400 RMB, with pulleys and drive parts accounting for most of the cost. The mountain can be filled with bulk basic bricks, which keeps that part cheap.

**What do I do if the cabins stutter?**

First check that all tower-top pulleys sit in the same plane — any offset pulls the cable off line. Then check the rubber band tension: too tight and cranking becomes a chore, too loose and it slips. Once adjusted, running is much smoother.

**Can it be motorised?**

Yes — swap the hand crank for an M motor. Keep the motor speed low, though, or the cabins sway as they pass through a station. The conversion costs about 60 RMB.

**Is this cableway MOC worth building?**

Yes. It is one of the few MOCs with real moving mechanics, the hand-cranked loop is genuinely fun to operate, and the mountain scene displays well — better value than a set at the same price.

**How is a cableway different from a Ferris wheel build?**

A Ferris wheel turns around a fixed axle and its structure is simple. A cableway is linear circulation along a closed cable, which involves pulley sets and tension adjustment: richer mechanics, and a harder build.
