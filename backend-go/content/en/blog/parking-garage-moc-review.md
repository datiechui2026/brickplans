# Parking Garage MOC Review: Spiral-Ramp Multi-Storey Build

Once your street layout has houses and shops in place, the missing piece becomes obvious: you have cars and roads, but nowhere to put the cars. For this parking garage MOC review I built a multi-level garage from the instructions, working through the spiral ramp, the parking bays, the barrier gate and the rooftop details, so you can get a straight answer on whether it deserves a spot in your district.

## 1. Instructions and parts: big volume, built one floor at a time

The model is drawn at 1:48 minifigure street scale and comes in at roughly 2,600 pieces spread over 9 numbered bags, which puts it on the large side for a street building. The manual works strictly bottom-up: foundation, first floor, second floor, roof. Every step highlights the parts being added, and the repeated floors call out the few pieces that differ between levels, so it is genuinely hard to build the same storey twice by mistake.

The palette is restrained: concrete grey, white, warning yellow and dark grey. That keeps the model architectural rather than toy-like. The standout elements are the railings and columns: the columns use thick axle parts and the railings are printed bars, which will outlast stickers by a wide margin. Missing parts were rare. Instructions and parts score 8.5/10.

## 2. Structure and ramp: the cars really do drive up

The usual failure mode for a parking garage MOC is a flat box with rectangular holes punched in it. This one avoids that:

- **Spiral ramp.** Between the first and second floors, four large slope panels form two turns of a helix. A small car can actually be pushed up the ramp onto level two, and the turnaround points use pinned parts plus roller elements, so the roll stays smooth instead of snagging.
- **Vertical support.** Thickened corner columns carry the load, and the second-floor deck gets liner plates underneath. Fill it with cars and it does not sag.
- **Zoned floors.** Level one holds standard bays, level two is the reserved area for mechanical stacker parking, and the roof is open-air parking. The hierarchy reads clearly at a glance.

The whole assembly is rigid, and the ramp survived repeated car runs without loosening. Structure: 8.5/10.

## 3. Play value: barrier gate, parking bays and roof parking

This is where the model wins people over. A barrier arm sits at the entrance, built from a thin rod and a clip connection, and the lift-and-drop action feels unusually good: push a car up to it and the arm gives way to let it through. The ground floor has painted bay lines, with stud pieces marking out different bay sizes so sedans and vans both fit. The rooftop terrace gets railings and a stairway entrance, which sets up scenes like parking up top to look down over the street. More thoughtful still, a guard booth and a QR-code payment pole are built in, so adding an attendant minifigure gives you a full pay-on-entry story.

The compromise: the ramp does not continue spiralling all the way to the roof, and the roof is reached by stairs instead. Playability: 8.5/10.

## 4. Accuracy and architectural feel

The barrier, bay markings, ramp, columns and railings are all present, and the result is instantly recognizable as a standard multi-storey garage. Add grey-white lighting and the stacked structure between the first and second floors photographs beautifully; with headlights showing through, the night scene reads like traffic filing into the building. The small gripes are a fairly flat roof profile and outer walls that are closed off over much of their surface, which costs some daylight inside. Atmosphere: 8/10.

**Verdict: worth buying, especially for street-scene builders whose cars outnumber their buildings.** Difficulty is medium-to-high, around 6 to 7 hours, though a newer builder working alongside an experienced one will finish it comfortably. It suits three kinds of builder in particular: anyone sitting on a pile of small car MOCs with no parking scenario to put them in; mechanically minded builders who enjoy multi-level structure and pushing cars up a ramp to test it; and city planners who want their street to feel more municipal and finally have a place for vehicles to go. The weak points are that the ramp stops short of the roof and the outer walls feel a little closed in, but for most brick building fans that is a minor trade-off.
