# Ice Cream Truck MOC Build Guide: Serving Window & Colors

Start with proportions: at minifigure scale, keep the body 6 studs wide, 24 to 28 studs long, and no taller than 14 bricks. Build the chassis from technic beams with cross axles for the wheel axles, then add a hinged flip panel to create the serving window.

An ice cream truck is probably the most comforting subject in vehicle MOC building: a rounded body, bright colors, and an open serving hatch make it a natural focal point on any street scene. This guide walks through the chassis, the window, the color scheme, and the interior, one step at a time.

## Chassis and Body Proportions

Set the scale first. At minifigure scale, aim for a body 6 studs wide, 24 to 28 studs long, and no more than 14 bricks tall — that way it parks along a sidewalk without looking out of place. Build the chassis frame from technic parts: two technic beams plus cross axles for the axles, with friction technic pins holding the front and rear wheels so the model survives being pushed around. Enclose the body with plates and leave the side space reserved for the serving window. Up front, add a pair of 1x1 transparent bricks as headlights with a 1x2 plate as the bumper, and leave room for a license plate at the rear — the details immediately make it read as real. Curve the roof slightly, finishing the edges with 1x4x1 curved bricks or 1x2 curved slopes, and the rounded look appears right away.

## The Serving Window: Hinges and Flip Panel

The serving window is the soul of an ice cream truck MOC and the part most worth your attention. Go with a flip-up design: connect the window frame with 1x2 hinge bricks so it swings upward when opened, then use a technic axle as a prop rod to hold it in place, mimicking the awning window of a real food truck. Leave one stud of depth inside the window for the cash register and the ice cream machine. The machine can be two layers of 1x1 round bricks topped with a 1x1 cone as the dispenser, with a few 1x1 round plates hung nearby as scoops. Below the window, add a 2x4 plate that projects outward as a counter, so a minifigure can lean on the window and buy a cone — the interaction sells the whole build.

## Color Scheme: A Dessert-Inspired Formula

An ice cream truck MOC should look sweet, but not sickly. Use this formula: a main color in white or cream at roughly 60 percent, a secondary color in strawberry pink, mint green, or lemon yellow at 30 percent, and the remaining 10 percent in brown or orange as accents. A pink body with white stripes is the classic look, while mint green with dark brown feels more retro. Build the stripes by alternating 1x2 tiles — that reads more like brick building than stickers do. Keep the wheels dark gray and the fenders in the same family as the main color so everything hangs together. The ice cream model on the roof is the finishing touch: stack a 2x2 round plate with 1x1 cones to make a triple-scoop cone and mount it on a rooftop sign frame, and the truck is recognizable from a distance.

## Interior Details and Common Problems

A good ice cream truck MOC has to show its interior through the serving window, or the model looks hollow. Put a cash register inside (a 1x2 tile plus a 1x1 transparent brick), a few drinks (transparent cup parts with 1x1 round plates), and a minifigure clerk. At the rear you can hang an external freezer or a trash can to enrich the side profile. Two problems come up often: first, the window hinge is loose and the panel will not stay up — switch to a friction hinge or add a technic axle as a stop; second, the colors get busy — remember the 60-30-10 rule, since any attempt at more than three main colors tends to fall apart. When it is finished, don't forget to take an "open for business" scene photo. ## FAQ

**Q: How big should an ice cream truck MOC be?**
A: At minifigure scale, aim for 6 studs wide, 24 to 28 studs long, and no more than 14 bricks tall so it sits naturally along a sidewalk; build the chassis from two technic beams with cross axles for the axles, and secure the wheels with friction technic pins.

**Q: How do I build the serving window?**
A: Use a hinge-and-flip-panel structure: mount hinges along the top edge of the window and a support plate along the bottom, so the panel opens and locks horizontally to become a counter for ice cream parts; add a layer of transparent pieces inside the window to simulate glass.

**Q: How do I get a dessert-like color scheme?**
A: Start with low-saturation colors such as cream, pale pink, and mint green, then accent with a little chocolate brown or bright yellow; a two-tone body works best, and a large ice cream element on the roof makes the theme obvious at a glance.

**Q: Which interior details stand out most?**
A: Prioritize the serving counter and the menu board: place small ice cream parts and a tray on the counter and use printed pieces on the wall as a menu; add a minifigure handing over an order and the scene immediately gains story.
