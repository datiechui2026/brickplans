# Star Wars MOC Instructions: Choosing Ships, Vehicles and Dioramas

Star Wars is probably the most durable subject in brick MOC building. Every icon comes with its own building problem: the folding wing mechanism on an X-wing, the unmistakable disc silhouette of the Millennium Falcon, the hemispherical shell of the Death Star. This guide to choosing Star Wars brick MOC instructions gets straight to the point about how to pick plans for starfighters, vehicles and dioramas, and where the details that matter most for accuracy actually live.

## Start with the subject: starfighter, vehicle or diorama

Star Wars MOCs broadly fall into three types, so decide which one you want to build before you go looking for instructions. Starfighters are the lowest barrier to entry and photograph beautifully; an X-wing or TIE fighter is a manageable size, and a row of them on a shelf looks genuinely imposing. Vehicles are the step up: the Millennium Falcon and Star Destroyer are large, part-hungry and slow to build, but the finished model is spectacular. Dioramas demand the most design skill — a Death Star control room or a Tatooine marketplace lives or dies on atmosphere and storytelling in a small space.

Each type asks something completely different of its instructions, so settle your budget and the time you can commit before you choose, or you risk running out of parts halfway through the build.

## Starfighter instructions: wing mechanisms and silhouette proportions

When you choose starfighter MOC instructions, look at the wings first. The X-wing's S-foils need to open and close, and whether the instructions use hinges or technic parts to achieve that decides whether the finished model can actually "transform." On a TIE fighter, the twin hexagonal wings have to stay symmetrical, and the arms connecting the cockpit ball to the wings need enough strength — otherwise the wings sag over time on display.

Silhouette proportions matter just as much. An X-wing's nose has to be long and slender, and the ratio of wingspan, once open, to fuselage length should be close to what the films establish. Plenty of instructions look great in renders but build up "a size too chubby," purely because the wingspan-to-body ratio wasn't controlled. Look at photos of finished builds before you buy, not just the renders.

## Vehicle instructions: structural notes on the Millennium Falcon and Star Destroyer

Vehicle instructions test the internal structure hardest. Take the Millennium Falcon: a disc-shaped hull built only from square bricks will open up gaps along the top and bottom where the curve falls away. Reliable instructions build a skeleton of radial supports inside the disc and then lay panel pieces over the outside. The cockpit should sit offset and protrude slightly, and the radar dish — small as it is — is the finishing touch; without it, recognisability drops by half.

On large vehicles like the Star Destroyer, the biggest pitfalls are mounting and centre of gravity. The hull is an inverted triangle, so the model sits high and tips easily; good instructions usually suggest adding weight to the stand. Large vehicles also consume enormous quantities of dark grey and light grey plates, so check the grey share of the parts list before committing, or you'll be short of parts mid-build.

## Diorama instructions: atmosphere and detail on the Death Star and other scenes

Dioramas are all about atmosphere. The trench-like grooves across the Death Star's surface are its signature feature, and how the instructions use slope and plate pieces to create that regular latitudinal and longitudinal texture is the thing most worth comparing between versions. For interior scenes such as control rooms and corridors, look at where the lighting goes and how clear parts are used — good instructions leave you space to install lights.

Star Wars scenes have another characteristic: heavy reliance on specialised and printed parts.
