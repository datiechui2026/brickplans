# MOC Building Tutorial: Basic Techniques for Beginners

Most builders meet MOC (My Own Creation) models for the first time through someone else's wild build: a castle with overhanging towers or a sports car with a flawless curve. How is any of that actually held together? Nearly every impressive model rests on a handful of fundamental skills. This **MOC building tutorial** won't promise overnight mastery. Instead, it covers the basic building techniques every beginner needs, a build sequence you can follow step by step, and the mistakes that trip people up most often.

## Step 1: Know Your Parts

Before you dig into a pile of loose pieces, work out what each part is called and what it does. The catalog looks endless, but the core falls into a few groups: **bricks** form the skeleton, **plates** handle refinement and clean edges, **slopes** create pitched surfaces and rooflines, and **specialty parts** such as hinges, turntables and Technic bricks with holes add angles and function.

You don't need a huge stock of specialty parts at the start. Spend your budget on the workhorses: plates, bricks and slopes, plus a small number of 1x1 bricks with a side stud and some inverted-stud parts. For color, pick a neutral main palette such as gray, white or tan, then expand the accent colors gradually. Remember: **part count sets the scale, part variety sets the possibilities.**

## Step 2: Three Core Building Techniques

### 1. Stacking: where every structure starts

Stacking means laying bricks one on top of another. It looks trivial, but there is a right way to do it. Watch your **stud alignment** — every stud must seat fully into the underside of the brick above. Shift even slightly and the whole wall leans. Every few courses, cap the wall with a layer of plates; that single habit noticeably stiffens the assembly.

### 2. Staggering: the reason a wall holds together

Staggering is the technique beginners skip most and need most. The rule is simple: **a joint in the upper course should never line up with a joint in the course below.** Offset them the way a bricklayer offsets bricks in a real wall. If every vertical seam sits on the same line, the wall topples at the first nudge. Offset the seams and each brick is pinned by the courses above and below, making the structure several times stronger. Staggering applies to floors and roofs too, not just walls.

### 3. SNOT basics: a door into detail work

SNOT (Studs Not On Top) is the key technique for moving a model past a bumpy, stud-covered look. You don't need the full repertoire at first. Start with the simplest version: place a 1x1 brick with a side stud against the face of a standard brick to create a connection point on the side, then clip an inverted-stud part onto it. Studs that originally faced up now face outward, giving you a flat wall panel or a smooth face. Try it on a small area first, then scale up once it feels natural.

## Step 3: Practice With One Small Model

Theory only goes so far. Here is a beginner build you can follow exactly: a small house roughly 10 cm square.

1. **Lay the foundation**: use a 16x16 plate as the base and ring the edge with 2x2 bricks
2. **Build the walls**: raise all four walls in staggered courses, leaving gaps in the front wall for a window and a door
3. **Cap the walls**: run a layer of plates across the top to prepare for the roof
4. **Add the roof**: pair two slope bricks back to back for a pitched roof, or use flat plates for a flat top
5. **Detail it**: SNOT a window frame onto the opening, and lay a 1x2 tile at the doorway as a doormat

The whole build takes half an hour to an hour. The part count is low and the structure is simple, yet it exercises all three techniques: stacking, staggering and SNOT.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Most beginner failures cluster around a few recurring problems:

- **Seams that don't stagger**: the number one cause of structures falling apart. Train the habit until offsetting seams is automatic
- **Designing for looks and ignoring load**: thin columns and floating roofs look great until something touches them. Thicken the column or add internal support where needed
- **Random color choices**: the more colors, the cheaper a model looks. Keep a tight palette early on — three colors is a good ceiling
- **Discovering a part is backwards after the fact**: check stud direction before pressing a part down, and use a brick separator to lever parts out of a seam rather than forcing them
- **Building for hours without a break**: after three or four hours straight your attention drops and your error rate doubles. Work in sessions instead

## Final Thoughts

This **MOC building tutorial** covers the full beginner foundation: understanding your parts, mastering stacking and staggering, getting started with SNOT, completing one practice build, and sidestepping the usual traps. Get these fundamentals solid and buildings, vehicles or mechs will all feel manageable later on.
