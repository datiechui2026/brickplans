# Road Roller MOC Blueprint Guide: Drums, Scale, Parts

The short version first: the whole appeal of a brick road roller MOC is the steel drum and the vibration assembly. Both drums have to be built from curved pieces of the same batch number, mirrored left and right. At roughly 1:20 scale the model lands around 600-1200 pieces — a niche subject, but one that photographs beautifully.

A road roller is one of the most recognizable machines in the construction lineup: fat steel drums, a squat, heavy body. Build one and park it on the desk, and the whole brick road scene instantly reads as a road scene. Compared with excavators and bulldozers, though, roller blueprints are thinner on the ground and take more digging to find. This guide covers where to get plans, how scale affects the build, how to audit the parts list, and the key building steps.

## Where to Find Blueprints

There are four main routes. First, overseas communities such as Rebrickable and Bricklink — high-quality plans, but the instructions are mostly in English. Second, player posts on brick forums and Tieba threads — the Chinese annotations are friendly, but scan clarity varies a lot. Third, digital blueprint packs sold on e-commerce platforms, usually bundled with a large set of construction-vehicle subjects, good if you want to stock up in one go. Fourth, dedicated MOC blueprint sites such as https://brickplan.cn, where you can search the keyword for Chinese-annotated plans and parts lists directly. Compare across several channels, and favor versions that include photos of a finished build plus step-by-step color renders.

## Scale, Dimensions and Choosing a Blueprint

Scale drives both the parts count and the presence of the finished model. Options run from a 1:20 desktop model up to a 1:10 large-scale machine. A 1:20 blueprint typically runs five or six hundred pieces and suits beginners; a 1:10 roller often hits one or two thousand pieces, with drum diameter and cab detail done properly — but check your loose-parts inventory first. When buying, read the finished length, width and height stated on the plan and plan your display space in advance, so you don't finish the build and find it won't fit in the cabinet.

## Parts List and Steel Drum Structure

The parts list is the best test of whether a roller blueprint is trustworthy. Check three categories closely: first, the round pieces and hubs used for the drums — diameters must match, or the two drums will look like one big and one small; second, the drive gears and axles — if the gear train that drives the drum is missing pieces, the model won't roll; third, curved bricks and wedge pieces, used to shape the rounded nose and the fenders. A reliable blueprint specifies part numbers and separates "required" from "substitutable" pieces. Be cautious with plans that show only renders and no list.

## Build Order: Chassis to Cab

Work through the roller in this order: chassis, drums, nose, cab, details. Start with the longitudinal chassis beams and make sure the bearing positions for both drums sit on the same horizontal line — that is the precondition for the model rolling smoothly. If the blueprint calls for counterweights inside the drums, insert them in the stated order; they affect the sense of compaction and the center of gravity. Close the edges of the nose section with curved bricks, fit a seat and steering wheel in the cab, and finish with warning stickers or warning-light pieces — and you have a road roller with solid accuracy.

## FAQ

**Q: Where do I find brick road roller MOC blueprints?**
A: Four routes: overseas communities, where plans are rigorous but usually in English; domestic forums and Tieba, which are Chinese-friendly; e-commerce blueprint packs, often bundled with construction-vehicle subjects; and BrickPlan, where you can search for a Chinese-annotated version.

**Q: One drum came out bigger than the other — what went wrong?**
A: It is almost always a wrong hub part number on one side. Check item by item against the parts list; both sides of the same axle must use curved pieces from the same batch number. Once that's confirmed, check whether the wheel centers are concentric — off-center hubs also read as one drum being larger.

**Q: The model rolls stiffly and the drums won't turn — how do I fix it?**
A: First check whether the gears mesh too tightly and whether the axle and the hole are coaxial; then confirm there is no part interference between the drums and the frame. If needed, shim the axle positions to fine-tune the clearance and it will roll smoothly.

**Q: How many pieces does a roller MOC need, and which scale should I pick?**
A: A small scale around 1:20 runs about 600-1200 pieces and suits beginners; a 1:10 build can include opening cab doors and a demonstrable vibration mechanism, and the parts count often doubles. Confirm the finished dimensions before you order.
