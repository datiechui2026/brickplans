# How to Read LEGO Instructions: 3-Step Beginner Method

The short answer first: reading LEGO instructions comes down to three moves — recognize the symbols, follow the step images in order, and cross-check the parts list. Get those three habits down and even a 2,000-piece set becomes a steady, low-stress build. "How do I read LEGO instructions?" is one of the most common questions from new builders, and the good news is that official manuals and the MOC instructions on brickplan all use the same underlying logic. Here is the whole system in one pass.

## Step 1: Learn the Basic Symbols First

Four symbols appear most often in LEGO instructions. Arrows show the direction a piece is inserted. Dashed lines show the position of pieces that are hidden from view. A circle highlights the pieces newly added in that step. Numbers give the assembly order. Take a set of roughly 1,000 pieces as an example: the manual runs about 180 pages, but each page usually shows only 2 to 3 new steps, so following the numbering keeps you on track without confusion.

Beyond those four basics, watch the color cues. Dark grey normally marks pieces that are already in place, light grey marks the pieces added in the current step, and colored pieces mark key structural elements. Most manuals put a legend page in the first two pages. Spend five minutes reading that legend before you start — understanding the key saves more time than diving straight into the build. Also stop and read any callout box with an exclamation mark; those usually flag the spots where it is easy to install something facing the wrong way.

## Step 2: Build Step by Step in Order

Every step image has its step number in the lower right corner. The grey portion of the image is what you have already built; only the colored portion is new. The most common beginner mistake is skipping steps, and the rework that causes typically eats up 30% of the total build time. A good habit is to look back every 10 steps and confirm that every stud is properly aligned. Going a little slower beats tearing something apart.

Your build environment matters for efficiency too. Work somewhere with plenty of light and a flat table, and sort the pieces into a few boxes by color before you start. Saving 10 to 20 seconds on every piece search adds up to more than an hour across a 1,000-piece set. If you get interrupted mid-build, photograph the current step with your phone. When you come back, the photo puts you right back on track without re-checking the whole model from the beginning.

## Step 3: Verify Quantities Against the Parts List

The last few pages of a manual usually contain the parts list, with bricks grouped by color and type. Count your pieces against that list before you start building. A missing-piece rate under 1% is considered normal. If something really is missing, look up the part number on the brickplan instructions detail page, then order a replacement through official channels. MOC parts lists usually note quantity, color and part number, and sorting from that list can halve your prep time.

When you check the list, print it out or keep it on your phone and tick off each type as you count it. If a piece shows up in the instructions but not on the list, stop and check whether you are confusing two similar parts — a 2x2 brick and a 2x2 tile are mixed up all the time. On brickplan, MOC parts lists on the detail page are grouped by color, and you can copy the part numbers straight over to shopping sites to compare prices. On a 500-piece MOC, comparing three sellers typically saves around 20% on the cost of parts.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I read LEGO instructions without making mistakes?**
A: Learn the symbols first, then build in numbered order and look back every 10 steps to compare. Grey means existing pieces, color means new ones. Never skip a step — that alone cuts your error rate by more than half.

**Q: Can I ignore the dashed lines in the instructions?**
A: No. Dashed lines show where a hidden piece goes, usually paired with an arrow, and they warn you to push a piece into a spot you cannot see. It only counts as seated when you hear the click.

**Q: What is the difference between MOC instructions and official ones?**
A: Official instructions have finer step breakdowns and more standardized symbols because professional teams produce them. MOC instructions are made by hobbyists and vary in style, but the basic symbols are universal — after a few on brickplan you will adjust quickly.

**Q: Can I find instructions for free if I lose mine?**
A: Many official manuals can be downloaded as free PDFs from the LEGO website. For MOCs it depends on what the author allows; most instructions on brickplan are free, and paid ones only cost a few dollars.
