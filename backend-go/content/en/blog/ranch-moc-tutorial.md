# Brick Ranch MOC Tutorial: Cabin, Corral and Farmland

A ranch is the most soothing setting in the whole countryside genre: one small wooden cabin, a ring of fencing, a few plots of grass, plus a farmer and some animals, and you have a quiet rural picture. The problem is that many builders end up with "a pile of houses on a green baseplate" — no life in it at all. This **brick ranch MOC tutorial** works through layout, cabin, corral and farm machinery detail by detail so your build has a story and a bit of lived-in warmth.

## 1. Plan the Layout First: Give the Ranch a Route

Sketch the layout on paper before you place a single brick. For a standard small ranch, try this: cabin in the bottom-right corner, corral on the left, a dirt road running through the middle to the main gate, a small vegetable patch in front of the house, and a few strips of meadow in the distance. The key is to **leave walking room** — lay a road of dirt-colored plates and tiles right across the scene so the cabin, corral and field are all connected, and your minifigures have a path to follow. Push all the buildings to one side and keep the open activity space in the front-middle, otherwise the composition feels blocked. Draw the map before you build, or you will finish the cabin and discover the road is walled off.

## 2. The Cabin: The Visual Center of the Ranch

The cabin is the star of a **brick ranch MOC**, and a small gable-roofed hut is the best choice. Build the walls from light brown and beige bricks to imitate plank siding, and use 1x1 bricks with a round hole in the side to suggest wood grain. For the roof, use dark brown and red slope bricks in an inverted V; close the ridge with ridge cap pieces, or butt two slopes against each other. Add a porch at the front: two columns plus a top plate, with a rocking chair and a bucket underneath. Run a chimney of gray bricks up through the roof slope and top it with a dark slope as a chimney cap. Keep the cabin small — a 12x12 stud footprint — so it reads as small but detailed.

## 3. Corral and Fences: The Soul of the Countryside

The corral is what makes a ranch feel like a ranch. The most honest fence method: stand a 1x1 round bar every 2 studs, then run 1 or 2 horizontal bar pieces between them to form a wooden rail fence; at corners, use an L-shaped piece or interlock two fence sections. Cover the corral floor with dirt-colored plates in tan and dark tan, then scatter a few 1x1 round plates as hay. Keep the fence low enough to **see through** — build it too tall and it hides the meadow behind. Leave a fence gate at the entrance, made openable with hinge pieces or a pin, so cows, horses and minifigures can walk in and out. That single detail adds instant interaction.

## 4. Livestock and Farm Machinery: Make the Ranch Busy

Animals are where the life comes from. For a cow: build the body from white or black-and-white 1x2 curved slope bricks, the head from a 1x1 round brick plus horns, and the four legs from short bar pieces. For a horse, buy an official horse piece or build a brown silhouette. Do not skip the tools: a small red tractor (2x6 plate body plus two large wheels), a stack of hay bales (yellow cylinder pieces plus brown bars), and a wheelbarrow. Park them beside the corral and along the dirt road and the scene immediately looks like someone is working. Use bright red and bright yellow for the machinery so they pop against the warm countryside palette.

## 5. Fields and Meadow: Build Color in Layers

Never cover the grass with a single green baseplate — layer it. Start with a dark green base, then randomly overlap light green and yellow-green plates to create depth of grass color. For the vegetable patch, build beds from brown plates, set a row of green slope pieces on the ridges as seedlings, and edge the plot with low plates or tiles. In the distance, add a fence line (same method as the corral) or a few trees (brown stem plus dark green slope canopy) as background. Separate the field from the meadow with a dirt road so the color transition feels natural. For water, add a small well or a drinking trough (gray half-round pieces plus a water blue plate) — one detail of animals drinking and the countryside feel jumps.

## 6. Build Order and Pitfalls to Avoid

Recommended order: base and grass layers, then the dirt road, then the cabin, then the corral fence, then the field, then livestock, machinery and trees to finish. Three things go wrong most often: using one green for all the grass, which makes the scene look flat; building the fence gate fixed shut so minifigures cannot get in; and spreading the machinery and animals too evenly so nothing feels grouped. Remember **leave a route, layer the colors, group the props** and your **brick ranch MOC** stops being a display piece and becomes a living scene.

Once the cabin and corral are done, try adding a threshing floor, a few chickens and ducks, and a street lamp for evening light — the picture gains even more story.
