# Brick Gear Transmission Techniques: Ratios, Types, Worm Drives

Most Technic builders have lived through it: the motor is spinning, but the wheels do not move — or the gears grind and lock with a nasty clicking sound. Nearly always, the root cause is a gear train that was never thought through. Gear transmission is the drivetrain of any mechanical MOC, from a carousel to a tracked vehicle, and it is what carries rotation from the motor to wherever the motion is needed. This guide works from gear types and gear ratios through to worm gears and clutch gears, so you can plan a drivetrain that runs smoothly and stays reliable.

## Why gear transmission matters

A gear's job is to transfer rotation and change how that rotation behaves. Without gears, a motor can only drive one axle directly — no turning corners, no speed reduction, no reversing direction. Understanding transmission means building a "chain" mindset: decide where the power comes from, where it has to go, and how many stages sit in between, and only then start building. When something goes wrong in a complex mechanism, you can then trace the chain stage by stage instead of tearing the whole model apart.

## Choosing the right gear type

Brick gears fall into roughly four families, each with a different job. The spur gear is the most common: it transfers motion between parallel axles and is the default choice for gearboxes and reducers. Bevel gears change the direction of drive, turning a horizontal axle into a vertical one — standard equipment in turntables and differentials. A worm gear paired with a worm wheel delivers a large reduction and also blocks reverse drive, which makes it ideal for self-locking structures such as crane arms. Rack gears convert rotation into linear motion, perfect for sliding doors and push-pull mechanisms. Stock these four types and you can handle the vast majority of builds.

## Calculating gear ratios

Gear ratio is the heart of the whole system, because it sets the trade-off between speed and torque. The maths is simple: divide the tooth count of the output gear by the tooth count of the input gear. The larger the ratio, the slower the output and the greater the torque. Fit an 8-tooth pinion on the motor and drive a 24-tooth gear, for example, and you get a 3:1 ratio — one third of the speed and three times the force. Want brute force? Chain several small-to-large reductions. Want speed? Go large-to-small to overdrive. One rule to remember: it is better to add another reduction stage than to force a single gear pair to carry an overload.

## Connecting axles and gears

A beautiful drivetrain is useless if the connections are loose. When mounting gears, check the hole type: a round hole lets the gear spin freely on the axle (useful for idler gears), while a cross-axle hole locks it so it turns with the axle. Run a single continuous axle through as many support points as possible to avoid cantilevers; on long axles, add a bearing or frame support in the middle so the axle cannot bend under load. Leave a little backlash between meshing gears — too tight adds friction and wears parts faster. And always align the teeth before you turn anything: get into the habit of checking mesh first and testing rotation second, and you will avoid a lot of stripped teeth.

## Worm gears and clutch gears

The worm gear and worm wheel pairing is one of the smartest tricks in brick mechanics. It gives a big reduction ratio in a very small space, and it is self-locking: the worm can drive the wheel, but the wheel cannot drive the worm. That makes it the right choice for crane arms and gates. The clutch gear is your protection device — under excessive load it slips internally, saving the motor and the parts. It is a great comfort on winches and steering mechanisms. Both are small parts, but they are what make a model feel dependable.

## Common mistakes and how to avoid them

In practice, failures cluster in four places. First, gears installed the wrong way round: bevel gears and worms are directional, and reversing them jams the train. Second, axle spacing that is not accurate, so gears either fail to reach each other or jam too tightly — fix the spacing with standard frame pieces. Third, neglected friction: an axle passing through too many holes drags the drivetrain down, so use bearing rings wherever you can. Fourth, chain-style layouts with no tensioning, where slack causes the chain to skip teeth. When you hit one of these, isolate and test each stage of the chain and you will find the culprit quickly.

## Practice projects

Technique only sticks through building. Work through three small projects in order: start with a two-stage reduction gearbox to feel how the ratio changes, then build a turntable to practise bevel-gear direction changes, and finally take on a crane with a worm-gear lift that combines self-locking and clutch protection. After each build, write down the tooth counts and axle spacing you used, and you will gradually assemble your own library of drivetrain designs. When you get stuck, browse the Technic MOCs and build write-ups shared by other builders on BrickPlan (brickplan.cn) and study how they lay out their gearing — it often unlocks a new approach. Once gear transmission clicks for you, your mechanical MOCs genuinely come to life.
