# How to Build a LEGO Viaduct MOC: Multi-Level Interchange Guide

The short version first: three rules govern every viaduct build — align the piers, add a spine under the deck, and let ramps climb 1 brick for every 8 studs of run. For a two-level interchange, offset the levels so each keeps at least 6 bricks of clearance.

A brick viaduct is the element that gives a city diorama its depth: the roadway floats overhead, vehicles pass above and below, and one interchange turns a flat street scene into something three-dimensional. It is also where structural failures cluster — sagging decks, leaning piers and ramps that will not line up are the three problems builders hit most often. This guide breaks the logic down from pier layout and deck structure through ramps and multi-level interchanges.

## Pier Layout and Load-Bearing Design

The first rule of any viaduct is aligned piers and evenly distributed load. Mark the pier positions on your base layout first, and space them evenly at roughly 8 to 16 studs apart; span them any wider and the deck will inevitably sag. Build piers from 2x2 or thicker columns, or from technic truss columns, reinforce the bottom with a large baseplate, and add a plate on top as a cap beam so the deck's weight transfers evenly down into the piers. Piers on curves need to be closer together, because a curved deck carries load unevenly and is the most likely section to lean.

One thing to watch while building: piers must sit on solid ground, never directly on a thin baseplate, or the entire viaduct will warp along with the plate underneath it.

## Deck Assembly and Reinforcement

The deck is the roadway your vehicles drive on, so it has to be both flat and strong. The basic approach is a surface of large plates with technic beams or long bricks running lengthwise underneath as stiffeners — effectively a spine for the deck. A more advanced approach uses plates plus SNOT side building to create a full cross-section with curbs and a median. When you join deck sections, stagger the connectors above and below the seam so you never get a visible step.

For realism, leave a green strip down the middle of the deck, sandwiching green plates between two grey roadways — it looks better and adds longitudinal rigidity at the same time. Run bar pieces along both edges for crash barriers, and use flexible connectors to transition through curves.

## Ramps and Slopes

Ramps are the soul of an interchange and the hardest part to align. An on-ramp has to solve the height difference: climbing 1 brick per 8 studs of horizontal run gives a comfortable grade — steeper than that and vehicles cannot drive it, and a minifigure pushing a cart will struggle too. Build the ramp in sections, raising each one with slope pieces before laying the road surface, and stagger the joints between sections. For loop ramps, join curved plates with a turning radius of no less than 8 studs, otherwise the width difference between the inner and outer lane becomes obvious. Where a ramp merges into the main deck, allow a taper section so the two lanes blend smoothly; butting standard pieces straight together produces a visible offset.

Draw the interchange's plan view on paper before you design elevations — it saves a great deal of rework.

## Multi-Level Interchanges: From Flat to 3D

The key to a two- or three-level interchange is layering with an offset: shift the two decks half a pier spacing apart in plan so the piers above and below do not collide and sightlines can pass through. A common arrangement is ground-level road on level one, the main elevated line on level two, and a ramp flyover on level three, with at least 6 bricks of clearance between each layer. Connect levels with spiral or straight ramps — a spiral ramp eats space but delivers huge visual impact and works well as the focal point of a diorama.

Stability matters even more on multi-level structures: run lower piers all the way up to support the upper level, and add vertical connectors between the decks so the two levels do not sway independently.

## Fitting It Into a Scene

A viaduct ultimately has to belong to a city scene. Use the space underneath for planting, a bus stop or a parking area, turning the shadowed zone below the bridge into visible detail. Place a few small-scale cars and trucks on the deck and the sense of scale appears immediately. On parts budget, piers and decks consume standard plates and technic beams heavily: a single-level viaduct costs roughly ¥200 to ¥500, while a multi-level interchange can reach ¥600 to ¥1500 depending on scale. When photographing it, shoot from about 45 degrees above to show off the layering best.

Piers steady, deck flat, ramps smooth — that is all your brick viaduct needs to stand firm. ## FAQ

**Q: How should I lay out the piers on a viaduct?**
A: The first rule is aligned piers and evenly distributed load: space them evenly at 8 to 16 studs apart, since oversize spans will make the deck sag. Build piers from 2x2 or thicker columns or technic truss columns, reinforce the base with a large baseplate, and add a plate on top as a cap beam.

**Q: How do I reinforce the deck so it does not sag?**
A: Use large plates as the road surface and add technic beams or long bricks lengthwise underneath as stiffeners — a spine for the deck. Stagger connectors above and below the seam when joining sections to avoid steps, and a green median strip down the middle also adds longitudinal rigidity.

**Q: What ramp gradient works best?**
A: Climbing 1 brick per 8 studs of horizontal run is comfortable; steeper and vehicles cannot drive it and minifigures pushing carts struggle. Build the ramp in sections, raise each with slope pieces before laying the surface, and stagger the joints. Keep loop ramp turning radius at 8 studs or more.

**Q: How do I build a two-level interchange without the levels clashing?**
A: Offset the levels: shift the two decks half a pier spacing apart in plan so the piers do not conflict and sightlines pass through. Leave at least 6 bricks of clearance between layers, run lower piers all the way up to support the upper level, and add vertical connectors between decks.
