# MOC Display Base Guide: Plates, Terrain and Stands

Plenty of builders pour hours into a building and then set it down on a bare desk — and the whole thing instantly looks cheaper. A thoughtfully designed **MOC display base** lifts the model up, completes the story and can turn an ordinary build into an exhibition piece. This guide walks you from choosing a plate through shaping terrain to building a display stand, so your model gets a base that both holds up and shows off.

## 1. Choosing the Base Plate: Scale and Outline First

The first decision is the plate itself. There are three common routes: a standard baseplate (32×32 or 48×48, for example), which suits square street scenes and is the easiest way to lay pavement and raise buildings; irregular plates, which let you build non-rectangular outlines such as a curved lakeshore; and a custom frame, where you build the border yourself from beams and plates and fill the middle with parts, for a fully bespoke footprint. When picking, settle three things. **Viewing direction** — will the model sit against a wall or be seen from several angles? That decides whether you need to leave an open viewing face. **Scale** — building and base should be in proportion; the base should extend at least a few studs beyond the building's footprint on every side, or the build looks cramped. **Expandability** — leave a connection edge so you can later extend into a whole street or a large diorama. For a first **MOC display base**, start with a standard baseplate plus a border ring: simple and reliable.

## 2. Shaping Terrain: Elevation Is the Soul of a Base

A flat baseplate is a drawing; elevation makes it a scene. There are three moves. First, **layered height**: stack thick plates into hillsides, steps and raised platforms, keeping the height difference to two to four studs — steeper than that reads as abrupt. Second, **greenery transitions**: lay grass slopes in several shades of green, close the edges with slope pieces, then dot trees and small flowers so the terrain feels soft. Third, **roads and water**: build roadways from gray plates with white dash tiles for lane markings; make water from transparent blue or dark blue plates, with irregular pieces along the bank so the shoreline curves naturally instead of looking stiff. To bring terrain to life, hide a small light inside a hillside, or put an orange plate underneath to suggest lava — the detail level jumps immediately.

## 3. Walls and Enclosure: Give the Scene a Boundary

A good base tells the viewer where the story happens. Walls and enclosures do that fast. Add a stretch of sidewalk and a streetlight to a street building and the base has everyday life in it; ring a castle with a wall and drawbridge and the base becomes a fortification; give a farm a wooden fence and dirt ground and it feels grounded. You do not need to seal all four sides — leave one or two openings as entrances or viewing faces and the space reads better. Street furniture such as lamps, benches and mailboxes is the finishing touch: inexpensive, but it quickly turns an empty plate into a lived-in corner.

## 4. Display Stand Design: Make the Build Visible

When a model is large enough or detailed enough, it deserves its own display stand. The core ideas are **raising and focusing**: put the main building on a two-brick-high plinth so it sits at eye level, and the viewing experience changes completely. The plinth interior can be hollow, housing battery boxes and hiding wiring, or divided into small storage compartments. The side can take a nameplate tile with the build's title and builder — a nice ceremonial touch at exhibitions. For diorama-style **MOC display base** projects, split the stand into several removable modules: assembly, transport and maintenance all get easier, and lighting wires can run between modules for a cleaner result.

## 5. Common Mistakes in Base Building

A few frequent problems to watch for. **Loss of proportion**: too large a base with too much detail buries the building — the base serves the subject, it does not compete with it. **Unstable structure**: raised terrain sections need to interlock layer by layer and be pinned in place, or they will come apart when moved. **Too many colors**: keep the base more restrained than the building, use neutral colors for large ground areas and save accent colors for plants and street furniture. **No room left for later**: if you plan to add lighting, leave wiring channels while you are laying the base — cutting slots afterwards looks bad. Avoid these four and your base will be both solid and good-looking.
