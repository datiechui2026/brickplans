# Bakery MOC Blueprint Review: Facade, Interior, Difficulty

Once you have a few modular buildings on the shelf, you notice that bakery blueprints are a theme you never get bored of. The shopfront is compact, the interior is packed, and the exterior wall carries a story — drop the finished build into a street layout and the block instantly feels lived in. This review compares several mainstream options side by side, working through the facade, the interior and the parts list so you know what you are buying before you start ordering bricks.

## Choosing Between the Common Options

Modular bakery MOCs on the market fall roughly into three camps.

The **European corner bakery** is the classic set of proportions: a corner display window, a two-storey structure and a small attic window, usually somewhere between 800 and 1,300 bricks. It is the natural pick for the corner anchor slot of a street layout.

The **French croissant shop** takes the refined route — a narrow frontage with a tall signage band, where the signboard and awning are the main event. The palette leans cream yellow, white and coffee brown, and it photographs beautifully.

The **Japanese bakery** is more understated: a wood-tone door frame, a warm-lit picture window and a glass display case. The style is clean, the part count is lower, and it makes a good first solo build for a newer builder.

## Facade Details Make or Break It

The exterior wall is where a blueprint earns its keep. A good plan builds the **shop window** from large trans-clear panels framed with fine mullions so the glass reads as a floor-to-ceiling window, rather than fobbing you off with a solid wall. The **awning** usually combines slope pieces with curved plates; the red-and-white striped version gets the best result. Check that the plan marks the exact clutch position between the top of the awning and the upper edge of the window — this is the joint that most often ends up crooked. For the **signboard**, prefer a printed part or sticker solution over hand-lettering, and add a row of small letter tiles above it for a more considered shopfront. The **bread display case** inside the window is the finishing touch: if the plan shows a two-tier display rack plus the small bread models that sit on it, that is a clear point in its favour.

## Interior Scenes and Parts List

The interior is where blueprints separate from each other. A good bakery plan includes an **oven** — usually a dark grey brick body with a trans piece for the door — plus a **counter** and tiered **shelving**, stocked with printed or brick-built bread, croissant and baguette models. Put a minifigure behind the counter and the scene comes alive.

On the parts list, check two things. First, whether each part is listed with its colour ID and quantity, so you can sort bricks as you work through the steps. Second, whether **special parts** — curved windows, slope plates, printed elements — have documented alternatives, because unusual colours are hard to source as replacements later.

## Difficulty and Instructions

In difficulty terms, a bakery is usually **medium-easy**, considerably friendlier than a large town hall. Beginners should look at a 600–800 brick Japanese bakery, which can be finished over two or three evenings. More experienced builders can take on the 1,000-plus brick European corner version.

Instruction clarity matters too. Step-by-step drawings, per-step part highlighting and a finished render are the baseline for a reliable plan. If the blueprint only offers full-page overhead views, newer builders should steer clear. As for build technique, there is really one rule: **sort by colour first, align each layer before pressing down, and double-check the clutch at the exterior corners.**

## Price Compared With Boxed Sets

On cost, a loose-parts MOC blueprint typically runs from a few dozen to around 200 CNY, with the bricks bought separately. A bundled parts pack usually lands in the 200–400 CNY range — better value than a comparable boxed set on the same theme, and generally with denser facade detail and interior fittings. Boxed sets win on convenience and mature instructions, but the signboard and shop window usually fall short of a well-refined blueprint. If the budget is tight and detail is the priority, go with the blueprint route.
