# Brick Town Design: Planning a Micro-Town Layout

Almost every builder hits the same thought after finishing a single building or a single street: can these pieces be joined into a complete town? The answer is yes, but brick town design is far more involved than a standalone building — it has to juggle traffic, zoning, landscape and a sense of story all at once. This guide follows a simple order — plan first, zone second, decorate last — and covers the key points of laying out a micro-scale town.

## Step 1: Draw the Town Skeleton First

### The street grid is your town's first blueprint

Don't rush to stack buildings. Sketch the street grid on paper first. Two main roads plus several secondary roads is a reliable starting scheme:

- Reserve 8-12 studs of width for main roads, so two vehicles can pass side by side;
- 6 studs is enough for secondary roads, and pedestrian zones can be narrower still;
- Mark intersection corners early so corner buildings have somewhere to go — otherwise you will not be able to squeeze them in later.

### Use modular baseplates to keep the overall scale in check

The biggest risk with a micro-scale town is losing control halfway through. Build zone by zone on baseplates of a single standard size (32x32 studs, for example), with each baseplate carrying one block, then join them with side connectors. This makes teardown and revision easy and keeps the whole project within a size you can actually manage.

## Step 2: Zone by Function So the Town Feels Ordered

An ideal brick town design is usually made up of a few functional blocks. The table below lays them out:

| Zone | Suggested location | Typical buildings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commercial | Along the main roads | Café, bookstore, supermarket |
| Residential | Secondary roads and street corners | Terraced houses, apartments, small garden houses |
| Public | Town centre | Town hall, clock tower, square |
| Transport | Edge of town | Station, gas station, warehouse |

Zoning is not a rigid rule, but grouping buildings with similar functions together makes the town read as real and believable.

## Step 3: Greenery and Water Turn Empty Land into Landscape

Many builders treat greenery as a detail to patch in at the very end. In fact it should be planned at the same time as the buildings:

- Use 1x1 green plates to suggest lawns — this looks more natural than large solid baseplates;
- Build rivers in two layers: cut a channel into the baseplate, fill the water with transparent blue pieces, and use curved slopes where the river turns;
- Place trees and street lamps at consistent intervals along the street to create visual rhythm.

## Step 4: Minifigure Scenes Pump Story Into the Town

Minifigures are the soul of a town. Rather than lining up a row of figures standing still, stage scenes that are happening right now: a postman knocking on a door, a child feeding pigeons by the fountain, a café worker wiping down the sign at the entrance. On BrickPlan you can find plenty of ready-made minifigure pose references and accessory instructions — copying them saves a lot of effort.

## Scale Control: Three Practical Brakes

1. **Set a budget ceiling.** Before you start, fix your parts budget and your piece count. If you go over, cut a project.
2. **Build in phases.** Finish the core blocks first, then expand outward step by step, so the town never stalls half-built.
3. **Unify the style language.** Use one colour logic for window frames, roofs and signs. However large the town grows, it will still look coherent.

## Final Thoughts

The pleasure of brick town design is not in finishing it all at once — it is in watching empty baseplates slowly turn into streets with life in them. Don't be afraid to tear things down and rebuild.
