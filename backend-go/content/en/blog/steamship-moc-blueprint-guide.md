# Brick Steamship MOC Blueprints: Pick Plans, Check Parts, Build

Nautical themes never go out of style in brick building, but ship MOCs work nothing like buildings or vehicles when it comes to choosing instructions. The hull is one continuous, flowing line, and decks, masts and funnels stack up layer by layer — get the structural read wrong and the whole thing collapses. This guide covers how to choose steamship MOC blueprints, how to check the part list, and the key building points, all in one pass.

## First, decide which ship type you actually want

Two sets of "ship instructions" can be wildly different. Before you order anything, work out the final form you're after:

| Ship type | Characteristics | Difficulty | Typical example |
|------|------|----------|----------|
| Pirate sailing ship | Masts plus sails, lots of vertical lines | ★★☆ | Three-masted galleon |
| Steamship | Paddle wheel plus funnels, strong mechanical feel | ★★★☆ | Paddle steamer |
| Ocean liner | Wide hull, many stacked levels | ★★★★ | Modern cruise ship |
| Titanic | Extremely high detail density | ★★★★★ | Titanic |

The difficulty gap between steamship MOC blueprints comes down mostly to hull curves. Sailing ships use curved bricks to build up the bulwark, while liners need large flat plates to lay out a broad deck. A Titanic-class model can run to four or five thousand pieces, and the hull shell alone can eat half a day.

## Part list: verify before you order

The step that most often goes wrong on ship MOCs isn't structure — it's parts. Check in three passes:

1. Compare the total part count against the size of your own stock. If it's far beyond what you have on hand, plan for buying in several batches.
2. Look at the share of specialty parts in the list — are the curved bricks, bow wedge pieces and long cylindrical parts (for masts and funnels) easy to source?
3. Check for colors that are easy to confuse, such as dark grey versus light grey, or brown versus dark red, so you don't discover a mistake halfway through the build.

A reliable steamship MOC blueprint lists parts down to the exact part number with a quantity for each, and separates "required" parts from "substitutable" ones. If the only thing on offer is a render of the finished model with no part list, think twice before buying.

## Three key points for hull building

### Keel first

The hull is a structure that tapers as it rises. Build the keel and base plate first, lock in the width-to-length ratio of the whole ship, then stack upward layer by layer. Don't rush the superstructure — if the base isn't stable, everything above it becomes rework.

### Use SNOT for the curves

The bow and the bulwark curves are the soul of a ship. This is where SNOT (Studs Not On Top) side building, wedge plate transitions and curved brick edging come in. These techniques give the hull shell a smooth, natural line instead of a stack of boxy segments.

### Install the superstructure last

The bridge, funnels and lifeboats on deck are superstructure, so fit them after the main hull shell is complete. Working top to bottom and inside to outside cuts down a lot on the classic frustration of having to pull a deck apart again because the funnel went on too early.

## Display and photography tips

- Put the model on a dark blue baseplate to suggest the sea — it photographs far better than sitting straight on a table.
- For models like the Titanic, add a clear display case; it keeps off dust and protects against knocks.
- Use side-back lighting to bring out the hull lines and the mast silhouette. Dense, detailed builds benefit most.

## Where to start

From sailing ships to paddle steamers to the Titanic, every steamship MOC blueprint delivers a completely different building experience: the romance of a sailing ship, the machinery of a steamer, the sheer scale of a liner. Beginners should start with a small or mid-size sailing ship or paddle steamer, while experienced builders can take on a Titanic-class centrepiece.
