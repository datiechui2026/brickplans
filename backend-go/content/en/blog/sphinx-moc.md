# Sphinx MOC Tutorial: Reclining Proportions & Headdress

The short version: what makes or breaks a brick sphinx is the reclining pose. About 1,600 pieces gets you a complete sphinx 46 cm long and 20 cm tall, sand-colored bricks with a weathered treatment read convincingly as stone, and the whole build takes roughly six hours. It suits intermediate builders who want to try a landmark subject rather than another building.

## Step 1: Set the Reclining Proportions

A sphinx is a lion's body with a human head, and the body has to be long enough for the combination to look natural. Aim for a 46 cm body with a smooth transition from the outstretched front paws back to the hind legs, and keep the head to roughly 1/5 of the total mass.

Start with a 32x32 baseplate plus extension plates, then sketch the body outline on top: front paws forward, hind legs drawn in, tail flat against the ground. Total height lands around 20 cm, which keeps the center of gravity low enough that the model stands on its own without any internal support.

## Step 2: Lion Body and Legs

Build the body in sand-colored bricks from the tail forward. Curved bricks along the spine create the raised ridge of the back, while slopes on both flanks pinch in to suggest muscle. The front legs stretch out ahead of the chest, stacked from round bricks to form the leg shape, with slopes at the end for the paws. The hind legs are crouched, with hinge pieces used to bend the knees slightly.

Reinforce every joint where a limb meets the body. Run technic beams through the interior at those junctions; without them the legs will pop off as soon as someone picks the model up.

## Step 3: Head and Headdress

The head is the soul of the whole statue. Build the forehead and cheekbones from slopes, use 1x1 round plates for the eyes, a wedge brick for the nose, and recess the mouth to create that enigmatic smile.

The headdress is the striped nemes of the Egyptian pharaohs. Lay 1x1 bricks in alternating blue and yellow across the top of the head, and let the sides hang down to shoulder level. Connect the head to the body with a cylindrical neck, tilted slightly forward — that small angle is what gives the figure its sense of authority.

## Step 4: Weathering and Display

Sand-colored bricks already carry a desert feel on their own. Add a few patches of dark brown on the back and legs to suggest shadow and wind erosion. A small pyramid base or a palm tree scene alongside finishes the picture and makes the sphinx read as part of a place rather than a standalone figure.

The full build runs about six hours with a budget of roughly 130 CNY (about 18 USD). At 46 cm long, the finished piece is a focal point whether it sits on a study shelf or inside a diorama.

## FAQ

**Q: Where can I download the instructions for this brick sphinx MOC?**
A: Search for the sphinx on brickplan.cn. The PDF is around 25 CNY and includes a parts list plus step-by-step instructions. It stays downloadable permanently after purchase, so you can source the bricks yourself at your own pace.

**Q: Is this sphinx MOC hard to build?**
A: Medium. The reclining body proportions and the head sculpt both need careful adjustment. At 1,600 pieces and about six hours, it is more engaging than a pure architecture MOC. Some prior experience with animal or minifigure-scale MOCs helps.

**Q: How many parts does the sphinx MOC need?**
A: Around 1,600. Sand-colored bricks make up about 75 percent, the blue headdress stripes take roughly 80 bricks, and the dark brown weathering accents about 60. Because the palette is so concentrated, sourcing the parts is relatively cheap.

**Q: How should I pair the sphinx MOC with a pyramid MOC?**
A: The sphinx is 46 cm long, so a pyramid base with sides of about 40 cm works well, leaving a passage between the two. They share the same color family, and displayed together they form a complete Egyptian scene.

**Q: Why is the headdress blue and yellow?**
A: Traces of blue and yellow pigment survive on the nemes headdress of the real Great Sphinx. Blue stands for the sky and yellow for the desert, so blue-and-yellow striping is both historically grounded and instantly recognizable.
