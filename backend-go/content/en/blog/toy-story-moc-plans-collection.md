# Toy Story MOC Plans Review: Woody, Buzz and Andy's Room

Bottom line first: judge a Toy Story MOC by its parts list and its step-by-step diagrams. If the model is going to be handled and played with, pick a scene build that includes working mechanisms; if it is going on a shelf, pick a character build.

For plenty of builders, Pixar begins with Toy Story. Woody, Buzz Lightyear, Jessie, Rex — what do they look like when they are rebuilt out of brick? This review works through a collection of Toy Story MOC plans to see how builders recreate the crowded, colourful world of Andy's room, one brick at a time.

## Character builds: from Woody to Buzz Lightyear

What MOC builders care about most is capturing the character's personality, not just their silhouette. A good set of plans tends to obsess over small things: the curve of the cowboy hat, the exact spot where the sheriff badge sits on the chest.

Woody is the harder of the two leads to plan. His plaid shirt and cowboy hat demand a lot of slope bricks and curved elements, and the pattern has to read clearly at a distance without turning into visual noise. Buzz Lightyear leans on a different skill set: the space suit rewards clever use of transparent elements and white bricks, plus enough SNOT work to keep the chest and wings clean.

After reviewing several plan sets, one pattern stands out — how well the head and the expression are reproduced usually decides whether the whole model works. A body can be technically neat and still feel wrong if the face is off. That is also the part of the build where an author's experience shows most clearly, because the head is where the fewest parts have to do the most work.

## Scenes and mechanisms: Andy's room is more than a backdrop

Not every plan goes after a single character. A lot of them put the emphasis on the setting instead. Andy's bed, the toy box and the three-eyed alien's spaceship can be combined into a complete Toy Story world, with the characters as residents rather than stand-alone display pieces.

The genuinely fun part is the mechanisms. There is a crane arm that rises when you pull a string, and a rocket that spins when you press a button. Small interactive details like these are what turn a static model into something that moves, and they are exactly what keeps younger builders coming back to a set.

Within a Toy Story MOC plans collection, scene builds usually ask for more patience than single-character builds — more parts, more sub-assemblies, more testing before everything lines up. The finished result tends to be more impressive too, and a scene gives you somewhere to place Woody and Buzz instead of leaving them on a bare baseplate.

## How to judge plan quality

When you are choosing plans, three checks cover most of the risk:

- **Is the parts list complete?** A missing or vague list means a build that stalls halfway through.
- **Are the step diagrams clear?** Look for consistent angles and readable part callouts, not just one render per stage.
- **Are alternate parts noted?** Good plans flag substitutions for rare or expensive elements.

Beginners should start with a character build that uses fewer parts — a few hundred pieces is a comfortable first project. Experienced builders can move on to scene projects in the thousand-plus piece range. ## FAQ

**Q: Which Toy Story MOC should a first-time builder choose?**

A: Start with a character build that uses fewer parts, such as a stand-alone Woody or Buzz Lightyear. A few hundred pieces is enough to finish, and once you are comfortable you can move up to scene projects in the thousand-plus piece range.

**Q: How do you judge the quality of Toy Story MOC plans?**

A: Three things matter: whether the parts list is complete, whether the step diagrams are clear, and whether alternate parts are noted. Miss any one of them and you are likely to stop halfway through the build.

**Q: Which is harder to build, Woody or Buzz Lightyear?**

A: Woody relies on a lot of slope bricks and curved elements for the plaid shirt and cowboy hat. Buzz Lightyear tests your use of transparent elements and white bricks. In both cases, reproducing the head and expression is what decides the model.

**Q: What makes scene MOCs like Andy's room fun?**

A: The mechanisms are the highlight: pull a string and the crane arm rises, press a button and the rocket turns. Andy's bed, the toy box and the alien spaceship also combine into a complete world, which makes the finished model more striking.
