# Eagle MOC Review: 980-Part Spread-Wing Raptor Build

The short version: this spread-wing eagle MOC looks commanding on the shelf, uses roughly 980 parts, and measures about 38 cm across the wings when finished. Overall accuracy to the real bird sits around 85%. The instructions are free to download on brickplan.cn, sourcing the loose parts costs about 260 CNY, and my timed build came in at roughly 4.5 hours. For a bird-of-prey subject, that is a strong value combination.

## 1. Exterior Accuracy

The spread-wing pose is the headline feature. Each wing builds its flight feathers layer by layer from curved elements and wedge plates, stacking toward the tip so the primaries read as separate feathers rather than a flat slab. The wingtips lift slightly, which stops the silhouette from looking stiff. Combined with the hooked beak and the round yellow eyes, the head captures the aggressive raptor expression — I would put likeness at about 85%.

The back-to-tail colour transition runs from dark brown into lighter tan, and the feather gradient reads naturally from above, which is the angle most people will display it from. In photos with a plain backdrop, the bird has real presence. The head is the one area where the model simplifies: at this scale the brow ridge and cheek shaping are suggested rather than fully modelled, which is where most of the missing 15% of accuracy goes.

## 2. Structure Design

The 38 cm wingspan is held by an internal skeleton of technic cross axles, connected to the torso through hinge elements. That hinge is what lets you change the wing angle: you can set the bird in a full glide, a mid-flap, or a folded-back perch stance without dismantling anything.

The tail feathers fan out in a wide arc and act as a counterweight. Together with the thick, heavy-set legs and feet, the stance is stable — I deliberately loaded one wing and the model did not tip sideways. If you plan to place it on a narrow shelf, the load path through the axles means the weight stays over the feet rather than cantilevering over the edge.

## 3. Build Experience

Using the PDF instructions from brickplan.cn, my build ran about 4.5 hours start to finish, split into 9 stages. The wings and their feather layers are concentrated in stages 4 through 6, and that is where the difficulty lives. Individual steps run between 15 and 30 parts, so nothing is overwhelming on its own; the challenge is cumulative.

Instruction callouts are clear throughout. The one thing to watch is feather layer alignment: each layer needs to seat on its specific clip point, and if you are off by one stud the next layer will not sit flush. A pair of tweezers helps considerably in the tight inner rows where fingers cannot reach. Sorting browns and tans into separate trays before you start also saves time, since the colour gradient work happens in long runs of similar-looking parts.

## 4. Value for Money

The instructions are free. Sourcing the parts costs about 260 CNY, and the palette is friendly — browns and greys are among the most common elements in any collection, so builders with a decent stock of bulk parts can push the cost under 200 CNY. Compared with a finished retail set of the same size, that works out roughly 40% cheaper.

Given the spread-wing pose and the adjustable wings, the value is hard to argue with. It suits raptor fans and intermediate builders who want a display piece that is not just a static sculpture.

All in, this eagle MOC balances silhouette and poseability well. Free instructions, controlled part cost, and a build that stays interesting through the feather stages make it one of the better entry-to-intermediate bird projects out there.
