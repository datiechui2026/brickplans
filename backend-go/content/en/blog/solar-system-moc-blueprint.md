# Solar System MOC Blueprint: Build All 8 Planets

Bottom line first: for a solar system MOC blueprint, pick a design built around an **orbit base with separate planets**. This approach uses about 1,500 pieces on a budget of roughly 400 RMB, sits on a 30 cm base, and the orbit rings actually rotate — the best value in its class, and something a builder aged 8 or over can finish in one sitting.

People ask constantly how to judge a solar system blueprint, and it really comes down to three things: how faithfully the planet sizes are represented, how rigid the orbit structure is, and how much there is to actually play with. The walkthrough below follows the eight-planet design step by step, so you can see where those three points matter in practice.

**Step 1: Sort the parts and check the instructions.** Split the 1,500 pieces into bins by colour: greys for the orbit frame, the bright sphere parts for the planets. Then check the inventory against the manual before you build anything. Every step lists its part numbers, so the check takes about 10 minutes. Don't skip it — a missing piece found halfway up the model costs far more time than a tray-by-tray count at the start. Grey structural pieces and grey detail pieces look nearly identical in a pile, so give them separate compartments rather than one big tray.

**Step 2: Build the Sun and the orbit base.** The base is 30 cm across and consists of three concentric rings, with the outermost ring carrying Neptune's orbit. The Sun is a large yellow sphere surrounded by flame elements, about 6 cm in diameter, and it carries roughly a third of the model's visual weight. Test that the central axle turns smoothly before you build anything on top — fixing a stiff axle later means tearing the rings apart again. Building the rings first also means any stiffness shows up while the base is still light and easy to handle.

**Step 3: Build the eight planets to scale.** The blueprint keeps planet diameters between 1 cm and 2.5 cm: Mercury smallest at about 1 cm, Jupiter largest at about 2.5 cm, a compression of roughly 1:3 billion. Each planet is a sphere plus colour-band parts. Jupiter and Saturn need extra stripe detailing, and Saturn's ring element is easy to forget until the planet is already mounted on its arm. Working from Mercury outward keeps the size progression obvious; an error is much harder to spot when the finished spheres are rolling around loose in a tray.

**Step 4: Attach the planet arms and orbits.** Each planet connects to its own orbit ring through a transparent arm, and the arms get longer from the innermost ring outward. Keep the gaps between neighbouring rings even at about 1.5 cm — uneven spacing is the usual reason the rotation jams. With everything mounted, push the outermost ring gently and all eight planets travel together. The transparent arms are the one place where a slightly loose connection is fine: they hold position, they don't carry load.

**Step 5: Details, finishing and display.** Add the planet name plates to the base in order from Mercury out to Neptune, then optionally fit a small LED into the Sun (about 15 RMB), which looks striking once the room lights are off. Total build time runs about 3 hours. Finished, the model measures 30 cm across and about 35 cm tall — small enough for a desk, and very photogenic. The name plates do double duty: they finish the base and they save guests from asking which dot is which.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose a solar system MOC blueprint?**
A: Prioritise blueprints with an orbit frame, so the planets move and the model displays well. This 1,500-piece, 400 RMB option is the most balanced on scale accuracy and structural rigidity at its price point, and it suits beginners.

**Q: Is a solar system brick model worth buying?**
A: Yes. About 1,500 pieces gets you a complete eight-planet model on a 30 cm base, and rotating orbits give it both teaching value and display value. Around 400 RMB counts as mid-to-upper value for an MOC, and kids genuinely enjoy it.

**Q: Are the planet sizes accurate?**
A: Bricks can't do true scale — at real scale Jupiter would swallow 1,300 Earths. The blueprint uses a compressed scale, from Mercury at 1 cm up to Jupiter at 2.5 cm, so the size differences still read clearly. That's enough for teaching.

**Q: What if the orbits won't turn?**
A: First check that the ring gaps are even, which means about 1.5 cm, then check that no stray parts are jammed into the axle. The instructions include a dedicated smoothness test at step 3; follow it and jamming is rare.

**Q: Can I build it with my child?**
A: Yes — ages 8 and up is the sensible range. Parents take the base and the orbits, kids build the planet spheres, and the split gets you about 3 hours of teamwork plus a natural run through the eight planets while you go.
