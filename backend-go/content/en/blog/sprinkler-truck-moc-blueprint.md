# Sprinkler Truck MOC: Tank, Spray Arm and Chassis Guide

Short version first: a sprinkler truck wins on recognizability. Three things sell it — a round water tank, a rear spray arm, and the sanitation-green livery. The part count leans on plates, cylindrical bricks and curved slopes, and the difficulty sits comfortably at moderate.

## Why the Sanitation Theme Deserves a Spot in Your City

Among city-vehicle MOCs, the sprinkler truck is one of the most instantly readable. The fat rounded tank, the boom hanging off the tail, the classic sanitation green — park one on a street layout and the whole city scene suddenly feels lived in. Next to the urgency of fire engines and police cars, a brick sprinkler truck carries an easy, everyday street-corner mood. It is also a good step up for builders moving from simple cars into vehicle MOCs: the parts are mostly plates, cylindrical bricks and curved slopes, and the difficulty stays moderate.

## Water Tank and Body: The Round Tank Is the Whole Ballgame

The round tank is the single most important visual element of the model. There are two usual approaches. The first is to build the barrel from cylindrical bricks — fast, efficient, and hard to get wrong. The second is to stack curved slopes layer by layer to close the circle, which gives you a rounder silhouette and total freedom over dimensions, but demands a much deeper parts bin.

When judging a blueprint, watch the tank-to-chassis ratio above all. The tank should run roughly one third to one half of the truck's overall length; push it taller and the model immediately looks top-heavy. On top of the barrel, remember to add a manhole hatch, and run level markings down both sides (thin bar pieces do the job) — those two details fill the model out instantly.

## Spray Arm and Rear Mechanism: Making the Build Feel Alive

The rear assembly is what separates a sprinkler truck from an ordinary cargo truck. A complete tail system includes a horizontal spray boom (technic beams plus nozzle pieces work well), wash nozzles on both sides, and optionally a fog cannon mounted on the rear bumper. When choosing a sprinkler truck MOC blueprint, check whether it actually specifies a folding and unfolding structure for the boom. Using hinge pieces or a turntable to make the spray arm movable adds a great deal of play value. The plumbing route from the tank back to the boom — hose or long bar pieces — also needs a clear path in the instructions, otherwise the finished model looks hollow.

## Color and Livery Suggestions

The classic sprinkler palette is sanitation green — a dark green plus a light green — paired with a white tank and silver wheels for a clean, fresh look. If you want the truck to pop on a street layout, try an orange tank with white striping, or follow municipal liveries and add reflective bands. Stickers or printed pieces raise the finish level quickly: lettering along the cab sides and a ring stripe around the tank are both easy to pull off. For tires, go wide with construction or truck tires and pair them with a tandem rear axle — the stance gets noticeably more imposing.

## Blueprint Buying: Pitfalls to Avoid

There are three traps when shopping for a sprinkler truck MOC blueprint. First, an incomplete tank parts list — cylindrical bricks and curved slopes are very hard to substitute once you are short. Second, a spray arm that is purely decorative and stuck on with no moving structure, which kills playability. Third, a lazy chassis borrowed from a generic truck, leaving the wheelbase and tank proportions out of balance.

Favor blueprints that include a numbered parts list and state clearly whether they are compatible with the Gobricks or LEGO system. Build in the order chassis, then cab, then water tank, then rear mechanism, and unify the colors at the very end — the finished model comes out much cleaner that way.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose a brick sprinkler truck MOC blueprint?**

A: Check three things first: whether the tank is a round barrel rather than a square box, whether the spray arm actually moves, and whether the livery follows classic sanitation green. Those three decide whether the finished model reads as a sprinkler truck at all. The whole build runs about 3 to 4 hours.

**Q: How do I build the round water tank?**

A: Enclose the barrel with cylindrical bricks or curved pieces. Build the internal frame first so the circle stays true, then lay curved pieces around the ring to close the surface. Add a filler cap piece on top and retaining straps on both sides, and the rounded look falls into place.

**Q: How do I make the spray arm movable?**

A: Connect the rear spray boom to the body with hinge pieces so it can be lowered and folded back up. Line up the nozzles using thin bar pieces with small nozzle elements, then run a feed pipe back to the tank — both the motion and the detail land where they should.

**Q: How should I decide the color scheme?**

A: Classic sanitation green is the safest bet. Block the tank and body in green and white, use dark gray for the chassis and wheels, and add a single yellow reflective stripe to the barrel. Keeping the palette to three colors or fewer always looks best.
