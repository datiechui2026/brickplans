# Brick Aircraft Carrier MOC: A Complete Beginner's Guide

## Introduction: Why Carrier MOCs Hook Builders

Aircraft carriers sit at the centre of modern naval power, and everything about them draws military fans and brick builders alike: the massive hull, the flight deck packed with aircraft, the sheer reach of a carrier strike group. Recreating one as a brick MOC (My Own Creation) combines imagination, engineering and patience into a single project.

Whether you want the Liaoning, a Ford-class ship, a World War II classic or a futuristic concept vessel, carrier MOC instructions let these steel giants sail into your own home. This guide walks through every stage, from choosing a scale to sourcing parts, and it is written for both first-time builders and seasoned MOC makers.

## What Is an Aircraft Carrier MOC Plan?

A carrier MOC plan is a building blueprint designed by a hobbyist rather than a toy company. Unlike official sets, these designs usually come with a part list, step-by-step instructions and 3D renders. A high-quality carrier plan typically includes:

- **A complete part list** with part numbers, colours and quantities
- **Step-by-step building diagrams** taking you from keel to flight deck
- **Sticker or printed part designs** for deck markings and island details
- **Scale and size references** so you know how big the finished model will be

## How to Choose the Right Carrier MOC

### 1. Choose by Scale

Common carrier MOC scales include:

- **1:2000 micro scale**: compact, desktop-friendly, usually 200-500 parts
- **1:700 small scale**: moderate detail, roughly 1,000-3,000 parts, ideal for beginners through intermediate builders
- **1:400 mid scale**: rich detail, roughly 3,000-6,000 parts, suited to builders with some experience
- **1:200 large scale**: a monster project that can exceed 10,000 parts and demands serious patience and budget

New builders should start at 1:700 or 1:400. You still get satisfying detail without a part count that makes you give up before the hull is finished.

### 2. Choose by Ship Class

The most widely available carrier plans cover these classic classes:

- **Ford class (CVN-78)**: the most modern design, with a clean, almost sci-fi island
- **Nimitz class (CVN-68)**: the classic American carrier, well documented with mature, reliable plans
- **Liaoning / Shandong**: ski-jump flight decks, popular with Chinese carrier enthusiasts
- **Admiral Kuznetsov**: the Russian carrier, with a distinctly different look
- **WWII carriers such as Enterprise and Akagi**: the first choice for history fans

## Core Building Techniques for Carrier MOCs

### Part Selection Strategy

A carrier MOC succeeds or fails largely on part choices. Key advice:

**1. Use dark bricks for the basic structure**

Carrier hulls are usually built in dark grey, black or dark blue. Dark parts match real carrier paint schemes, they hide scratches and wear far better, and they are often cheaper than light-coloured parts.

**2. Use plates and tiles for the deck**

A flight deck is the face of the model. Use large plates such as 2x4 and 2x6, plus tiles, to get a flat, smooth surface. Runway lines and landing-zone markings can be done with stickers or tiles in specific colours.

**3. Use slopes and tiles for the island**

The island is the command centre and the visual focal point. Slopes and tiles let you shape modern radar arrays and bridge structures. LED lighting kits can also be tucked into the island to simulate navigation lights and radar signals.

**4. Do not skip the aircraft**

A complete carrier MOC needs its air wing. Small fighters such as the F-35C, F/A-18E and J-15 can each be built from 30-80 parts. You can also find dedicated aircraft MOC plans on BrickPlans (brickplan.cn) that pair perfectly with your carrier.

### Structural Strength and Stability

Carrier MOCs are large, so structural strength matters:

- **Internal frame**: build a skeleton from Technic bricks and beams to carry the load
- **Modular design**: split the ship into bow, forward deck, island, stern and underwater sections, build each separately, then join them for easier transport and display
- **Reinforced deck**: support the deck from below with plenty of column bricks (1x1 brick with stud on the side, for example) to stop it sagging

### Deck Aircraft Layout

Real carriers park and launch aircraft in several patterns. For a MOC, you can reference these layouts:

- **Bow launch area**: 2-4 fighters positioned for takeoff
- **Parking area ahead of the island**: densely packed aircraft on standby
- **Angled landing area**: kept clear to simulate recovery operations
- **Near the stern elevator**: aircraft that have just come up from the hangar

A sensible layout boosts visual realism and makes the display far more interesting.

## Common Problems and Fixes

### Problem 1: Not enough parts

This is the most common issue. Before you start, check your inventory against the plan's part list, then buy the missing pieces in one order through a platform like BrickPlans rather than placing several small orders and paying extra shipping each time.

### Problem 2: You spot an error in the instructions

Good MOC plans are usually tested several times, but small mistakes still slip through. Read the whole plan before building, especially the complex structural sections. If you find a genuine error, report it in the community; the designer will normally correct it quickly.

### Problem 3: The finished model is bigger than expected

Always confirm the finished dimensions listed on the plan before you start. If you have a fixed display space, measure it first and then choose a plan. A 1:700 carrier is usually 40-50 cm long, while a 1:400 ship runs around 70-100 cm.
