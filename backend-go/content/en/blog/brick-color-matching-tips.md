# Brick Color Matching: 60-30-10 and Other MOC Color Rules

Plenty of builders pour their time into structure and mechanisms, then treat color as an afterthought. In practice, color is what decides the first impression of a model. The same city street model can look clean and expensive with a controlled palette, or cheap and noisy with a random one. This article covers the underlying logic of brick color matching plus the practical methods you can apply on your next build.

## Understand the Color Wheel First

The first step in brick color matching is understanding how colors relate on the wheel. Put simply, the wheel has three primary colors: red, yellow and blue. Mix them in pairs and you get secondary colors such as orange, green and purple. Colors sitting next to each other are analogous; colors sitting opposite each other are complementary. Once you know these relationships, you stop grabbing parts on instinct alone. If you want a roof and wall to sit harmoniously together, pick adjacent colors on the wheel such as brown and beige. If you want visual impact, reach for complementary pairs like red and green, or orange and blue.

## The 60-30-10 Rule Keeps the Hierarchy Clear

Many builders try to use every color they like on the first attempt, and the result looks busy and muddled. A useful guideline borrowed from interior design is the 60-30-10 rule: the main color takes 60%, the secondary color 30%, and the accent color 10%. When building an MOC, set the dominant tone first — say a modern building that is mostly gray — then add 30% secondary color such as wood-toned doors and window frames, and finish with 10% of small-area accents like a red sign or some greenery to lift the whole thing. Hold to that ratio and the model immediately reads as designed rather than assembled.

## Three Classic Color Schemes

- **Monochrome**: build with different shades of a single color, for example layering dark gray, medium gray and light gray. It suits sci-fi bases and modern architecture — crisp and clean without feeling flat.
- **Analogous**: choose two or three neighboring colors on the wheel, such as blue, blue-green and green for an ocean theme, or yellow, orange and red for a desert or autumn scene. The overall result stays warm and unified.
- **Complementary**: pair two opposite colors on the wheel, such as orange with blue, or purple with yellow. This suits mecha and painted-livery models and delivers strong visual impact, but keep the accent area under control.

## Color in Practice: Common Subjects

For city street buildings, lean on earth tones plus white, and transition the roof through several shades of brown — that gives depth without becoming busy. Military subjects mostly use olive green, sand and black-gray, supported by a small amount of red markings for a steady, recognizable look. Models aimed at children work well with high-saturation pure colors, but don't exceed three main colors at once. A mistake beginners often make is mixing transparent parts with large areas of pure white, which actually kills the sense of translucency. Test a color combination on a small area first, then decide whether to roll it out across the whole build.

## Use Tools and Learn From the Community

If you can't settle on a palette, generate one first with the official LEGO color chart or an online palette tool, then match it against the parts you actually have in your inventory. Browsing MOC communities and studying how experienced builders handle color is also the fastest way to improve your brick color matching. On [BrickPlans](https://brickplan.cn) you can find plenty of high-quality MOC instructions — before downloading, look at which colors the designer used and how they transition between them. That usually teaches you more than ten builds done in isolation.

## Final Thoughts

There is no absolute right or wrong in color matching, but knowing the basic rules of brick color matching puts your model a step ahead from the start. Begin with the 60-30-10 rule today: rebuild an old model three times using each of the three schemes above, and you will see the power of color for yourself. Here's to building models with real depth and quality.
