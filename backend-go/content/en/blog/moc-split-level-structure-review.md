# Split-Level MOC Review: Mezzanines and Duplex Floors

Duplex, loft, mezzanine — these words grab attention in a brick street scene faster than almost anything else. Split-level simply means putting floor plates at different heights inside one building, so the interior gains a sense of going up and down. Compared with a standard flat layout, it adds far more storytelling. This review draws on several **brick MOC** plans that use split levels, and lays out both the techniques that work and the places where builds fall apart.

## 1. The Three Basic Forms of a Split Level

### Half-Level Offset

The classic approach: two adjacent rooms sit half a storey apart and are connected by a short stair. It is the most parts-efficient form and the easiest to make look convincing, which makes it a good fit for pairing a living room with a dining room, or a study with a bedroom.

### Mezzanine

A mezzanine cantilevers a half-height platform out of a tall space — living room below, bedroom or reading nook above. It delivers the strongest visual impact, but it also places the highest demands on floor-plate support.

### Double-Height Atrium

Here you break both floor plates in the middle and leave an open atrium, then hang a pendant lamp or plant an indoor tree in it. This is the most impressive option to display and also the most demanding structurally.

## 2. Floor Cantilevers: The Core Difficulty of Split Levels

The thing a split level fears most is a floor plate that feels soft and wobbles. In testing, a cantilevered floor absolutely needs a hidden beam: run a Technic beam or 1x2 bricks through the interior of the plate and land both ends on load-bearing walls. That is far more stable than laying bricks flat and hoping. When a mezzanine cantilever extends more than 4 studs, add a diagonal brace or a column under the protruding end rather than counting on friction between bricks to hold it.

### Floor Thickness and Detail

Split-level plates usually end up thicker than a normal storey. One trick is to run a ring of bricks along the underside as a beam line — it hides the structure and adds detail at the same time. In testing, a lot of builders skip this, and the bare underside of the plate immediately reads as rough construction.

## 3. Stairs and Circulation

A split level needs stairs to tie the different heights together, and this is where mistakes happen most often. A half-level offset only needs 3 to 4 small steps; a mezzanine needs a straight run or a spiral staircase. The key is aligning the bottom of the stair with the opening in the floor plate — make the opening a little too large rather than letting the stair crash into the ceiling. Good plans usually mark this step explicitly, so follow the placement and you will not go wrong.

## 4. Daylight and Openness

A split-level space dreads becoming a sealed box. The tested recommendation is to open large windows or a skylight in the void area so light travels from top to bottom; edge the mezzanine plate with glass pieces or an open railing rather than a solid wall, and the sense of openness arrives immediately. If you build a full double-height atrium, add a transparent skylight piece at the top and the whole layering of the space comes through in one go.

## 5. Overall Verdict and Advice

Do a split level well and a street scene instantly takes on the air of an architect's project. Do it badly and the floor plate wobbles at best, or the stair ends up misaligned and the build cannot be finished at worst. For newcomers the advice is straightforward: build a half-level offset first to get the feel, then take on a mezzanine and an atrium. Studying finished plans is the fastest route — search "duplex", "mezzanine" and "split level" on BrickPlans (brickplan.cn) to find matching designs, and learning the skeleton from a plan is much quicker than trial and error on your own. Remember the four rules — hidden beams inside floor plates, braces under cantilevers, stair openings that line up, and open edges — and your split-level build will not fall apart.
