# MOC City Diorama Planning: From Blank Baseplate to Blocks

## Think It Through Before You Build: Why City Layouts Stall Out

Most abandoned city dioramas don't fail because the builder ran out of bricks — they fail because there was **no plan**. A 48×48 baseplate looks enormous until you start placing roads, buildings and plazas, and suddenly it's either bursting at the seams or weirdly empty. The first step in MOC city planning isn't stacking walls; it's deciding on paper how many residents the city holds, what kinds of businesses it runs, and which district each one belongs in. Treating the city as a single coordinated project saves you ten times the build hours and replacement-brick spending compared with improvising as you go.

## Baseplate Size and Scale: Lock Down the Foundation First

The thing that ruins a city diorama fastest is mixed scale. My recommendation is to **use minifigure scale (roughly 1:42) across the entire layout**, so buildings and figures look right standing next to each other. For baseplates, beginners should start with 32×32 or 48×48. If you're serious about a large diorama, join several 32×32 baseplates into a 64×64 footprint or bigger — and remember to lock each plate to its neighbour with baseplate connectors (technic holed plates plus axles) so the whole thing doesn't split apart when you move it. During planning, sketch the functional zones directly onto the baseplate: **city centre, residential area, shopping street, industrial corner, park and green space**. Mark out how much area each one gets before you place a single brick.

## Roads and Blocks: What Makes a Diorama Feel Alive

A good road system instantly gives a diorama that "city" feeling. Roads are usually **2 modules wide (16 studs)**, giving two-way traffic plus a sidewalk on each side; main arteries can go 3 modules with a planted median down the centre. For paving, use dark grey plates and tiles for the roadway and light grey or white plates and tiles for sidewalks, then run 1×1 slope bricks along the boundary as curbs — the depth reads immediately. When you lay out roads, leave room for **intersections and roundabouts**; they're the hardest things to fit and the easiest places to make the layout shine. Give each block a 1–2 stud "alley" gap and fill it with trash cans, streetlamps and minifigures, and the detail density jumps on its own.

## Modular Buildings: One Building at a Time, Not One Neighbourhood at a Time

The biggest trap in a city diorama is trying to build an entire district in one go. The right approach is **modularity**: make every building a self-contained, removable unit (say on an 8×8 or 8×16 stud footprint), finish it, then set it on the diorama. This pays off three ways. First, each individual building reaches a higher level of finish, which keeps motivation up. Second, you can rearrange buildings and swap storefronts whenever you like. Third, expanding later or rebuilding one structure doesn't mean tearing up the whole block. Between floors, build **separable floor plates** — hold each level with a few technic pins or studs — so you can open the interior to furnish it, and you avoid the classic "add another storey and it collapses" headache.

## Lighting, Greenery and Details: From Good-Looking to Rewatchable

A city diorama lives or dies on detail. **Lighting** is the fastest upgrade available: hide 2×2 light plates or fibre-optic lamps inside streetlamps, shop signs and windowsills, run them all off a single battery box, and the whole city switches on at night. For **greenery**, build street trees from brown stems topped with green canopy pieces, add shrubs and flower clusters in the park, and surface lawns with 1×1 plates or studs instead of one big green plate — the texture is completely different. Then layer in billboards, fire hydrants, mailboxes and traffic signals. Once the density is there, the diorama stays interesting to look at. Work in the order of **big structure first, small details last** — don't get stuck perfecting a single tree at the start.

## A Complete Step-by-Step Planning Workflow

Here's a sequence you can follow as written. Step one: fix your baseplate size and scale, and sketch the functional zones. Step two: lay the road grid and define main and secondary arteries. Step three: build each building as a module and number them. Step four: place the buildings on the diorama and adjust spacing and orientation. Step five: add greenery, lighting and small scene props across the whole layout. Step six: photograph the whole thing to check it, and fill in any areas that read as too empty. Spread the project over two or three weeks and move it forward a little each day — that works far better against burnout than one marathon weekend.

## Summary

The secret to MOC city diorama planning comes down to three moves: **zone first, modularise second, detail last**. Manage the baseplate, roads and blocks like a project, build one building at a time, and the city will stand up solidly.
