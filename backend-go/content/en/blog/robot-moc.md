# Brick Robot MOC Tutorial: Articulated Joints and Gear Linkage

Short version first: a robot MOC lives or dies on three things — poseable joints, gear linkage, and center of gravity. Use ball joints at the shoulders and hips, bury a gearbox inside the shoulder, and widen the feet so it stops nose-diving.

Plenty of builders finish a brick robot MOC that looks great and then discover it stands stiffly, with arms that can only stick straight up. The mechanical feel is missing. This guide covers the three core problems in order: **how to design the moving joints, how to lay out the gear linkage, and how to pick the right ball joint parts** — so you end up with a robot that can actually hold a decent pose.

## Start With the Torso Skeleton: Where Joints Begin

The skeleton decides how far the joints can travel. Start with the **spine**: chain a few sets of cross axles and axle connectors into a core that can pitch forward and back, then wrap the outside in bricks and plates to lock the shape. A torso that is too rigid makes every limb look stiff no matter how much articulation you add. Leave 3 to 5 degrees of sway and the stance immediately reads as more natural.

The shoulder is the soul of the build. Use a **ball joint** here — it rotates through multiple angles and carries load, which makes it the best choice for robot shoulders and hips. Two things to check when buying: first, ball joints come in large ball (roughly a 3.2 mm axle hole) and small ball versions; the torso is more stable on the large ones. Second, the clutch has to be tight — a sloppy ball joint collapses the moment you touch the model.

## Gear Linkage: Making the Arms Come Alive

Joints alone are not enough. A robot feels alive when it can *move*, and that is where **gear linkage** comes in. The simplest solution is a **gearbox** built inside the shoulder: place two gears in parallel behind the shoulder joint, one as the power input and one to lift the arm, then change the gear ratio to control how fast and how forcefully the arm swings.

There are three traps to avoid in a gearbox design. First, **leave enough meshing clearance** — if the center distance between two gears is wrong, you get binding or slipping. Second, a small gear as the driver will not deliver enough torque, so a heavy arm will sag backwards; add a **ratchet lock** so the arm holds its position. Third, always build a service hatch into the gearbox. Get the ratio wrong without one and you will be tearing apart half the torso to fix it.

## Leg Drive and Center of Gravity

The lower body decides whether the robot can stand. A **twin-gear differential** layout works well: each leg uses a gear-and-linkage pair to swing up and down, alternating left and right to suggest a walking rhythm. A display-only MOC does not need real locomotion, but it does need a stable stance. Use ball joints at the hips, and limit the knees to roughly 90 degrees with a **hinge plus a stop plate** so the joint cannot over-bend and pop out.

Center of gravity is the point beginners miss most. A forward-leaning torso plus a big backpack will make the robot pitch onto its face. Widen the footprint at the feet and hide counterweight bricks in a compartment at the back. Give the foot at least twice the ground contact area of the lower leg — that is what keeps the model from wobbling every time you adjust a pose.

## Parts Selection and Common Pitfalls

- **Core joints:** Favor high-precision ball joints and damped hinges from LEGO or Gobricks. Off-brand parts have loose tolerances, so joint tension ends up inconsistent from one limb to the next.
- **Gears:** The 40-tooth, 20-tooth and 12-tooth gears cover most needs — keep several sets on hand. Choose gear axles with a raised stop to prevent slipping.
- **Pitfalls:** Joints installed backwards, gear faces pointing the wrong way (an outward-facing gear will rub against the shell), and ball joints forced in so tight they crack. Test-fit every joint on a bare axle before assembly.
