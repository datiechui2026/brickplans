# LEGO Harry Potter MOC Blueprint Review: Hogwarts & Diagon Alley

After two years of building Harry Potter designs as both a fan of the books and a MOC builder, I have worked through enough blueprints to compare them properly, from full Hogwarts castles down to single Diagon Alley shopfronts. This review sorts the common **LEGO Harry Potter MOC blueprints** by theme and looks at the three things that actually decide whether a build is worth your money: how faithful it is, how hard it is, and what it costs you in parts.

## Hogwarts Castle Blueprints: Trade-offs of a Big Build

The castle is the theme nobody can avoid, and the blueprints split into two camps. The first goes after the silhouette: main tower, clock tower and Great Hall topped with the signature spires, built from white brick walls and slope pieces. Put one of these in a living room and it dominates the space.

The second camp goes inside instead, packing the Great Hall, common rooms and Potions classroom into the shell. You get far more layers and far more play value, but the part count and the build time both roughly double.

What makes or breaks a castle blueprint is tower proportion. The main tower cannot be too chunky, the spires need to taper course by course, and the windows should be offset with darker parts so the walls read as something magical rather than a plain box. The castle blueprint I built used over a thousand slope pieces on the roofs alone, and the second half of the build is a genuine test of patience. I would not hand that to a first-time builder.

## Diagon Alley and Scene Blueprints: Atmosphere in Miniature

If the castle is the big engineering project, Diagon Alley is where the atmosphere lives. Ollivanders wand shop, Madam Malkin's robe shop and Gringotts wizarding bank each have a distinct facade and signboard colour scheme. Built as standalone shops or as one short stretch of street, they photograph beautifully together. Part counts here are moderate, usually a few hundred pieces for a single shop, which makes them ideal for short evening sessions.

These scene blueprints live or die on signage and detail parts. The oversized wand above the wand shop door and the arched Gringotts entrance carry all the recognition value, and they depend on printed elements and specialised parts. When you pick a blueprint, look closely at what the render uses for those details. If the special-part ratio is high, confirm whether a substitution exists before you commit.

## Accuracy and Details: Where the Magic Hides

After enough Harry Potter builds you start to see that accuracy comes down to three places.

The first is broomsticks. The Nimbus 2000 and the Firebolt work as small standalone MOCs in their own right: brown parts curved into the handle, a small tuft at the tail for the bristles, and the flight pose finally looks natural.

The second is small props such as wands and the Sorting Hat. They are only a handful of bricks each, but if the shape is right, the character is right.

The third is movement. Some blueprints build the Platform Nine and Three-Quarters pillar so the figure appears to pass through the wall. That kind of detail is what pushes a design above the rest.

One warning: this theme leans heavily on printed parts and stickers. House crests, banners and the Marauder's Map all need patterned elements, so check availability against the parts list before you order anything.

## Parts Sourcing and Difficulty: Which Blueprint Suits Whom

Here is the short version by skill level. Beginners should start with a small Diagon Alley shop or a broomstick: fewer parts, fast build, quick sense of achievement. Intermediate builders with some experience can take on a single castle section or a scene with a full interior. Veterans chasing a display centrepiece should go straight for a large Hogwarts castle and stock up on white and light grey basic bricks and slopes first.

Part commonality matters more than anything else when choosing a blueprint. Versions with a lot of special and printed elements should be checked for availability before you place the order.
