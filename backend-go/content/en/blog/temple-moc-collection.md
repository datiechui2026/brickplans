# Chinese Temple MOC Review: 4,200-Part Hall, 45 cm Wide

The short version first: the temple instructions on brickplan capture the mood of classical Chinese building rather than just its silhouette. The main hall (Daxiong Baodian) version comes in at roughly 4,200 parts, takes about 20 hours to build, and measures 45 cm across when finished. Both the upturned flying eaves and the stacked dougong bracket courses are actually built, not faked with a single molded slope. If Chinese architecture is your corner of the hobby, this belongs on your list.

## Appearance and Details

The model uses a hip-and-gable (xieshan) roof, the double-pitch form that real halls of high rank wore, and the small work is where it earns its keep. The ridge beasts along the roofline and the dougong brackets tucked under the eaves are assembled from small elements piece by piece — a dougong being the layered bracket set that steps outward to carry a deep overhang, and the reason these roofs can reach so far past their walls. Nothing here is approximated.

Openings are functional too: doors and windows swing or slide open, and the interior is furnished, with a Buddha figure and an offering table inside the hall. The palette is restrained — red walls, gold tile — which is why it reads as furniture rather than a toy when it's done. Put it in the living room and guests will cross the room to look at the roof.

## Structural Stability

The genuinely hard problem in any ancient-architecture MOC is the eave overhang, which has a habit of sagging under its own weight or shearing off when the model is lifted. This design addresses it in two places. Hidden support columns sit under the eaves, invisible from the front but carrying the load, and the flying eaves themselves ride on an internal skeleton of Technic parts. The result survives handling — you can move the finished hall without bracing the roof or worrying that it will let go halfway across the room.

The base also leaves channels pre-routed for light strip wiring. That matters more than it sounds: adding LEDs after the fact usually means dismantling a wall or drilling a baseplate, and here the path is already there waiting for a strip and a battery box.

## Building Experience

The 4,200 parts are split into booklets by zone — base first, then main body, then roof — which is the order that keeps a build this size from turning into a hunt for one dark red plate. The logic is clear enough that you can work a session at a time without losing your place.

The roof is the repetitive stretch, roughly 40% of total build time, because the same bracket and tile assemblies repeat around all four sides. Sorting parts by color before you open that booklet noticeably speeds things up. brickplan's instructions also include a teardown explanation of the dougong structure, so the roof section becomes a short lesson in how a real bracket set steps load outward and down into the columns — you finish knowing why the roof looks the way it does, not just how to stack it.

## Value for Money

Part out the list through brickplan and the bill lands around 700 yuan. Retail ancient-architecture sets of comparable size generally sell for more than 1,500 yuan, so you are saving close to half the cost, and you get the version you actually want instead of whatever configuration a manufacturer decided to release.

The smaller gate (shanmen) version is a far lower entry point at 1,500 parts and about 250 yuan. That is the sensible way in if you want to test whether this style of building suits you before committing the time and money to the full hall.
