# Barbershop MOC Guide: Rotating Pole to Retro Storefront

Short version first: a barbershop lives or dies on three things — the red, white and blue rotating pole outside the door, the chair and mirror that carry the interior, and the storefront window that gives the whole street scene its face.

A barbershop is one of the most instantly recognizable shop types you can put in a brick street layout. Stand a three-color rotating pole by the door and everyone knows what the business is. The appeal of a barbershop MOC is that it is small but precise: the footprint doesn't need to be big, but the pole, the barber chair, the mirror and the signboard all have to be there. This guide works from the outside in, so you can build a street module with real character.

## The rotating pole: the shop's signature piece

The pole is the single most identifiable element of a barbershop MOC. The classic method is to stack 1x1 cylinder bricks in alternating red, white and blue stripes, then cap the top with a dome. If you want it to actually turn, run an axle through the middle and connect it to a small crank at the base.

Keep the pole level with the signboard or slightly taller than it, and stand it on one side of the door on a black base — the retro feel shows up immediately. If you go the other route and build the stripes from sloped or curved pieces wound around a core, hold the diameter to 2 or 3 studs so the pole reads as a detail rather than a tower.

## The barber chair and mirror: the interior core

Interior detail is where a barbershop MOC earns its keep. Build the chair from a seat element plus a curved backrest, set a turntable plate into the base so it spins a full 360 degrees, and choose dark red or black for the upholstery. Mount the mirror on the wall with silver reflective tiles, then place a counter in front holding scissors and comb props — a transparent piece plus a thin silver bar reads as scissors at this scale. A ring of small light pieces around the wall simulates mirror lighting and makes the interior look professionally lit.

## Storefront and display window: the face of the street

The signboard sets the style for the whole shop. For a retro look, use dark green or dark blue as the base color with gold lettering; for a modern look, white with black text and a large glass window. Inside the window you can display a mannequin head — a ball piece with a smooth tile on the front — alongside barber tools, lit with warm light so passers-by look twice.

An arched door frame looks more elegant, and potted plants on both sides soften the entrance. If you are building a corner unit, add an advertising light box on the side wall: transparent pieces over a light panel make it glow and pull the shop forward in the street. Spell the shop name with gold printed tiles or letter tiles — low cost, high perceived quality.

## Color and part choices

The classic barbershop palette is red, white and blue, echoing the pole, plus black, white and gray for a modern feel; a retro build leans on dark green, dark brown and gold instead. Common parts you will need: cylinder bricks, turntable plates, silver reflective tiles, transparent glass pieces and curved slopes.

Before you start, count your stock of transparent and silver parts. Both get used up fast in a barbershop interior, and running out mid-build breaks your rhythm. Also leave standing room for a minifigure at the door — a barber holding scissors beside the pole instantly gives the whole street a story.

## Common pitfalls and next steps

The mistakes beginners make most often: a rotating pole scaled so large it overpowers the signboard, and an interior packed so full that minifigures can't get inside. Set your module size first (32x32 studs, for example), then divide the space between exterior detail and interior before you commit.

More advanced builders can add a motorized rotating pole, a removable roof, or link the barbershop to the shops next door to form one continuous street — which gives your barbershop MOC far more narrative.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I build the rotating pole?**

A: Arrange red, white and blue sloped or plate pieces in an alternating spiral around a cylinder, and add an axle inside to suggest rotation. Keep the diameter between 2 and 3 studs and stand it on one side of the door — that alone identifies the shop at a glance.

**Q: How should I build the barber chair and mirror?**

A: Use smooth tiles for the leather backrest and pair them with an adjustable base element. For the mirror, apply a large transparent piece to the wall and ring it with small light pieces as mirror lights; the interior immediately looks professional.

**Q: How do I choose the shop's colors?**

A: Traditional red, white and blue is the safest bet: off-white or light wood walls, red and blue accents on the signboard and pole, and a dark gray floor. Keep the palette to three colors or fewer — only a small, precise shop avoids looking cluttered.

**Q: Is this kind of shop suitable for beginners?**

A: Very much so. The footprint is small and the structure relies on basic bricks and plates. The pole and the display window are the two spots that need patience, which puts this squarely between entry level and intermediate street-scene building.
