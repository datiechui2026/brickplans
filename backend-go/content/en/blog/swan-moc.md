# Swan MOC Build Guide: Curved Neck, Feather Layers and Water Base

Bottom line first: a swan MOC succeeds or fails on three things — a separately built S-curve neck, shingled feather wings, and a transparent-blue water base. Keep the neck 8 to 12 studs tall for the best-looking proportions.

Swans are one of the toughest subjects in animal MOCs because they test your curve work. Bricks are born with right angles; a swan is nothing but curves — the long neck, the folded wings, the body floating on water. Every part of it fights the straight line. Get three things right — neck curvature, feather layering and the water base — and a brick swan MOC jumps from "blocky goose" to a desktop piece people stop to look at. The breakdown below follows build order and works for both white and black swans.

## 1. The Hard Part: Building the S-Curve Neck

The neck is the soul of the bird and the spot where a swan MOC most often goes wrong. Build the neck as a separate sub-assembly first, then attach it to the torso. Chain 2 to 3 sets of 1x4 hinge plates, offsetting each set by 15 to 30 degrees — the S-curve appears on its own, and because the hinges have friction, the angle stays where you set it instead of sagging.

For the outer surface, use 1x2 curved slopes that narrow layer by layer from the base toward the head: two studs wide at the base, one stud wide at the head, and the curve transitions naturally. Shape the head with a 1x1 brick plus a 1x1 round plate; mount an orange or red 1x1 slope brick sideways for the beak, and use 1x1 round tiles for the eyes.

Match neck length to body size. In general, 8 to 12 studs tall reads best — shorter looks clumsy, longer is fragile and prone to snapping.

## 2. Wings and Feather Layers: Offset Shingling Is the Core

How convincing the wings look comes down to whether the feathers have depth. Start with the wing bone: a 6x8 or 4x6 wedge plate as the base. Then shingle upward — each layer alternates 1x2 slope bricks with 1x1 tiles, and every new layer overlaps half of the one below it, staggered like roof tiles.

To make the feathers pop, switch the outer layers to 1x2 curved slopes and inset each row by one stud at both ends, which forms the folded wingtips. A wings-tucked pose only needs 2 to 3 layers; a spread-wing pose can go to 5 layers, but add a 1x4 plate at the wing root as reinforcement so it does not come apart in your hands. For a black swan, mix black with dark grey across the wings and leave a trace of white at the wingtips — the bird becomes instantly readable.

## 3. Water Surface and Base Scene: Making the Swan Float

Swans are water birds, and a single bird standing alone always feels incomplete. A water base is what turns the build into a finished swan MOC. Use a 16x16 or 24x24 baseplate and simulate the surface with transparent blue elements: lay 2x4 transparent blue plates as the bottom layer, then stack transparent blue 1x2 bricks and 1x1 transparent round plates at staggered heights above it for a glittering, rippled look.

Sink the lower part of the swan's body one or two studs into the "water", then scatter a few white slope bricks around it as foam, and the floating effect appears. For a more polished result, use SNOT (studs not on top) to wrap a ring of transparent blue plates around the sides of the baseplate, so the edges read as water rather than exposed baseplate.

## 4. Parts List and Colour Advice

Gather your parts before you start: white (or black) 2x4 and 2x6 basic bricks in quantity, 1x2 and 2x3 wedge bricks, 1x2 curved slopes, 1x4 hinge plates, ball joint pieces (to connect the wings to the torso), 1x1 round tiles (eyes), orange or red slope bricks (beak), transparent blue plates and transparent blue 1x2 bricks, and a 16x16 baseplate.

On colour: for a white swan, keep it mostly pure white with a small amount of light grey-blue on the underside as shadow transition — it has more volume than flat white. For a black swan, use pure black as the base, dark grey on the insides of the wings, and a red beak with red feet for strong contrast.

For a model around 20 cm long, expect roughly 500 to 900 pieces and a cost of about 100 to 200 yuan. Transparent elements and curved slopes are the expensive categories, so it pays to hunt down loose bricks on secondhand marketplaces in advance.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I build the S-curve neck?**
A: Build the neck as a separate sub-assembly and then attach it to the torso. Chain 2 to 3 sets of 1x4 hinge plates, offsetting each set by 15 to 30 degrees, and the S-curve appears. For the outer surface, use 1x2 curved slopes that narrow layer by layer from base to head.

**Q: How long should the neck be?**
A: Generally 8 to 12 studs tall looks best — too short reads clumsy, too long breaks easily. Keep it two studs wide at the base and one stud wide at the head, shape the head with a 1x1 brick plus a round plate, mount the beak with an orange or red 1x1 slope brick on its side, and use 1x1 round tiles for the eyes.

**Q: How do I get the shingled feather look on the wings?**
A: Build the wing bone first (wedge plate as the base), then shingle upward: each layer alternates 1x2 slope bricks with 1x1 tiles, and every layer overlaps half of the one below. A tucked pose needs 2 to 3 layers; a spread pose can go to 5, with a plate added at the wing root for reinforcement.

**Q: How do I build the water base?**
A: Use a 16x16 or 24x24 baseplate. Lay 2x4 transparent blue plates as the bottom layer, then stack transparent blue 1x2 bricks and 1x1 round plates at staggered heights for the ripples. Sink the lower body one or two studs into the water and add white slope bricks around it as foam.
