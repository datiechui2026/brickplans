# Brick Zebra MOC: Stripes, Stance and Structure

Short version first: a zebra MOC succeeds or fails on stripe direction and center of gravity. Offset your layers to get diagonal stripes, give the legs roughly half the model's height, and add weight in the belly to stop the forward lean.

Zebras are one of the most photogenic subjects in the savanna animal category: hard black-and-white contrast, long legs, a mane that seems to move. Set one on a desk and you have a piece of the grasslands. Then you start building and discover the hard part is exactly the feature that makes a zebra recognizable. Lay black bricks in straight lines and the stripes break up, drift out of alignment, and the finished animal looks like a zebra crossed with a cow. This guide covers the stripe method and the body structure, so the black-and-white art actually works.

## Stripe Method: Offset Beats Aligned

The stripes are the soul of a brick zebra MOC, and the technique decides the result. The most reliable approach is offset layering: alternate 1x2 black bricks and 1x2 white bricks along the body side, shifting each course one stud to the right. The stripes then flow diagonally on their own, which reads far more like a real zebra than perfectly vertical columns. Neck and belly stripes run in different directions — the neck stripes are nearly vertical, the belly stripes curve horizontally — and you bridge the change with 1x1 bricks. For finer work, place 1x1 tiles stud by stud. It is slow, but you can taper the stripe width for a hand-painted look, and this is where a builder's skill shows most clearly.

## Mane and Head: Making the Zebra Alive

The head is a compact structure. Narrow the muzzle with dark gray or black parts, but do not make the whole snout black — leave a lighter transition and it looks far more natural. Use a black 1x1 round tile for the eye, set slightly above the midline of the muzzle, and small slope bricks for the ears. The mane is the band connecting head to neck: run black 1x1 bricks with clips, or small tooth elements, in a single row along the neck ridge, from the top of the head back to the shoulder blades. Let the mane flare slightly outward rather than lying flat; the extra depth is worth it. Connect head and torso with 2 to 3 ball joint pieces so the zebra can lower its head to graze or lift it to look into the distance. The pose comes alive immediately.

## Legs and Stance: Elegance Is a Balance Problem

People read zebras as tall and elegant, so the legs should account for roughly half the height of the whole model. Stack the front legs straight with 1x2 bricks, then add a 1x2 hinge brick at the knee to create a slight bend. The hind legs should be a little longer than the front, with the hip line raised using slope bricks, or the stance will look wrong. Finish the hooves with small black parts, widened by one stud to resist tipping. For balance: the head and neck reach forward, the hips sit back, and the center of gravity lands between the front and hind legs — all four feet should sit at the corners of a rectangle. If an over-long neck pushes the model forward, put two weight bricks in the belly or raise the hind legs by one layer, and the problem disappears.

## Part Selection: Black, White and Curves

Black and white basic bricks are the bulk of the build. Stock up on 1x2 and 2x4 bricks in both colors before you start, working from the parts list. Curved and slope bricks are what give the body its roundness: you need a few 2x3 curved bricks over the hips and a few over the chest. Mane and hoof details are small black pieces, so extra 1x1 parts never hurt. Stay strict about the color scheme — pure black and white, with at most a touch of dark gray at the eyes and hooves. Add any other color and the elegance of the animal is gone.

## FAQ

**Q: How do I make the black and white stripes look natural?**
A: Use offset layering. Alternate 1x2 black and white bricks along the body side and shift each course one stud to the right; the stripes pick up a diagonal flow. Keep the neck stripes nearly vertical and the belly stripes curved horizontally, and bridge the change with 1x1 bricks.

**Q: How do I get the head and mane to show character?**
A: Narrow the muzzle with dark gray or black parts and leave a lighter transition at the snout. Use a black 1x1 round tile for the eye, set slightly above the muzzle midline. Run black bricks with clips along the neck ridge for the mane, flaring it slightly outward for depth.

**Q: My zebra will not stand and keeps tipping forward. What now?**
A: The legs need to be about half the model's height. Stack the front legs with 1x2 bricks and add a hinge brick at the knee for a slight bend; make the hind legs a little longer and raise the hip line with slopes. Set all four feet at the corners of a rectangle, and if it still leans, add weight bricks in the belly.

**Q: Which parts should I prepare, and how strict is the color scheme?**
A: Black and white basic bricks are used most, so get a batch each of 1x2 and 2x4 in both colors, plus 2x3 curved bricks for the hips and chest. Keep the palette pure black and white, with at most a little dark gray at the eyes and hooves — any other color kills the elegance.
