# Brick Church MOC Blueprints Reviewed: Spires, Rose Windows, Scale

A church is one of the most recognisable subjects in architectural MOC building, and also one of the easiest to get wrong. There is no shortage of blueprints out there; what is rare is a blueprint that actually builds into something that reads as a church. This review looks at common brick church MOC blueprints across five dimensions — style, parts, difficulty, instructions and build technique — so you can judge whether a blueprint is worth paying for.

## Start by sorting out the four church styles

**Gothic spired churches** are the most popular: tall twin towers, pointed-arch windows, a large rose window. Blueprints are plentiful but so are the traps — the moment tower proportions drift, the model turns into a box. **Village chapels** are single-tower, low and compact, usually off-white walls with a red roof, and they work well as a mid-block building in a street scene. **Orthodox onion domes** depend on hemisphere pieces plus a cross finial, so check that the blueprint's dome piece count is actually sufficient. **Modern churches** have clean lines and large glazed areas, yet they demand more skill with arch and pointed-arch construction than the traditional styles do. Always start with the cover render: settle the style first, then discuss everything else.

## Parts lists and fidelity

A good blueprint's parts list must specify **part number, colour and quantity**, and flag any **rare pieces**. Gothic churches lean on 1x1 corner slopes, curved plates, pointed-arch elements (such as 22388) and transparent window pieces, and these are the parts most likely to be missing from your stock. Fidelity is best judged on the **rose window**: high-end blueprints build the radial petals from wedge plates, while ordinary ones fudge it with stickers or printed parts, and the difference on the finished model is obvious. When you evaluate a blueprint, count the list once against your own inventory — do not shrug off a design that is "only a few transparent pieces short", because sourcing those later can cost more than the blueprint itself.

## Difficulty tiers and instructions

Difficulty generally splits into three levels. **Beginner** (500–1500 pieces, village chapel) suits a first architectural build. **Intermediate** (1500–3000 pieces, with a bell tower and spire) adds real complexity. **Advanced** (3000+ pieces, twin-tower cathedral) demands very tight **proportion control**. Judge the instructions on three points: whether steps are grouped sensibly (no more than 15 pieces per step), whether the spire's layer-by-layer taper is shown in staged diagrams, and whether the stained glass and arches get enlarged detail views. A manual that mixes plan views with axonometric views is far easier to build from than one made entirely of top-down shots.

## Build notes: towers, arches and interior

For spired towers, remember **narrow it layer by layer, stagger the corners**: use plates for a belt course at each level, stack the spire from slopes with staggered seams, and keep the tower body roughly 4 to 6 times as tall as it is wide. Build **arches** from curved plates turned upside down, with 1x1 bricks packed in at the springing points. Leave open windows in the **bell tower** and hang a small bell inside; frame the rose window with plates stacked into a surround. Do not skimp on the interior: **pews** are two rows of 1x2 plates topped with slopes, the **altar** is a single stepped platform with a cross element, and candles are transparent 1x1 pieces. Finish the inside and the whole church immediately comes alive.

## How it compares with an official set

An official set builds straight out of the box with reliable part quality, but it gives you no freedom. A blueprint MOC gives you control over scale, room to modify, the option to mix in compatible third-party bricks, and a free choice of colour scheme. On price, blueprints typically run CNY 10–60, far less than official sets that cost several hundred or more; the trade-off is that you supply the parts yourself. **Who it suits**: builders who love street-scene architecture, who want to practise pointed arches and tower technique, or who want to add a landmark to a city layout. Beginners should start with a village chapel blueprint.
