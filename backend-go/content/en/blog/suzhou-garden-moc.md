# LEGO Suzhou Garden MOC: Layout, Lattice Windows and Rockery

The short version first: a Suzhou garden MOC is hard because of placement, not because of any single model. Put the pavilion near the golden-section point, give the water roughly a third of the base, keep the rockery porous and slender, and two or three lattice windows are plenty.

Suzhou gardens are built on the idea that a garden should be made by human hands yet look as if it grew there on its own. In bricks, the difficult part is composition: in a small footprint you need both density and breathing room, layering, and a guided line of sight. This guide translates four classic garden devices — borrowed scenery, lattice windows, rockery, and pavilions with waterside halls — into brick terms.

**Layout and borrowed scenery.** The biggest mistake in a small garden MOC is spreading your effort evenly. Pick one main viewing angle. Set the pavilion near the golden-section point, let the rockery hold one corner, give the water about a third of the area, and leave an open patch in the middle — in garden terms that is the courtyard, and it is where the sense of breathing room comes from. Borrowed scenery can be built as a framed view: open a doorway or a lattice window in the garden wall and let the sightline pass through it to a pavilion or rockery further back. The photographic composition appears immediately, and it is the most photogenic trick in the whole build.

**Lattice windows.** They are the eyes of a Suzhou garden, and everything depends on openwork. In bricks you can build that with 1x1 bricks with a hole, grille tiles, or transparent plates set inside a window frame, forming ice-crack or begonia patterns. The easiest shape with the biggest payoff is the moon gate: build a half circle from 1x1 curved plates and join two halves into a round opening, and you instantly get the garden-within-a-garden feeling. Don't overdo it — two or three across the whole garden is enough; any more and the wall starts to feel blocked up.

**Rockery is the technical difficulty.** Neat, regular bricks won't work here. Aim for controlled chaos: mix wedge bricks, slope bricks, 1x1 round plates and irregular pieces, use larger parts at the base and switch to smaller ones as you rise, then hang a few plants on the surface with SNOT (1x1 plant pieces). The rockery should be porous, open and slender, not a solid mound of earth. Holes through the middle are what give it life.

**Pavilions and waterside halls provide the focal point.** Build a small pavilion from four brown 1x1 round bricks as columns, with four slope bricks meeting at the top to form a pyramidal roof. A waterside hall sits half over the water: cantilever plates outward and show the depth of the water below with transparent blue pieces. Lay the water surface with a mix of light blue and transparent plates, edge it with white plates as the bank, and float one or two lotus leaves (green round dish pieces) on top — the scene comes alive.

**Paving and planting set the base tone.** Alternate light gray and off-white plates on the ground to imitate pebble mosaic. Put a banana plant in the corner (large green leaf pieces on a brown stem) and clumps of bamboo along the corridor (stacked 1x1 green round plates), keeping the heights uneven for a wilder feel. Add a zigzag bridge over the water — three plate sections joined with hinges into a Z shape — and the small bridge over flowing water idea is complete. Gardens are about changing scenery with every step: from each new angle there should be a new main subject.

**Colour and pitfalls.** Keep the palette restrained: white walls, gray roof tiles, brown timber, with a touch of green and red (pavilion columns, lanterns). The pitfalls to watch: first, rockery and pavilion out of proportion — the pavilion should stand slightly taller than the rockery; second, water that reads as flat — use several shades of blue plate to suggest ripples; third, losing the whole in the details — step back and check the overall view after finishing each element. For structured study, search "Suzhou garden" MOC instructions on brickplan and look at the plan layout and lattice window treatment of finished builds; it is far faster than working it out alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the hardest part of a Suzhou garden MOC?**
A: Composition. In a small footprint you need density, layering and a guided line of sight. Pick one main viewing angle, put the pavilion near the golden-section point, give one corner to the rockery and about a third of the base to water, and leave an open courtyard in the middle so the scene can breathe.

**Q: How do I build lattice windows and a moon gate?**
A: Use 1x1 bricks with a hole, grille tiles or transparent plates inside a window frame to create ice-crack and begonia patterns. The moon gate gives the best result — two half circles of 1x1 curved plates joined into a round opening. Don't overdo it; two or three is enough.

**Q: How do I make a brick rockery look alive?**
A: Controlled chaos. Mix wedge bricks, slope bricks, 1x1 round plates and irregular pieces, keep larger parts at the base and smaller pieces higher up, then attach a few plants with SNOT. The rockery should be porous, open and slender rather than a solid mound.

**Q: How should paving and planting be handled?**
A: Alternate light gray and off-white plates to imitate pebble mosaic. Put a banana plant in the corner (large green leaf pieces on a brown stem) and bamboo along the corridor (stacked 1x1 green round plates) at uneven heights for a wilder look. Add a three-section, Z-shaped zigzag bridge over the water.
