# Brick Airplane MOC Plans: Fighters, Airliners, Helicopters

Among all MOC subjects, aircraft remain one of the most popular directions. The reason is simple: planes come with a naturally streamlined silhouette and dense mechanical detail, and they offer both a static display shape and working features such as retractable landing gear and opening canopies. Put the finished model on a desk and it is instantly a centerpiece. A good brick airplane MOC plan often decides whether your weekend is a pleasure or a grind. This review sets classic fighter, airliner and helicopter plans side by side and walks through what makes each one tick.

## Fighter plans: the tug-of-war between part count and accuracy

Fighter MOC plans are the largest and most competitive category right now. Mainstream builds fall into two tiers: an entry level of 1,000 to 2,000 pieces, and a display-centerpiece tier of 3,000 pieces and up. The first mostly stacks slopes to shape the fuselage outline; the advantage is a fast start and a finished model in half a day. The second relies on a layered design that separates internal frame from outer skin, and tries to match real-aircraft proportions for intakes, canards and the canopy.

To judge whether a plan is any good, look at three things. First, whether the parts list is clearly labeled with official color IDs. Second, whether the building steps follow a disassembly logic where each step only changes a local area. Third, whether the end of the instructions includes notes on the working features. A military MOC plan that clears all three is generally a version you can safely keep in your library.

## Airliner plans: trading structure against finished size

Airliners are the opposite of fighters. What matters is not detail density but structural rigidity at large scale. These plans regularly run 5,000 to 8,000 pieces, and the finished wingspan often exceeds 60 cm. Without a continuous beam-and-column skeleton running through the fuselage, the model will sag and deform after standing for a while. The builds worth recommending in this review usually make the cabin interior into detachable segmented modules and pair them with reinforced wing-root connectors, so the model is both easier to move and tougher to survive handling.

For newcomers, airliner plans are not easy. A sensible route is to start with a small twin-turboprop build for practice, then move up to a wide-body jet.

## Helicopter plans: working mechanisms at the best value

Helicopter MOCs are the lowest-barrier and best-value option of the three. Classic builds mostly sit between 600 and 1,200 pieces, and mechanisms such as linked main rotors, tail rotor drive and opening cabin doors can be done with just a few driveshafts. The sense of achievement arrives remarkably fast.

When picking a plan, check the parts list for the rotor section carefully. Older plans often lack blades with special angles, and you only discover at the end that substitutions are needed, which is a hassle. As a first airplane brick plan for a beginner, a helicopter is almost impossible to get wrong.

## How to judge plan quality and how beginners should choose

Judging whether a brick airplane MOC plan is reliable takes three simple steps: check whether there are clear renders and screenshots of the parts list; check whether anyone in the comments has actually finished the build and posted photos; check whether the author keeps updating and correcting the file. For beginners, remember one principle: pick from the group with a small part count, simple structure and few working mechanisms, and put the quality of your first building experience first.

The appeal of aircraft MOCs is visible progress. From a first small helicopter to a heavy fighter that can actually sit on its landing gear, every brick airplane MOC plan is a mark of how far you have come.
