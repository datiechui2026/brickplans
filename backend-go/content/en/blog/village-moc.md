# Brick Village MOC Plans: Cottages and Four-Season Scenes

Verdict first: start your brick village with a cottage. Beginners should build a single country cottage of roughly 500-1500 pieces to practice wall panels and roof structure, then move up to a complete village of 3000+ pieces with a barn and a windmill.

Villages are one of the few MOC subjects that look good however you build them. A row of slightly crooked fences, a few leafy trees and smoke drifting from a chimney are enough to summon up country life. There are a lot of village plans out there, so this guide covers how to choose one, which parts to stock and how to build all four seasons.

## 1. How to Choose a Brick Village MOC Plan

Scale comes first. Beginners should start with a single cottage, about 500-1500 pieces, which is enough to get comfortable with wall panels and roof structure. Advanced builders can then take on a full village with a barn, windmill and livestock shed, often 3000 pieces and up.

Style comes second. European half-timbered houses use bright color blocking, Chinese farm buildings rely on dark roof tiles against white walls, and Japanese farmhouses lean on timbered eaves. Pick whichever one you fall for at first glance.

Plan completeness comes last, and it matters: look for step-by-step renders and a parts list (BOM) so you don't run out of pieces halfway through the build. BrickPlan carries several village-themed plans, and you can filter them by piece count and style.

## 2. Parts List for a Country Cottage

The basics you must have on hand: cream and sand-colored plates for the walls, dark brown bar pieces for beams and window frames, red and dark grey slopes for the pitched roof, and plenty of green and brown parts for trees and grass.

Detail parts are the soul of the build: fence pieces, flower pieces, a round barrel or water butt, small farm tools, plus one warm yellow light element to glow inside the window. If you plan to build all four seasons, add light grey frost-and-snow plates and leaf pieces in red, orange and yellow so you can switch the theme whenever you like.

## 3. Build Order and Structural Techniques

The recommended order is: foundation and roads, then main walls, then roof, then courtyard dressing. Lay the foundation on baseplates and leave the line of the road open first. Build walls with interlocking construction, offsetting the seams between courses so the walls stay rigid. For the roof, build the sloped skeleton first, then tile from the eaves up to the ridge, layer by layer, and finish the edges with slope pieces.

Here is the key trick: make the cottage a module you can lift off in one piece. Seal the bottom flat with plates and clip it onto the foundation with studs, so you can remodel or add lighting later. Build the courtyard separately from the main building and join the two at the end, which makes moving and photographing much easier.

## 4. Four-Season Scenes: Spring, High Summer and Harvest

Seasons are where village MOCs photograph best. Spring: a cream cottage with fresh green canopies and drifts of flower pieces, plus a small handcart beside the fence. High summer: denser dark green foliage, a well and a vegetable patch for that baking-hot feel. Harvest: swap the leaves for orange and yellow pieces, pile pumpkins and hay bales by the door, and hang corn to dry under the eaves. Winter: white plates for snow on the roofs.

For lighting, switch on warm yellow window lights at dusk against a blue-violet twilight backdrop and the whole village comes alive.

## 5. Layout Advice and Mistakes to Avoid

Aim for staggered, not lined up: vary the height of the houses, offset trees front and back, and curve the road slightly instead of running it in a straight line. Pave roads with light grey plates in an offset pattern and edge them with dark pieces for soil.

Four things to avoid: press every roof course down firmly or it will work loose; test-fit doors and windows before fixing them so you don't have to redo the alignment; use small ball joint pieces for tree foliage so you can recolor it each season; and leave cable channels for the lighting in advance instead of cramming wires in at the end.

Building a small village is really a way of laying out your idea of the good life, piece by piece. Start with a solid village MOC plan, pair it with the right parts and some patience, and you can have your own miniature countryside with four distinct seasons. ## FAQ

**Q: How do I choose a brick village MOC plan?**
A: Start with scale: beginners should begin with a single cottage of about 500-1500 pieces; advanced builders can then take on a village with a barn, windmill and livestock shed, usually over 3000 pieces. Then pick a style, whether European, Chinese or Japanese.

**Q: What basic parts does a village MOC need?**
A: Cream and sand plates for walls, dark brown bar pieces for beams, frames and doors, a small number of slope bricks for the roof and green parts for trees. Check that the plan includes step renders and a parts list so you don't run short mid-build.

**Q: How long does a full village take to build?**
A: A single cottage takes 3-4 hours. A complete village with a barn and windmill usually takes 10-20 hours, so build it as several separate building modules in batches, then lay out the ground and roads at the end.

**Q: How do I lay out a village so it feels rural?**
A: Break up the regularity with rooflines at different heights and fences that lean slightly, then add trees, a well and street lamps. Use sand and green plates for field ridges and paths on the ground, and change the colors with the seasons: spring green, autumn yellow.
