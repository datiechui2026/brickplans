# MOC Design Software Compared: Studio, LDD and Mecabricks

Plenty of builders who have just fallen down the brick rabbit hole ask the same question: what software do you actually use to design a MOC? I have been building for years, and I have installed every mainstream design tool on the market and run a full side-by-side comparison. What follows is a first-person account of how each one really feels in daily use, so you can skip part of the trial and error when choosing your own tool.

## The main design tools on the market

The most popular option in the community right now is **Stud.io**, now officially named **BrickLink Studio**. It sits on top of BrickLink's enormous marketplace data, its part library is comprehensive and updated quickly, and it has become the default choice for new builders almost by acclamation. **LDD (LEGO Digital Designer)** is the software LEGO itself released years ago. The interface is plain, and the part library stopped around 2016 — it is no longer updated, but plenty of people still use it as an entry-level exercise. **Mecabricks** is a purely browser-based modelling tool: no installation, cross-platform, ideal when inspiration strikes on a machine that is not your own or when your computer is not especially powerful. Beyond those, there are older open-source tools such as LeoCAD and BrickSmith, which suit advanced builders who are comfortable driving everything from keyboard shortcuts.

These tools are not really competing on the same axis. Studio is a modelling environment tied to a marketplace, LDD is a discontinued official product kept alive by habit and nostalgia, and Mecabricks is a web app that trades local horsepower for convenience. Keeping that in mind makes the rest of the comparison easier to read.

## Learning curve and interface

If the question is which MOC software is easiest to pick up, my answer is Stud.io. Its drag-and-snap placement and automatic alignment are extremely smooth — a beginner can put together a first wall in about fifteen minutes. LDD's interface looks dated, but the operating logic is straightforward, so it also works as a starting point for newcomers; the incomplete part library only becomes a real problem once you push into more advanced builds. Mecabricks has a clean browser interface and its mouse-driven workflow is quick to learn, though positioning complex parts can occasionally be irritating. Overall, the friendliness ranking is roughly Stud.io > Mecabricks > LDD.

The difference shows up most clearly in the first hour. With Stud.io you spend that hour building; with LDD you spend it learning a smaller vocabulary of parts; with Mecabricks you spend a little of it fighting the camera. None of those are dealbreakers, but if you are brand new, the tool that keeps your hands moving is the one that keeps you interested.

## Part libraries and export options

The part library determines how faithfully you can reproduce a design. Stud.io's library covers almost every part on BrickLink and plugs directly into marketplace data, so a design you finish can move straight toward sourcing the pieces. LDD's library is frozen around 2016, which means it cannot handle the connection points of many newer parts or the sideways building techniques that depend on them. Mecabricks updates its library diligently, but a few obscure parts are still missing.

On export, all three can produce PDF instructions and LDraw files. Stud.io's instruction generation is the most complete of the three: it splits steps automatically and labels the parts list, which makes a model easy to share with other builders — for example, publishing to our BrickPlan community (https://brickplan.cn) so fellow fans can rebuild it together. That step-splitting saves a lot of manual work; anyone who has hand-ordered instructions for a 500-piece model knows how much time it eats.

## Rendering and instruction generation

Whether the images look good directly decides how a finished model comes across. Stud.io has a solid built-in renderer with one-click adjustment of lighting, background and depth of field; the straight output is good enough to post on social media without extra work. Mecabricks also offers cloud rendering, which removes the need for a locally installed high-end graphics card — the downside is waiting in a queue, which is noticeable when you are iterating on a scene. LDD's rendering is plain, and in practice you export the file and hand it to third-party software.

On instruction generation, both Stud.io and Mecabricks output clear step-by-step build images; LDD trails a little behind. For anyone who plans to share designs rather than just keep them on a hard drive, that gap matters more than rendering quality, because bad instructions are what stop other people from actually building your model.

## Which software should you pick?

Ultimately there is no single right answer to which software to use for a MOC — it depends on your stage and your needs. A complete beginner should go straight to Stud.io, since its ecosystem is the most complete. If you want to test ideas fast and would rather not install anything, choose Mecabricks. Veterans who only build retro themes will find LDD sufficient.

My own routine is to use Stud.io as the workhorse for renders and instructions, then publish the finished model to https://brickplan.cn as a record. Tools are only a means; the fastest progress comes from building often and taking apart good work.
