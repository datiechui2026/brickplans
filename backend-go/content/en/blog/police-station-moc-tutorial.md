# Police Station MOC Tutorial: From Blueprint to Finished Model

Most street-scene builders start with houses and shops, then find themselves drawn to civic buildings — and the police station is the most recognizable building on any block. This tutorial lays out a complete path from blueprint to finished model for newcomers. The build breaks down into four stages: sourcing a blueprint, planning the structure, zoning the interior, and designing the color scheme. Take them one at a time and you will find it is far less intimidating than it looks.

## Step 1: Get a Reliable Blueprint First

Even the best idea needs a dependable blueprint to land properly. A proper blueprint is more than an exploded view and step-by-step instructions: it also gives you a parts list and brick count for each floor, so you know the scale of the project before you open a single bag.

### Why the Blueprint Matters So Much

For a beginner, a police station tutorial without a blueprint quickly turns into an endless cycle of building, undoing and rebuilding. A well-made blueprint plans the load-bearing structure, the door and window positions and the wall thickness for you in advance. Street-scene blueprint resources are already fairly plentiful — on brickplan.cn (https://brickplan.cn), for example, you can find police station and other street-scene building blueprints that cover the full sequence from foundation to roof, which makes them a good reference to learn from.

## Step 2: Planning the Structure

Once you have the blueprint, resist the urge to start snapping bricks. Read the whole thing through first, then split the building into four modules — base, main body, entrance, and badge details — and work up floor by floor.

### The Base

The base determines how stable the whole building is. Start with a large baseplate, then lay two or three courses of reinforcing wall around the perimeter, leaving openings for the doorway and for the load-bearing sections of wall. The base does not need to be fancy: flat, square and structurally sound come first.

### The Main Body

Build the main body one floor at a time. Put the public-facing functions on the ground floor and the office space above. On each level, stand up the four corner pillars first, then fill in horizontally with wall bricks, leaving openings for windows and doors. You can add a few sideways-mounted bricks to the walls to give the façade more depth.

### The Entrance

The police station entrance needs to be unmistakable. A double glass door with a dark frame works well, with transparent pieces left in the doors so the interior lighting shows through — the street scene improves immediately once it is lit up at night.

### The Badge Detail

The badge is the focal point. Use special pieces in gold or silver to build a shield-shaped emblem above the door lintel, then flank it with two small wall lamps. You do not need many details; one or two are enough to hold up to repeat viewing.

## Step 3: Zoning the Interior

Street-scene buildings are judged on the façade from outside and on the functionality from inside, and a police station needs at least three zones.

### Reception Area

Reserve the spot directly facing the entrance for reception: a counter, a standing desk lamp and a small sign reading "Reception" create the impression that someone is on duty.

### Office

Set up the office along the window side with a desk, chairs and a filing cabinet. A small computer or a stack of file bricks on the desk makes it immediately clear that this is where the day-to-day work happens.

### Holding Cell

The cell is the part of the police station with the most story potential. Use bar bricks to enclose a compartment, fit it with a small door that has a lock, and add a simple bed. You can even place a "mystery figure" minifigure against the wall to leave a little narrative room in the scene.

## Step 4: Color Design

Color is what finally decides whether a street-scene police station looks right. Use blue as the dominant tone: light and dark blue bricks over large areas of wall, with white accents on the roof and window sills for a crisp blue-and-white contrast. Dark grey and dark blue work for the pillars and door frames, anchoring the overall palette; small areas such as the badge get gold for brightness, so they stand out without looking cluttered. Keep the hierarchy of primary and secondary colors clear and the scheme will not go wrong.

## Wrapping Up: Let the Blueprint Carry You Through Your First Police Station

This tutorial has walked from sourcing a blueprint through interior zoning and color design, and the core idea stays the same throughout: plan first, build second. If you would rather start building from a ready-made blueprint, have a look around brickplan.cn (https://brickplan.cn), where police stations and other street-scene brick buildings are collected. Once your first police station is standing firmly on the corner, you will realize the fun of building with bricks has only just begun.
