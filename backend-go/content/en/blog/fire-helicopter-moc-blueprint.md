# Fire Helicopter MOC Blueprint: Rotor, Skid and Winch Guide

Short version first: pick the scale before you pick up a brick — 6-wide for parts compatibility, 8-wide for detail — then get the rotors, the skids and the winch right, and the rest of the build falls into place.

Fire and rescue is one of the most popular themes in brick MOC building, but most builders stop at fire trucks and fire boats. A brick-built fire helicopter is where the theme really earns its keep: a red airframe in a display case is instantly readable, and the mechanical parts that only a helicopter has — rotor head, hoist, skids — each give you a real problem to solve.

## Decide scale and colour scheme first

Before you open Studio or dig through the bins, choose a scale. The usual approach follows the 6-wide standard of city sets: parts are common, and the model sits comfortably next to an existing town layout. If you want more detail, go to 8-wide or even 10-wide — the fuselage gets fuller, rounder lines, but your part count roughly doubles, and so does the cost of gathering it.

For colour, fire helicopters are mostly red with white or yellow as the secondary. Don't forget a generous stock of transparent parts for the cockpit windscreen and the warning lights, since those are the pieces that sell the aircraft at a glance. Red plates and panels, plus bricks that let you build red-and-white striping, are the parts that turn up most often in any fire helicopter blueprint — buy them in quantity rather than in ones and twos.

## Fuselage and rotors: the soul of the aircraft

For the fuselage, SNOT (Studs Not On Top) is the technique to reach for. Building the sides sideways lets you create smooth, curved surfaces that read as a streamlined airframe instead of a stack of stepped bricks. The cockpit can be a single-piece windscreen or a run of sloped glass; taper the tail boom slightly toward the end and the silhouette starts to look like the real machine rather than a box with a propeller.

The rotor is the part that matters most. Fix long wing plates to a cross axle, add a turntable in the middle, and you get full 360-degree rotation — play value jumps immediately. Don't forget the tail rotor: a small propeller mounted at a reversed angle sells the pose at a glance and costs almost nothing in parts. If you want everything to move together, a gear train can link the main and tail rotors, but beginners should build static first and spend their energy on the exterior, where the eye actually lands.

## Skid landing gear and the winch

Most fire helicopters use skid landing gear. The structure is simple, which is exactly why it is easy to build crooked. My rule is that the two skid tubes must be strictly symmetrical, joined by technic beam cross-pieces — strong, and it keeps them parallel. Check the alignment against a straight edge on your desk before you commit the assembly to the fuselage; fixing a twisted skid afterwards means tearing half the underside apart.

The winch is the most recognisable functional part of a fire helicopter: a spool or a small winch drum as the winding mechanism, a hook and a firefighter minifigure on the end, plus a length of chain or flexible hose as the cable, and you can stage a water-bucket rescue scene. Add a hatch that opens and the story writes itself — the model stops being a shape and starts being a scene.

## Pitfalls: where these builds go wrong

First, centre of gravity. A helicopter sits high, and the rotor and tail boom stick out a long way, so the model will tip forward if it stands for long. Add ballast in the nose or rest it on a display stand.

Second, don't make the rotor too long. It throws off the proportion against the tail boom, and the blades will catch nearby parts — or the neighbour's model on the shelf — as they turn.

Third, transparent parts scratch easily. Use tweezers rather than a fingernail when pressing a windscreen into place; a scratched canopy cannot be polished back.

Fourth, red bricks vary noticeably between production batches. Buy them all in one go if you can, or your fuselage ends up with a patchy, piebald finish that no amount of detailing will hide.

## Where to find blueprints and how to choose one

There are a few places to look for a fire helicopter MOC blueprint. The brickplan site has a good number of fire-themed designs, and you can order the parts straight from the parts list. Overseas, Rebrickable and Bricklink both turn up plenty of strong builds if you search "fire helicopter MOC".

Three things to check before you commit to a design: whether the parts are rare or long out of production, whether the instructions include proper step-by-step diagrams, and whether other builders have posted photos of the finished model. Photos in the comments are the most honest reference you will get, and they save a lot of grief — especially on a model where the skids and the winch are the parts most likely to be simplified in the renders.

A fire helicopter MOC isn't hard, exactly, and it isn't simple either. But if you get the three core structures right — rotor, skids, winch — the finished model will not disappoint. If you have a better structural idea for any of them, share it with the brickplan community.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: What scale should a beginner use for a fire helicopter MOC?**
A: Start with the 6-wide standard from city sets — parts are easy to source and the model fits alongside existing scenery. Going to 8-wide or even 10-wide gives a fuller fuselage, but roughly doubles the part count.

**Q: How do I make the main rotor actually rotate?**
A: Fix long wing plates to a cross axle and put a turntable in the centre for 360-degree rotation. A gear train can link the tail rotor to it, but beginners should build a static exterior first.

**Q: What are the most common mistakes on a fire helicopter MOC?**
A: Four of them: a high centre of gravity that tips the model forward over time (add nose ballast), an over-long rotor that hits nearby parts as it turns, scratched transparent pieces (use tweezers), and colour mismatch across red brick batches (buy in one order).

**Q: Where do I find fire helicopter MOC blueprints, and is it worth the challenge?**
A: Look for fire-themed designs on brickplan, where you can also order from the parts list, or try Rebrickable and Bricklink. Get the rotor, skids and winch right and the result will hold up.
