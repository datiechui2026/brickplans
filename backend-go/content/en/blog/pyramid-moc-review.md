# Pyramid MOC Review: Sand Bricks and a 32x32 Base

Verdict first: this pyramid MOC follows the proportions of the Great Pyramid of Khufu, sits on a 32x32 stud footprint and stands roughly 26 cm tall, faking a smooth triangular slope with a stepped, layer-by-layer inset. It is a mid-size display piece rather than a tabletop trinket.

When people hear "pyramid," most picture the Great Pyramid on the Giza plateau. Recreating that millennia-old structure in brick is a long-standing ambition for many MOC builders. The model reviewed here is a mid-size take on those proportions, and at 32x32 studs across with a 26 cm height, it has real presence on a desk.

## Shape Accuracy: Geometry Done Precisely

The hardest part of any pyramid MOC is making square bricks read as a smooth slope, and that is where the designer's skill shows. This build uses the classic stepped approach: every layer is inset by half a stud, so from a distance the four faces look almost like flat triangles. All four sides are strictly symmetrical, and the apex is capped with a single gold printed brick, echoing the limestone casing and gilded peak of the original. A narrow entrance passage is left open on one side, with a hidden chamber tucked inside, which is a nice bit of attention to detail.

## Color Scheme: Sand Tones and Accents

The main body relies on Sand and Beige bricks, with a scattering of dark brown pieces standing in for weathering. The palette stays tightly unified and reads convincingly as desert stone. Around the entrance, black and dark grey bricks create shadow and add depth. The standout touch is a ring of transparent light blue bricks laid around the base to suggest the Nile's water. That detail keeps the model from feeling monotonous, and it has become a signature move in many pyramid MOCs.

## Structure and Play Features

A pyramid is a static building, so movement is naturally limited, but the designer worked in two clever touches. First, the entire top section lifts off to reveal the inner chamber and passage structure. Second, the base is built as a rotating display stand, so all four faces can be viewed in detail through 360 degrees. For a static architecture MOC, that is a solid amount of interactivity.

## Building Experience: Repetitive but Not Tedious

The most time-consuming stretch is the middle section, roughly twenty identical stepped layers. The instructions split every five layers into a separate step pack, and the parts themselves are bagged by layer, so the repetition settles into a relaxing rhythm instead of a grind. Total build time runs about 6 to 8 hours, putting this in the medium-sized category. No unusual connectors are used anywhere: everything relies on stacked, interlocking bricks, and the result is stable enough to move without falling apart.

## Parts List

Around 2,800 pieces in total, dominated by basic 1x1, 1x2 and 2x2 bricks, which make up more than seventy percent of the count. Next come slope bricks in a range of angles, used to shape the stepped faces. Specialized parts are limited to a few printed bricks and transparent pieces. That means if your basic brick stock is deep enough, you could plausibly build your own version of this pyramid MOC from what you already own.

## Who It Suits

This pyramid MOC fits builders who like architecture subjects and want a display piece, and it also works well as an entry-level mid-size project for parents and kids building together. Anyone chasing complex mechanisms may find it a little simple, but as desk decor its level of finish is high. A: Use the stepped inset method: pull each layer in by half a stud and keep all four faces strictly symmetrical, and from a distance it reads as a smooth triangular face. Cap the apex with a gold printed brick to echo the gilded peak of the original.

**Q: How many parts and how long does a pyramid build take?**
